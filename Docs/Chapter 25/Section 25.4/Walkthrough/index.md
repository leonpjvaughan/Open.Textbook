# 25.4 Scatter graphs and correlation — Learn

**Prerequisites:** plot coordinate pairs and describe a trend using everyday language.

**Objectives:** plot paired data, describe correlation, and use a line of best fit for estimates. This section covers S6.

A **scatter graph** plots pairs of measurements as points. **Correlation** describes how two variables change together. Positive correlation means both tend to increase; negative correlation means one tends to decrease as the other increases. No correlation means there is no clear pattern.

**Example 1.** A class records hours of revision and test scores. Plot each pupil as $(\text{hours},\text{score})$. The points rise from left to right, so the data show positive correlation.

Correlation may be strong when points lie close to a line, or weak when they lie more widely around it. A **line of best fit** follows the overall pattern, with points balanced around it.

**Example 2.** A line of best fit passes through $(0,30)$ and $(10,70)$. Estimate the score for 6 hours.

The line rises $70-30=40$ points over 10 hours.

The estimated rise per hour is $40/10=4$ points.

At 6 hours, the estimate is $30+6\times4=54$ points.

This is interpolation because 6 hours lies within the plotted range. An estimate outside the data range is extrapolation and may be less reliable.

> **Watch out.** Correlation does not prove that one variable causes the other. Another factor may affect both; check whether the graph shows association only.

## Progress check

More hours of travel might be associated with later arrival times. Name a possible third factor that could influence both measurements.

## You should now be able to

- plot paired data and describe positive, negative or no correlation;
- describe the strength of a relationship from its pattern;
- draw a line of best fit and use it for interpolation;
- explain why correlation does not prove causation.
