# 7.4 Upper and lower bounds — Learn

## Before you begin

You should be able to write error intervals and substitute values into a formula.

## Objectives

- Identify the lower and upper bounds of a rounded measurement.
- Use bounds to find possible limits for a calculated quantity.
- State whether an endpoint can be reached.

## Bounds of one measurement

The lower bound is the smallest possible value consistent with the rounding. The upper bound is the greatest boundary; the measurement is less than it.

**Example 1.** A length is $6.4$ cm to the nearest $0.1$ cm.

$$
6.35\leq L<6.45
$$

So the lower bound is $6.35$ cm and the upper bound is $6.45$ cm.

## Bounds in calculations

For positive values, a maximum quotient uses the largest numerator and smallest denominator. A minimum quotient uses the smallest numerator and largest denominator.

**Example 2.** A journey is $120$ km to the nearest kilometre and takes $2.0$ hours to the nearest tenth. Find the greatest possible speed.

$$
119.5\leq d<120.5,\qquad1.95\leq t<2.05
$$

$$
\text{speed}<120.5\div1.95\approx61.8\text{ km/h}
$$

Thus $61.8$ km/h is the upper bound (to 3 significant figures); the speed is below the exact quotient.

> **Watch out.** For a maximum quotient, do not pair the two upper bounds. A smaller positive denominator makes the quotient larger.

> **Progress check.** A rectangle has length $5.2$ m and width $3.1$ m, each rounded to the nearest $0.1$ m. Which bounds give its maximum area?

## You should now be able to

- find lower and upper bounds from a rounded measurement;
- select bounds to maximise or minimise a positive calculation;
- explain why an endpoint is included or excluded.
