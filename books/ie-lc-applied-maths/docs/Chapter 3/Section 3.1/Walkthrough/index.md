# 3.1 Displacement, velocity and acceleration — Learn

**Prerequisites:** measuring a length and a time, and dividing one number by
another.

**Objectives:** tell distance from displacement, and speed from velocity; work out
an average speed, an average velocity and an acceleration; use a sign to show a
direction.

Motion is easier to describe once you separate two ideas that everyday language
runs together: how far something went, and where it ended up. This section sets up
the quantities that every later section uses.

## Distance and displacement

**Distance** is how far you travel along the path. It has a size and nothing else,
so it is a **scalar**. Distance is never negative.

**Displacement** is the change in position from start to finish. It has a size and
a direction, so it is a **vector**. In one dimension we show the direction with a
sign: one way along the line is positive, and the other way is negative.

$$\text{displacement} = \text{final position} - \text{initial position}$$

If you walk 5 m east and then 5 m west, you have walked a distance of 10 m. Your
displacement is 0 m, because you are back where you started.

**Example 1.** A cyclist rides 120 m east, then turns and rides 80 m west. Find
the distance travelled and the final displacement. Take east as positive.

Distance adds the two legs, because both are part of the path:

$$d = 120 + 80$$

$$d = 200\,\text{m}$$

Here $d$ is the distance in metres and $s$ is the displacement in metres.

For displacement, the second leg points the other way, so it counts as negative:

$$s = 120 - 80$$

$$s = 40\,\text{m}$$

The displacement is 40 m east, because the answer came out positive.

## Speed and velocity

**Speed** is how fast you travel along the path. It is a scalar.

**Velocity** is how fast your position changes, in a stated direction. It is a
vector.

$$\text{average speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}$$

$$\text{average velocity} = \frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{time}}$$

The word **average** matters here. Both quantities describe a whole journey, not a
single moment of it.

**Example 2.** A runner covers 400 m in 50 s. Find the average speed.

$$v = \frac{d}{t}$$

Here $v$ is the average speed in metres per second and $t$ is the time in
seconds.

$$v = \frac{400}{50}$$

$$v = 8\,\text{m/s}$$

**Example 3.** A runner completes one lap of a 400 m track in 50 s and stops at
the starting point. Find the average velocity.

The displacement is 0 m, because the runner finishes where they started.

$$v = \frac{s}{t}$$

$$v = \frac{0}{50}$$

$$v = 0\,\text{m/s}$$

The average speed for the same lap is 8 m/s. Both answers are right, because they
describe different quantities.

> **Watch out.** A fast lap does not mean a large average velocity. If a runner
> returns to the start, the displacement is 0 m, so the average velocity is
> 0 m/s, however fast they ran. Check which quantity the question asks for before
> you divide. A question that says **speed** uses the distance. One that says
> **velocity** uses the displacement.

## Average and instantaneous velocity

**Average velocity** is the displacement divided by the time interval. It is the
steady velocity that would give the same displacement over the same time.

**Instantaneous velocity** is the velocity at one moment. A car's speedometer
shows a value like this, and it changes from second to second.

The two are linked. Shrink the time interval around one moment, and the average
velocity over that interval gets closer to the instantaneous velocity at that
moment. Section 3.4 makes that idea precise with calculus. For now, note the
difference: average velocity describes a whole interval, and instantaneous
velocity describes a single moment.

## Acceleration

**Acceleration** is the rate at which velocity changes. It is a vector, and its SI
unit is metres per second squared, written m/s².

$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$

- $a$ is the acceleration, in m/s²;
- $u$ is the initial velocity, in m/s;
- $v$ is the final velocity, in m/s;
- $t$ is the time taken, in s.

A velocity that changes by 2 m/s every second has an acceleration of 2 m/s².

**Example 4.** A car speeds up from 8 m/s to 20 m/s in 6 s. Find its acceleration.

$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$

$$a = \frac{20 - 8}{6}$$

$$a = \frac{12}{6}$$

$$a = 2\,\text{m/s}^2$$

**Example 5.** A train slows from 30 m/s to 10 m/s in 8 s. Find its acceleration,
taking the direction of travel as positive.

$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$

$$a = \frac{10 - 30}{8}$$

$$a = \frac{-20}{8}$$

$$a = -2.5\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The answer is negative. The acceleration points opposite to the direction of
travel, so the train is slowing down. Its magnitude is 2.5 m/s². When an
acceleration reduces a speed, we call it a **deceleration**.

## Choosing a positive direction

Every problem in this chapter needs one decision before any calculation: which way
along the line counts as positive. Write it down.

Once you have chosen, the signs carry the meaning:

- a positive displacement means the object finished on the positive side of the
  start;
- a positive velocity means it is moving in the positive direction;
- a positive acceleration means the velocity is changing in the positive
  direction.

An object can move in the positive direction and slow down at the same time. Its
velocity is positive and its acceleration is negative. The signs describe
direction, not whether the object is speeding up.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. A ball is kicked from the ground towards a wall. It
leaves at 20 m/s at 30° to the ground. The wall is 3 m high and stands 15 m away.
You want to know whether the ball clears the wall, and where it lands.

You cannot answer that yet. You can do something useful with it now: sort the
numbers by the kind of quantity they are.

Write down the three quantities below, each with its unit.

- the 15 m to the wall;
- the 20 m/s at which the ball leaves;
- the 9.8 m/s² with which gravity pulls the ball down.

For each one, say whether it is a scalar or a vector. Then choose a positive
direction for the horizontal motion and a positive direction for the vertical
motion, and write both choices down. Keep them. Section 3.7 uses them to work out
the ball's height as it passes the wall.

## You should now be able to

- tell distance from displacement, and say which one is a vector;
- tell speed from velocity, and work out an average speed and an average velocity;
- explain the difference between an average velocity and an instantaneous
  velocity;
- find an acceleration from a change in velocity, and read its sign;
- choose a positive direction, and use signs to describe motion.
