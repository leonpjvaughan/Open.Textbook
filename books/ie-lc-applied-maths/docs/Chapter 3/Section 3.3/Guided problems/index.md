# 3.3 Motion graphs — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Find the gradient of the straight line through $(1, 4)$ and $(5, 20)$.
2. Find the area of a triangle with base $6$ and height $10$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $\dfrac{20 - 4}{5 - 1} = \dfrac{16}{4} = 4$. A gradient is a rise divided by
   a run, and on a motion graph that division gives a velocity or an
   acceleration.
2. $\dfrac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 10 = 30$. On a velocity–time graph the area of
   a triangle is a displacement.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A displacement–time graph is a straight line from $(0, 0)$ to
$(5, 25)$. Find the velocity.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The velocity is the gradient of a displacement–time graph.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$\text{gradient} = \frac{25 - 0}{5 - 0}$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{gradient} = \frac{25 - 0}{5 - 0} = 5\,\text{m/s}$$

The velocity is 5 m/s. The line is straight, so the velocity is constant.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A velocity–time graph is a horizontal line at $8$ m/s from $0$ s to
$6$ s. Find the displacement.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The displacement is the area under the graph. Here the area is a rectangle.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$s = 8 \times 6$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$s = 8 \times 6 = 48\,\text{m}$$

The area is a rectangle with height 8 m/s and width 6 s, so the displacement is
48 m.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A velocity–time graph rises in a straight line from $4$ m/s at $0$ s to
$16$ m/s at $6$ s. Find the acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The acceleration is the gradient of a velocity–time graph.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$a = \frac{16 - 4}{6 - 0}$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{16 - 4}{6 - 0} = \frac{12}{6} = 2\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The acceleration is 2 m/s².

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A velocity–time graph is a straight line at $6$ m/s from $0$ s to $4$ s,
then falls in a straight line to $0$ m/s at $10$ s. Find the total displacement.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Split the area into a rectangle and a triangle.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The rectangle is $6 \times 4$. The triangle has base $6$ s and height $6$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$s = 6 \times 4 + \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 6$$

$$s = 24 + 18 = 42\,\text{m}$$

The total displacement is 42 m.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A velocity–time graph is a straight line at $+4$ m/s from $0$ s to $5$ s,
then a straight line at $-2$ m/s from $5$ s to $9$ s. Find the displacement and
the distance travelled.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The area below the axis counts as negative displacement. Distance adds the
sizes of both areas.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The positive area is $4 \times 5 = 20$ m. The negative area is
$2 \times 4 = 8$ m.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$s = 20 - 8 = 12\,\text{m}$$

$$d = 20 + 8 = 28\,\text{m}$$

The displacement is 12 m, because the journey back cancels part of the journey
out. The distance is 28 m, because the object moved along both parts of the
path.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A velocity–time graph passes through the points $(0, 0)$, $(2, 6)$ and
$(4, 12)$. Read the acceleration from the graph.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The three points lie on a straight line, so the gradient is the same between
any pair.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Use the first and last points: $\dfrac{12 - 0}{4 - 0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{12 - 0}{4 - 0} = 3\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The acceleration is 3 m/s².

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** A ball is thrown straight up at $12$ m/s. Its velocity–time graph is a
straight line from $+12$ m/s at $0$ s to $-12$ m/s at $2.45$ s. Find the
greatest height the ball reaches.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The greatest height is the area above the time axis, up to the moment the
velocity is zero. That moment is half of $2.45$ s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The triangle has base $1.224$ s and height $12$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$H = \frac{1}{2} \times 1.224 \times 12 = 7.35\,\text{m}$$

The ball rises about 7.35 m. The area below the axis is not part of this height,
because it describes the fall back down.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 1

**8.** In Chapter 1 a model was judged by what it leaves out. A straight line on
a velocity–time graph is a model: it says the acceleration is constant. State
one thing this model leaves out about a real journey, and one question the
straight line can still answer well.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about traffic, hills and the driver.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A real journey has changes in acceleration that a single straight line cannot
show.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The model leaves out every change in the acceleration. Real traffic, hills and
the driver all alter the acceleration, so a real velocity–time graph is a curve
rather than one straight line.

The model still answers a useful question: the displacement over a short
interval where the acceleration is nearly constant. On that interval the area
under the straight line is close to the area under the real curve.

</details>
</details>
</details>
