# 3.3 Motion graphs — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 3.1, velocity and acceleration; Section 3.2, the
equations of motion.

**Objectives:** read a velocity from the gradient of a displacement–time graph;
read an acceleration from the gradient of a velocity–time graph; read a
displacement from the area under a velocity–time graph; handle a graph that
drops below the time axis.

A graph shows a whole journey at once. The gradient and the area turn the
picture into numbers, and this section shows which is which.

## The displacement–time graph

A **displacement–time graph** plots displacement on the vertical axis and time
on the horizontal axis. Its gradient is the velocity.

- A steeper line means a greater speed.
- A horizontal line means the object is at rest.
- A line sloping down means the object moves in the negative direction.
- A curve means the velocity is changing. The gradient at one point is the
  instantaneous velocity.

**Example 1.** A displacement–time graph is a straight line from $(0, 0)$ to
$(4, 20)$. Find the velocity.

$$\text{gradient} = \frac{20 - 0}{4 - 0}$$

$$= 5\,\text{m/s}$$

The object moves at a steady 5 m/s, because the line is straight.

## The velocity–time graph

A **velocity–time graph** plots velocity on the vertical axis and time on the
horizontal axis. It gives two things.

- The **gradient** is the acceleration.
- The **area** between the graph and the time axis is the displacement.

**Example 2.** A velocity–time graph rises in a straight line from $0$ m/s at
$0$ s to $12$ m/s at $6$ s. Find the acceleration and the displacement.

The gradient gives the acceleration:

$$a = \frac{12 - 0}{6 - 0} = 2\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The area is a triangle with base $6$ s and height $12$ m/s:

$$s = \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 12 = 36\,\text{m}$$

Here $s$ is the displacement in metres.

## Area below the time axis

When the graph dips below the time axis, the velocity is negative. Area above
the axis counts as positive displacement, and area below it counts as negative.

The **distance** travelled adds the size of every area, because distance counts
the whole path.

**Example 3.** A velocity–time graph is a straight line at $+6$ m/s from $0$ s
to $4$ s, then a straight line at $-3$ m/s from $4$ s to $6$ s. Find the
displacement and the distance.

The positive area is $6 \times 4 = 24$ m. The negative area is
$3 \times 2 = 6$ m.

$$s = 24 - 6 = 18\,\text{m}$$

$$d = 24 + 6 = 30\,\text{m}$$

Here $d$ is the distance travelled, in metres.

The displacement is 18 m and the distance is 30 m.

> **Watch out.** The height of a velocity–time graph is the velocity, not the
> speed, and not the distance. Three quantities come from three features. The
> gradient is the acceleration, the area is the displacement, and the height is
> the velocity itself. Reading the height as a distance is the most common
> error. Check the axis labels before you read anything from the graph.

## Sketching a graph from a motion

You can sketch a graph from a description. Decide first what the axes are, then
track the velocity through time.

**Example 4.** A ball is thrown straight up at $10$ m/s and caught again at the
same height. Sketch its velocity–time graph.

The velocity starts at $+10$ m/s. Gravity reduces it by $9.8$ m/s each second,
so the graph is a straight line falling to zero at about $1.02$ s. It then goes
negative, and at $2.04$ s it is $-10$ m/s. The line is straight, because the
acceleration is constant.

The area above the axis is a triangle with base $1.02$ s and height $10$ m/s:

$$\frac{1}{2} \times 1.02 \times 10 = 5.1\,\text{m}$$

That is the greatest height the ball reaches.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The ball's vertical velocity starts at $+10$ m/s.
Gravity reduces it by $9.8$ m/s each second.

Sketch the vertical velocity–time graph from $t = 0$ to $t = 2.04$ s. The graph
is a straight line. It crosses the time axis at $t = 1.02$ s.

Now read the greatest height from the area above the axis. That area is a
triangle with base $1.02$ s and height $10$ m/s.

$$\text{area} = \frac{1}{2} \times 1.02 \times 10 = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\,\text{m}$$

The area is about $5.1$ m, so the ball rises about $5.1$ m. Section 3.7 finds
its height at the wall.

## You should now be able to

- read a velocity from the gradient of a displacement–time graph;
- read an acceleration from the gradient of a velocity–time graph;
- read a displacement from the area under a velocity–time graph;
- treat area below the time axis as negative displacement, and add sizes to get
  the distance;
- sketch a velocity–time graph from a description of a motion.
