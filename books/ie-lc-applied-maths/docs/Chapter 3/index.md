# Chapter 3: Describing motion

Motion is the first thing applied mathematics learns to describe. You already
know how to measure a distance and a time. This chapter turns those measurements
into a model, and the model predicts where a moving object will be next.

## The chapter problem

**The kick that has to clear the wall.** A ball is kicked from the ground
towards a wall. It leaves at $20$ m/s at $30^\circ$ to the ground. The wall is
$3$ m high and stands $15$ m away. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$.

Two questions follow from that one picture.

- Does the ball clear the wall?
- Where does the ball land?

You cannot answer either yet, and you are not expected to. Make a prediction
first. Write down whether you think the ball clears the wall, and roughly where
it lands. Keep your prediction.

By the end of the chapter you can split the ball's velocity into a horizontal
part and a vertical part, follow each part through time, and find both answers.
Then you can check your prediction.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- use the four equations of motion for constant acceleration, and choose the
  one that fits the quantities you know;
- read a velocity and an acceleration from the gradient of a motion graph, and
  read a displacement from the area under a velocity–time graph;
- differentiate a displacement to get a velocity, including with the chain rule;
- integrate a velocity to get a displacement, and use the fundamental theorem of
  calculus;
- derive the equations of motion from $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt}$ by calculus;
- treat the horizontal and vertical parts of a projectile's motion separately;
- find a time of flight, a range and a greatest height;
- find a projectile's range at any launch angle, and say which angle gives the
  greatest range.

## Where it is used

Anything that moves is described this way. A car's braking distance, a train's
timetable, a ball's flight and a rocket's climb all use the same few equations.
Sports coaches use the range of a projectile to plan a jump. Engineers use the
same model to design a runway, a roller coaster and a road's stopping distance.

## Your turn to connect it

Somewhere in your own week something travels through the air and has to reach a
target: a basketball shot, a football pass, a stone skimmed across water, or a
splash from a diving board.

Choose one. Write down what you can measure about it, and what you would like to
predict. You will be able to answer the question properly by the end of the
chapter.
