# Chapter 3: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The kick that has to clear the wall.** A ball is kicked from the ground
towards a wall. It leaves at $20$ m/s at $30^\circ$ to the ground. The wall is
$3$ m high and stands $15$ m away. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

**Split the velocity.** The horizontal component is constant, because no force
acts sideways. The vertical component changes, because gravity acts downwards.

$$u_x = 20\cos 30^\circ = 17.32\,\text{m/s}$$

$$u_y = 20\sin 30^\circ = 10\,\text{m/s}$$

Here $u_x$ and $u_y$ are the horizontal and vertical components of the
velocity, $t$ is the time, and $y$ is the height. $T$ is the time of flight and
$R$ is the range.

**Does the ball clear the wall?** The wall is $15$ m away. Find the time from
the horizontal motion, then the height from the vertical motion.

$$t = \frac{15}{17.32} = 0.866\,\text{s}$$

$$y = 10 \times 0.866 - 4.9 \times 0.866^2 = 8.66 - 3.675 = 4.99\,\text{m}$$

The ball is about $4.99$ m high when it passes the wall. The wall is $3$ m high,
so the ball clears it by about $2$ m. If you predicted that, your reasoning was
right.

**Where does the ball land?** The ball lands when its height returns to zero.
The time of flight is

$$T = \frac{2u_y}{g} = \frac{20}{9.8} = 2.04\,\text{s}.$$

The range is the horizontal velocity times the time of flight:

$$R = u_x T = 17.32 \times 2.04 = 35.35\,\text{m}.$$

The ball lands about $35$ m from the kick, well beyond the wall. Its greatest
height is $5.10$ m, reached at the halfway time of about $1.02$ s.

**The whole picture.** One problem needed four ideas from four sections:
resolving a velocity into components, the equations of motion, the time of
flight, and the range. The method is always the same. Split the velocity, find
the time from one direction, and use that time in the other.

**And if the angle changed?** At $60^\circ$ the ball would land the same
$35.35$ m away, because $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ are complementary. The
greatest range for this speed is $\dfrac{400}{9.8} = 40.82$ m, at $45^\circ$.
The ball would still clear the $3$ m wall at either $30^\circ$ or $60^\circ$.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which method you will
use and why** before you calculate anything. Some of them need an idea from an
earlier chapter, and two of them are about choosing the right method rather than
the right number.

1. A car accelerates uniformly from $12$ m/s to $30$ m/s in $6$ s. Find its
   acceleration and the distance it travels.
2. A displacement–time graph is a curve that rises steeply and then flattens.
   Describe what the object is doing, and say what the gradient at one point
   means.
3. Differentiate $s = (3t + 1)^4$ with respect to $t$.
4. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^3 (2t + 4)\,dt$.
5. Derive $v = u + at$ from $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt}$ for a constant acceleration.
6. A ball is thrown at $18$ m/s at $40^\circ$ above the horizontal. Find the two
   components of its velocity, and its greatest height.
7. A stone is dropped from rest from a cliff and lands after $3$ s. Taking
   $g = 9.8$ m/s², find the height of the cliff.
8. A ball is kicked at $24$ m/s at $30^\circ$. Find its time of flight and its
   range.
9. A ball is thrown at $20$ m/s at $60^\circ$. Find its range, and name the
   other launch angle that gives the same range.
10. A ball is kicked at $16$ m/s and lands $22$ m away on level ground. Find the
    two possible launch angles.
11. A question gives the initial velocity, the acceleration and the time, and
    asks for the displacement. Say which equation of motion you would use, and
    why you would not use the others.
12. A network has vertex degrees $3, 3, 2, 2, 2$. Find the number of edges, and
    name the result you used. This question comes from Chapter 2, and it is here
    to remind you to check which chapter a question belongs to.
13. In the projectile model, gravity is the only force. State which assumption
    you would examine first if the ball were a table-tennis ball, and why.

## What you can now do

- tell distance from displacement, and speed from velocity, and use a sign to
  show a direction;
- use the four equations of motion for constant acceleration, and choose the
  one that fits the quantities you know;
- read a velocity and an acceleration from the gradient of a motion graph, and a
  displacement from the area under a velocity–time graph;
- differentiate a power and a composite function with the chain rule;
- integrate a power, and evaluate a definite integral with the fundamental
  theorem of calculus;
- derive the equations of motion from $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt}$ by calculus;
- resolve a launch velocity, and treat the horizontal and vertical motions
  separately;
- find a time of flight, a range and a greatest height;
- find a range at any launch angle, and say which angle gives the greatest
  range.

## Thinking back

Look again at the throw, kick or jump you chose at the start of the chapter.
You wrote down what you could measure and what you would like to predict.

You can now answer the question. Split the launch velocity into a horizontal and
a vertical component. Follow each one through the same time. The horizontal
component stays constant, and the vertical component changes at $9.8$ m/s each
second. That is the whole method.

If your measurements are rough, say so, and say which number you are least sure
of. If the object spins or the air pushes on it, the model leaves that out. That
is worth writing down too. Deciding what to leave out is what makes a model
useful, and saying what you left out is part of the applied mathematics. It is
also what the modelling project asks you to do.
