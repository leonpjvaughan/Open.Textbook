# 3.7 Projectile motion — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Find $20\sin 30^\circ$ and $20\cos 30^\circ$, each to $3$ significant
   figures.
2. A ball falls from rest for $2$ s. Taking $g = 9.8$ m/s², find its vertical
   velocity.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $20\sin 30^\circ = 10$ and $20\cos 30^\circ = 17.3$. A launch speed is split
   into these two components.
2. $v = gt = 9.8 \times 2 = 19.6$ m/s downwards. The vertical motion of a
   projectile is the same as this, once you know its starting vertical velocity.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**The symbols.** Here $u$ is the launch speed and $\theta$ the launch angle, so
$u_x = u\cos\theta$ and $u_y = u\sin\theta$ are the components of the
velocity. $x$ is the horizontal distance and $y$ is the height, both in
metres, and $g$ is the acceleration of free fall.

**1.** A ball is kicked at $20$ m/s at $30^\circ$ above the horizontal. Find
the horizontal and vertical components of its velocity, each to $3$
significant figures.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $u_x = u\cos\theta$ and $u_y = u\sin\theta$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$u_x = 20\cos 30^\circ \qquad u_y = 20\sin 30^\circ$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$u_x = 20\cos 30^\circ = 17.32\,\text{m/s}$$

$$u_y = 20\sin 30^\circ = 10\,\text{m/s}$$

The two components are at right angles and have the same effect as the single
$20$ m/s velocity.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A stone is thrown with a vertical component of $15$ m/s upwards. Taking
$g = 9.8$ m/s², find its height after $2$ s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The vertical motion is $y = u_y t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$y = 15 \times 2 - 4.9 \times 2^2$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$y = 30 - 19.6 = 10.4\,\text{m}$$

The stone is 10.4 m high after 2 s. The horizontal component does not change
this, because gravity acts downwards only.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A ball has a horizontal component of $17.32$ m/s. A wall stands $15$ m
away. Find the time the ball takes to reach the wall.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The horizontal velocity is constant, so use $x = u_x t$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$t = \frac{x}{u_x} = \frac{15}{17.32}$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$t = \frac{15}{17.32} = 0.866\,\text{s}$$

The ball reaches the wall after about 0.866 s. The vertical motion does not
change this time.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** The same ball has a vertical component of $10$ m/s upwards and reaches
the wall after $0.866$ s. Taking $g = 9.8$ m/s², find its height at the wall.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the time you found in question 3 in the vertical equation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$y = 10 \times 0.866 - 4.9 \times 0.866^2$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$y = 8.66 - 3.675 = 4.99\,\text{m}$$

The ball is about 4.99 m high when it passes the wall. A wall 3 m high is
cleared by about 2 m.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A ball has a horizontal component of $12$ m/s and is in the air for
$3$ s. Find the horizontal distance it travels.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The horizontal velocity is constant, so use $x = u_x t$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$x = 12 \times 3$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$x = 12 \times 3 = 36\,\text{m}$$

The ball travels 36 m horizontally. The vertical motion shares the same 3 s
without changing the horizontal distance.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A ball is thrown at $25$ m/s at $40^\circ$ above the horizontal. Its
vertical component is $16.07$ m/s. Taking $g = 9.8$ m/s², find its height after
$1.5$ s, to $3$ significant figures.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Substitute into $y = u_y t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$y = 16.07 \times 1.5 - 4.9 \times 1.5^2$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$y = 24.105 - 11.025 = 13.08\,\text{m}$$

The ball is 13.08 m high after 1.5 s, to three significant figures.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** One ball is dropped from a height. At the same moment, a second ball is
projected horizontally from the same height. Explain which ball lands first,
and why.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the vertical motions of the two balls.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Both balls start with a vertical velocity of zero, and gravity is the only
vertical force on either of them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Both balls start with a vertical velocity of zero, so their vertical motions
are identical. They land at the same time.

The projected ball also moves horizontally, so it lands further from the
starting point. That horizontal motion uses the same time without changing it.
The two directions are independent.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 1

**8.** In Chapter 1 a model was judged by what it leaves out. The projectile
model here assumes that gravity is the only force on the ball. State one thing
this model leaves out, and one question it can still answer well.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the air, and about the size and speed of the ball.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Air resistance pushes back along the path of the ball.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The model leaves out air resistance, the spin of the ball and any wind. A light
ball such as a table-tennis ball feels all three, so its real path falls short
of the model.

The model still answers a useful question well: the path of a heavy, compact
ball over a short flight, such as a cricket ball or a shot put. For those, the
effect of the air is small compared with the pull of gravity.

</details>
</details>
</details>
