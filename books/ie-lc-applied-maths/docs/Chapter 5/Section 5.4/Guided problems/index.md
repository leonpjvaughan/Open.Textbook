# 5.4 Statics on a rough plane — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Work out $\arctan 0.5$ in degrees, to one decimal place.
2. Work out $0.4 \times 10 \times 9.8 \times 0.8660$, to four significant
   figures.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $26.6^\circ$. That is the greatest angle for rest when the coefficient is
   $0.5$.
2. $33.95$. That is a limiting friction: coefficient $0.4$ on a normal reaction
   of $84.87$ N.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A block rests on a rough plane. The coefficient of friction is $0.3$.
Find the greatest angle at which the block stays at rest, to one decimal place.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At the critical angle, $\tan\theta = \mu$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$\theta = \arctan 0.3$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

At the point of sliding, $mg\sin\theta = \mu mg\cos\theta$, so
$\tan\theta = \mu = 0.3$. Then

$$\theta = \arctan 0.3 = 16.7^\circ$$

The block stays at rest on any slope up to $16.7^\circ$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A block rests on a rough plane. The coefficient of friction is $0.75$.
Find the greatest angle at which the block stays at rest, to one decimal place.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $\theta = \arctan\mu$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$\theta = \arctan 0.75$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\theta = \arctan 0.75 = 36.9^\circ$$

The block stays at rest on any slope up to $36.9^\circ$. A larger coefficient
means a steeper slope is needed before it slides.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A block of mass $10$ kg rests on a rough plane inclined at $30^\circ$.
The coefficient of friction is $0.3$. Find the least force up the plane that
holds the block at rest, and the force needed to just start it moving up the
plane, each to four significant figures. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the weight component down the plane and the limiting friction first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$mg\sin 30^\circ = 49\,\text{N}$$

$$\mu mg\cos 30^\circ = 0.3 \times 84.87 = 25.46\,\text{N}$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The weight component down the plane is $49$ N. The limiting friction is

$$\mu mg\cos 30^\circ = 0.3 \times 10 \times 9.8 \times 0.8660 = 25.46\,\text{N}$$

With the smallest force, the block is on the point of sliding down, so friction
acts up the plane:

$$P = 49 - 25.46 = 23.54\,\text{N}$$

With the force about to move the block up, friction acts down the plane:

$$P = 49 + 25.46 = 74.46\,\text{N}$$

Any force between 23.54 N and 74.46 N holds the block at rest.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A block of mass $8$ kg rests on a rough plane inclined at $45^\circ$. The
coefficient of friction is $0.25$. Find the least force up the plane that holds
the block at rest, and the force needed to just start it moving up the plane,
each to four significant figures. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

On a $45^\circ$ plane, both the weight component and the normal reaction use
$0.7071$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$mg\sin 45^\circ = 8 \times 9.8 \times 0.7071 = 55.44\,\text{N}$$

$$\mu mg\cos 45^\circ = 0.25 \times 55.44 = 13.86\,\text{N}$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The weight component down the plane is $55.44$ N. The limiting friction is

$$\mu mg\cos 45^\circ = 0.25 \times 8 \times 9.8 \times 0.7071 = 13.86\,\text{N}$$

The least force holds the block against sliding down, so friction acts up the
plane:

$$P = 55.44 - 13.86 = 41.58\,\text{N}$$

The force to start it moving up has friction acting down the plane:

$$P = 55.44 + 13.86 = 69.30\,\text{N}$$

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A block of mass $10$ kg rests on a rough horizontal floor. The
coefficient of friction is $0.4$. A force acts at $30^\circ$ above the
horizontal. Find the smallest force that just starts the block moving, to four
significant figures. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The upward part of the force reduces the normal reaction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$R = mg - P\sin 30^\circ = 98 - 0.5P$$

$$P\cos 30^\circ = 0.4R$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Perpendicular to the floor, the upward part of the force is $P\sin 30^\circ$,
so

$$R = 98 - 0.5P$$

On the point of moving, friction is limiting, so

$$P\cos 30^\circ = 0.4R$$

Substituting for $R$ gives $0.8660P = 0.4(98 - 0.5P)$. Expanding and
collecting:

$$0.8660P + 0.2P = 39.2$$

$$P = \frac{39.2}{1.0660} = 36.77\,\text{N}$$

The force needed is 36.77 N. A horizontal push would need $39.2$ N, so the pull
at an angle is more efficient.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A block of mass $5$ kg rests on a rough plane inclined at $20^\circ$.
The coefficient of friction is $0.5$. Find the friction force, and show that the
block does not slide. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The block is at rest, so the friction force $F$ balances the weight component
down the plane.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$F = mg\sin 20^\circ = 5 \times 9.8 \times 0.3420$$

The greatest friction is $\mu mg\cos 20^\circ$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The block is at rest, so the forces along the plane balance:

$$F = mg\sin 20^\circ = 5 \times 9.8 \times 0.3420 = 16.76\,\text{N}$$

Friction is 16.76 N up the plane. The greatest friction the plane can provide is

$$\mu mg\cos 20^\circ = 0.5 \times 5 \times 9.8 \times 0.9397 = 23.02\,\text{N}$$

The friction needed, 16.76 N, is less than the greatest available, 23.02 N, so
the block does not slide.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** Explain why an extra force acting at an angle changes the friction force
on a body at rest.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Friction depends on the normal reaction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

An extra force with a component perpendicular to the surface changes $R$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The limiting friction is $\mu R$, so anything that changes the normal reaction
changes the friction. An extra force at an angle has a component perpendicular
to the surface. If that component presses the body into the surface, $R$ grows,
and the greatest friction grows with it. If the component lifts the body, $R$
falls, and the greatest friction falls.

The extra force also has a component along the surface, which acts with or
against the other forces there. Both components matter, so resolve the force
into the two directions first.

</details>
</details>
</details>
