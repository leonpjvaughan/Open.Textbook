# 5.5 Drag proportional to a power of speed — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Work out $0.3 \times 10^2$.
2. Solve $2a = 14.6$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $30$. That is a drag force at $10$ m/s when $k = 0.30$ kg/m.
2. $a = 7.3$. Dividing both sides by 2 gives an acceleration when the resultant
   force is 14.6 N and the mass is 2 kg.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** An object falls with $D = kv$, where $k = 0.6$ kg/s. Find the drag at
$8$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Substitute the speed into $D = kv$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$D = 0.6 \times 8$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$D = kv = 0.6 \times 8 = 4.8\,\text{N}$$

The drag is 4.8 N, acting upwards while the object falls.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** An object falls with $D = kv^2$, where $k = 0.5$ kg/m. Find the drag at
$12$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Square the speed first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$D = 0.5 \times 12^2 = 0.5 \times 144$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$D = kv^2 = 0.5 \times 12^2 = 0.5 \times 144 = 72\,\text{N}$$

The drag is 72 N. Because the speed is squared, doubling the speed multiplies
the drag by four.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A ball of mass $1.5$ kg falls through the air. The drag force is $4$ N.
Find the acceleration, to three significant figures. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The weight acts down and the drag acts up.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$mg = 1.5 \times 9.8 = 14.7\,\text{N}$$

$$1.5a = 14.7 - 4$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$mg = 1.5 \times 9.8 = 14.7\,\text{N}$$

The resultant downwards is $14.7 - 4 = 10.7$ N, so

$$a = \frac{10.7}{1.5} = 7.13\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The acceleration is 7.13 m/s² downwards, less than $g$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A ball of mass $0.4$ kg is thrown vertically upwards. The drag force is
$1.6$ N. Find the acceleration, taking the upward direction as positive. Take
$g = 9.8$ m/s².

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

On the way up, the weight and the drag both act downwards.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$0.4a = -(3.92 + 1.6)$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$mg = 0.4 \times 9.8 = 3.92\,\text{N}$$

While the ball rises, both forces act downwards:

$$0.4a = -(3.92 + 1.6) = -5.52$$

$$a = \frac{-5.52}{0.4} = -13.8\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The acceleration is $-13.8$ m/s², so the ball slows down.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** For an object falling through the air, the drag is proportional to the
square of the speed. At $5$ m/s the drag is $10$ N. Find the drag at $15$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The speed is multiplied by 3. What happens to the square of the speed?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$D_2 = D_1 \left(\frac{v_2}{v_1}\right)^2 = 10 \times 3^2$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The speed rises from $5$ m/s to $15$ m/s, a factor of $3$. The drag depends on
the square of the speed, so the drag rises by a factor of $3^2 = 9$:

$$D = 10 \times 9 = 90\,\text{N}$$

The drag at 15 m/s is 90 N.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A parachutist of mass $80$ kg descends at a constant speed. Find the
drag force. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A constant speed means the acceleration is zero.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The resultant force is zero, so the drag balances the weight.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A constant speed means zero acceleration, so the resultant force is zero. The
drag upwards balances the weight downwards:

$$D = mg = 80 \times 9.8 = 784\,\text{N}$$

The drag is 784 N. This is the same balance that gives terminal velocity in
Section 5.6.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** Explain why the acceleration of a falling object falls as its speed
increases.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What happens to the drag as the speed grows?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The resultant force is the weight minus the drag.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The weight of the object stays the same, but the drag grows as the speed grows.
The resultant force is the weight minus the drag, so the resultant falls as the
drag rises. Since $F = ma$, the acceleration falls with it.

The object still speeds up, but more and more slowly. If it falls far enough,
the drag grows until it equals the weight. The resultant is then zero, the
acceleration is zero, and the speed stops rising.

</details>
</details>
</details>
