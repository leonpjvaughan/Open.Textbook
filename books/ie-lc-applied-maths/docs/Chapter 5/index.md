# Chapter 5: Friction and resistance

Nothing slides for ever. Push a book across a table and it stops. Drop a
feather and it drifts down slowly. Something is taking energy out of the
motion, and this chapter puts a number on it. You will model friction between
surfaces and resistance from the air.

## The chapter problem

**The skier on the slope.** A skier of total mass $70$ kg, including equipment,
is on a straight slope inclined at $30^\circ$ to the horizontal. The coefficient
of friction between the skis and the snow is $0.20$. The air resistance on the
skier is proportional to the square of the speed, $D = kv^2$, with
$k = 0.30$ kg/m. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

You cannot answer the questions yet, and you are not expected to. Make a
prediction first.

- Will the skier slide from rest, or stay still?
- Will the skier keep speeding up for ever, or reach a top speed?

Write both predictions down and keep them. Then the questions:

- What is the normal reaction from the snow?
- What is the friction force while the skier slides?
- What is the acceleration at the start, before air resistance matters?
- On a gentler slope, what is the steepest angle at which the skier can stand
  still?
- How large is the air resistance at $10$ m/s?
- What is the fastest the skier can go on this slope?

By the end of the chapter you can find the normal reaction, work out the
limiting friction, use $F = ma$ along a rough slope, solve a statics problem,
and find a terminal speed. Then you can check your predictions.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- explain what friction is, and say which way it acts;
- find the normal reaction on a horizontal surface and on a slope;
- explain why friction is not always at its maximum value;
- define the coefficient of friction, and use $F = \mu R$;
- find the coefficient from the angle at which a block just slides;
- find the acceleration of a particle on a rough plane, both up and down;
- decide whether a particle on a rough slope stays at rest or slides;
- solve a statics problem on a rough plane, including the greatest angle for
  rest;
- write the drag force for resistance proportional to $v$ or to $v^2$;
- find a terminal velocity from the balance of forces.

## Where it is used

Friction is what lets a car grip the road, a climber hold a rock and a brake
stop a wheel. It is also what wears out an engine and heats up a bearing.
Engineers choose the materials and the surfaces to get the friction they want,
and to avoid the friction they do not. Air resistance sets the top speed of a
cyclist, the fuel used by a lorry and the landing speed of a parachute. Every
vehicle, from a skateboard to a spacecraft, is designed around the balance
between the forces driving it and the resistance slowing it down.

## Your turn to connect it

Somewhere in your week, friction or resistance decides what happens. A bicycle
braking on a wet road, a slip on a tiled floor, a ball falling through the air,
a swimmer pushing through water, a bus slowing down at a stop.

Choose one of these, or one of your own. Write down the two surfaces that rub
together, or the object and the fluid it moves through. Say which way the
resistance acts, and what would happen if it were removed. You will be able to
put numbers on it by the end of the chapter.
