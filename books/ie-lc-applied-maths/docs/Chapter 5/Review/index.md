# Chapter 5: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The skier on the slope.** A skier of total mass $70$ kg is on a slope
inclined at $30^\circ$. The coefficient of friction $\mu$ is $0.20$, and the air
resistance is $D = kv^2$ with $k = 0.30$ kg/m. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

**Does the skier slide from rest?** The slope slides the skier when
$\tan\theta > \mu$. Here $\tan 30^\circ = 0.577$, and $0.577 > 0.20$. So the
skier slides. If you predicted that, your reasoning was right.

**The normal reaction $R$.** The skier does not move perpendicular to the slope,
so the forces in that direction balance:

$$R = mg\cos 30^\circ = 70 \times 9.8 \times 0.8660 = 594.1\,\text{N}$$

**The friction force $F$.** The skier is sliding, so friction is limiting:

$$F = \mu R = 0.20 \times 594.1 = 118.8\,\text{N}$$

Friction acts up the slope, against the sliding.

**The acceleration at the start.** At low speed the air resistance is small, so
the only forces along the slope are the weight component and friction. The
weight component down the slope is

$$mg\sin 30^\circ = 70 \times 9.8 \times 0.5 = 343\,\text{N}$$

The resultant is $343 - 118.8 = 224.2$ N, so

$$a = \frac{224.2}{70} = 3.20\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The same answer comes from $a = g(\sin 30^\circ - \mu\cos 30^\circ)$.

**The steepest slope for standing still.** If the skier stands still, friction
is not limiting; it takes only the value needed. The greatest angle comes from
the point of sliding, where $\tan\theta = \mu = 0.20$. So
$\theta = \arctan 0.20 = 11.3^\circ$. On the $30^\circ$ slope the skier cannot
stand still. On any slope gentler than $11.3^\circ$, the skier stays put.

**The air resistance at 10 m/s.** Substitute into $D = kv^2$:

$$D = 0.30 \times 10^2 = 30\,\text{N}$$

At this speed the drag is still small next to the $224.2$ N resultant.

**The fastest speed on the slope.** As the skier speeds up, the drag grows. The
skier stops speeding up when the resultant force along the slope is zero, so
the drag supplies exactly the balance:

$$kv^2 = 343 - 118.8 = 224.2$$

$$v^2 = \frac{224.2}{0.30} = 747.3$$

$$v = 27.3\,\text{m/s}$$

The skier reaches a terminal speed of $27.3$ m/s, about $98$ km/h. The
prediction was right: the skier does not speed up for ever.

**The whole picture.** One problem needed an idea from every section: the normal
reaction on a slope, the coefficient of friction, $F = ma$ on a rough plane, the
greatest angle for rest, the drag law, and terminal velocity. The method is the
same each time: draw the forces, resolve along the slope and perpendicular to
it, then write the resultant along the slope.

**A last change.** Suppose the snow were icy and the coefficient fell to
$0.05$. The friction would drop to $0.05 \times 594.1 = 29.7$ N, the
acceleration would rise to $\dfrac{343 - 29.7}{70} = 4.48$ m/s², and the
terminal speed would rise to $\sqrt{\dfrac{313.3}{0.30}} = 32.3$ m/s. Less
friction means a faster skier.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which kind of problem
it is** and say why, before you calculate anything. Some of them need a method
from an earlier chapter, and two of them are about choosing the right idea
rather than the right number.

1. A box of mass $8$ kg rests on a rough horizontal floor. A horizontal force
   of $30$ N just starts it moving. Find the coefficient of friction.
2. A block of mass $6$ kg slides down a rough plane inclined at $30^\circ$. The
   coefficient of friction is $0.25$. Find the acceleration.
3. A ball of mass $0.2$ kg falls through the air with a drag force of $0.5$ N.
   Find its acceleration.
4. A car speeds up from $12$ m/s to $30$ m/s in $5$ s. Find its acceleration.
   Which chapter does this question belong to, and why?
5. A crate of mass $20$ kg rests on a smooth plane inclined at $30^\circ$. Find
   its acceleration down the plane.
6. A parachutist of mass $75$ kg descends at a constant speed. Find the drag
   force.
7. An object of mass $10$ kg falls with $D = kv^2$ and $k = 0.5$ kg/m. Find its
   terminal speed.
8. A block rests on a rough plane inclined at $20^\circ$ with $\mu = 0.3$.
   Decide whether it slides, and justify your answer.
9. Masses of $4$ kg and $7$ kg hang from a light string over a smooth pulley.
   Find the acceleration and the tension.
10. A skier of mass $60$ kg goes down a slope inclined at $30^\circ$. The
    coefficient of friction is $0.1$ and the drag is $D = kv^2$ with
    $k = 0.4$ kg/m. Find the acceleration at low speed, and then the terminal
    speed. Which two sections of this chapter does this question join together?
11. A student writes: "The friction force always equals $\mu R$." Give one
    situation in this chapter where that is true, and one where it is false,
    with a reason for each.
12. A block is projected up a rough plane inclined at $30^\circ$, with
    $\mu = 0.2$. Find its deceleration. Explain why this is larger than the
    acceleration of the same block down the same plane.

## What you can now do

- explain what friction is, and say that it acts along the surface against
  relative motion;
- find the normal reaction on a horizontal surface and on a slope, including
  when an extra force presses into the surface;
- explain why friction is a response, and why it is only sometimes equal to
  $\mu R$;
- find the coefficient of friction from a force and a normal reaction, and from
  the angle at which a block just slides;
- find the acceleration of a particle on a rough plane, down the plane and up
  it;
- decide whether a particle stays at rest on a rough slope;
- find the greatest angle for rest, and the range of a holding force, on a
  rough plane;
- write a drag force proportional to $v$ or to $v^2$, and find the acceleration
  it produces;
- find a terminal velocity, on its own and on a rough slope.

## Thinking back

Look again at the situation you chose at the start of the chapter: the bicycle
on a wet road, the tiled floor, the ball in the air, the swimmer, or the bus at
the stop.

Name the two surfaces that rub together, or the object and the fluid it moves
through. Write down which way the resistance acts, and what would change if the
surfaces were smoother or rougher. Then decide which question you are asking. Is
the object in equilibrium, so that friction is not limiting? Or is it sliding,
so that friction is at its maximum?

Write down your answer and your reasoning. If you cannot put a number on one of
the forces, say so. Deciding what you can and cannot measure is part of
modelling, and it is exactly what the modelling project asks you to do.
