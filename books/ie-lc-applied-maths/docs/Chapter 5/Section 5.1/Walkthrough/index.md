# 5.1 Friction and the normal reaction — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 4.2, resolving a force; Section 4.3, the normal
reaction on a horizontal surface; Section 4.4, the normal reaction on a slope.

**Objectives:** explain what friction is and which way it acts; find the normal
reaction on a horizontal surface and on a slope; explain why friction is not
always at its maximum value.

Every surface grips the object on it. That grip is a force, and this section
describes it. You also meet the force that sets its limit: the normal reaction.

## What friction is

**Friction** is a force that acts along a surface where two objects touch. It
opposes the relative sliding of the two surfaces.

The rule for its direction is short. Friction acts against the motion, or
against the motion that is trying to start.

- If the object is sliding, friction acts against the sliding.
- If the object is at rest, friction acts against whatever is trying to move it.

Friction comes from the roughness of the two surfaces. Even a polished surface
has small bumps and hollows, and the two sets grip each other. The rougher the
surfaces, the larger the friction.

Friction acts **along** the surface. It is parallel to the surface, never
perpendicular to it. The perpendicular force has a different name.

## The normal reaction

The **normal reaction** is the force from a surface, perpendicular to it. The
word *normal* means at right angles, so the normal reaction is at right angles
to the surface.

On a horizontal surface, the normal reaction balances the weight:

$$R = mg$$

On a plane inclined at $\theta$ to the horizontal, only part of the weight
presses into the surface:

$$R = mg\cos\theta$$

The normal reaction changes if another force pushes the object into the surface
or pulls it away. A force pressing into the surface increases $R$. A force with
a part lifting the object decreases $R$.

**Example 1.** A box of mass $12$ kg rests on a horizontal floor. Find the
normal reaction. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

The box does not move vertically, so the vertical forces balance:

$$R = mg$$

$$R = 12 \times 9.8$$

$$R = 117.6\,\text{N}$$

The normal reaction is 117.6 N upwards.

**Example 2.** A block of mass $8$ kg rests on a plane inclined at $30^\circ$
to the horizontal. Find the normal reaction. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

The block does not move perpendicular to the plane, so those forces balance:

$$R = mg\cos 30^\circ$$

$$R = 8 \times 9.8 \times 0.8660$$

$$R = 67.90\,\text{N}$$

The normal reaction is 67.90 N, to four significant figures. It is smaller than
the weight of $78.4$ N, because the plane is tilted.

## Friction has a limit

Friction is not a fixed force. It is a response to the other forces, and it
takes the value needed to keep the object at rest.

- While the object stays at rest, friction can be anything from zero up to a
  maximum.
- The maximum is called the **limiting friction**.
- While the object slides, friction sits at that maximum.

The maximum depends on the normal reaction and on how rough the two surfaces
are. Section 5.2 gives the rule, but the shape of it is worth stating now: the
larger the normal reaction, the larger the maximum friction.

**Example 3.** A block of mass $10$ kg rests on a plane inclined at $30^\circ$.
A force of $30$ N acts on it up the plane and the block stays at rest. Find the
friction force. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

The component of the weight down the plane is

$$mg\sin 30^\circ = 10 \times 9.8 \times 0.5 = 49\,\text{N}$$

The block is at rest, so the forces along the plane balance. The 30 N force
acts up the plane, so friction must supply the rest:

$$F = 49 - 30 = 19\,\text{N}$$

Friction is 19 N up the plane. It is not at its maximum, because the block is
not sliding. It takes only the value needed to hold the block.

> **Watch out.** Friction is not always at its maximum. A block at rest has
> friction equal to the force trying to move it, and no more. Two errors
> follow. The first is to write $F = \mu R$ for a block that is sitting still
> and is not about to move; there, friction is smaller than that. The second is
> to add friction to the normal reaction as if they were one force. They act at
> right angles, so keep them apart. Draw both on the diagram, mark the direction
> of each, and use the perpendicular direction to find $R$ before you touch
> friction.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The skier of mass $70$ kg is on a slope inclined
at $30^\circ$.

The skier does not move perpendicular to the slope, so the normal reaction
balances the component of the weight into the slope:

$$R = mg\cos 30^\circ = 70 \times 9.8 \times 0.8660 = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\,\text{N}$$

Keep that number. Section 5.2 uses it to find the friction force, and every
later section uses both.

## You should now be able to

- say what friction is, and that it acts along the surface against relative
  sliding;
- give the direction of friction for a sliding object and for one at rest;
- find the normal reaction on a horizontal surface from $R = mg$;
- find the normal reaction on a slope from $R = mg\cos\theta$;
- say how an extra force into or away from the surface changes $R$;
- explain why friction is only sometimes equal to its maximum value.
