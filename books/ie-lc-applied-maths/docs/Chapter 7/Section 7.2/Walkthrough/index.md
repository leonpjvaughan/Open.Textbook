# 7.2 Centripetal acceleration and force — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 4.1, forces and Newton's laws; Section 4.3, using
$F = ma$; Section 7.1, angular velocity and $v = \omega r$.

**Objectives:** explain why motion in a circle at constant speed is still
accelerating; use $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ and $a = \omega^2 r$; use
$F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$; state the direction of the acceleration and the force.

A car going round a roundabout keeps a steady speed, yet something is changing.
The speed is constant but the direction of travel is not, and velocity is a
vector. A change in direction is a change in velocity, so there is an
acceleration. This section finds its size and direction.

## Why circular motion is accelerating

Velocity has a size and a direction. A body moving in a circle at constant speed
has a velocity that points along the tangent, and the tangent turns as the body
moves. So the velocity changes, and the body accelerates.

The acceleration points towards the centre of the circle at every instant. This
is the **centripetal acceleration**, and its size is

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r}$$

where $v$ is the linear speed and $r$ is the radius. The word centripetal means
"seeking the centre".

Since $v = \omega r$, the same acceleration can be written with the angular
velocity:

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(\omega r)^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$$

Use whichever form matches the numbers you have.

**Example 1.** A ball of mass $0.2$ kg moves in a circle of radius $0.5$ m at a
steady $4$ m/s. Find its centripetal acceleration.

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{4^2}{0.5} = \frac{16}{0.5} = 32\ \text{m/s}^2$$

The acceleration points towards the centre of the circle.

**Example 2.** Check Example 1 with the angular velocity.

$$\omega = \frac{v}{r} = \frac{4}{0.5} = 8\ \text{rad/s}$$

$$a = \omega^2 r = 8^2 \times 0.5 = 64 \times 0.5 = 32\ \text{m/s}^2$$

The two forms agree, as they must, because $v = \omega r$.

## The centripetal force

Newton's second law says the resultant force is $F = ma$. The force that
produces the centripetal acceleration is the **centripetal force**:

$$F = ma = \frac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$$

The force points towards the centre, in the same direction as the acceleration.

A centripetal force is not a new kind of force. It is the resultant of the forces
already acting, and something must supply it:

- a tension in a string, for a ball whirled overhead;
- friction between the tyres and the road, for a car on a bend;
- the normal reaction from the track, for a train on a curved rail;
- gravity, for a satellite in orbit.

**Example 3.** Find the force on the ball in Example 1.

$$F = ma = 0.2 \times 32 = 6.4\ \text{N}$$

The force is the tension in the string, and it points towards the centre.

**Example 4.** The chapter-problem car of mass $1200$ kg goes round a roundabout
of radius $20$ m at $10$ m/s. Find its centripetal acceleration and the force
needed.

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{10^2}{20} = 5\ \text{m/s}^2$$

$$F = ma = 1200 \times 5 = 6000\ \text{N}$$

The friction between the tyres and the road supplies this $6000$ N towards the
centre of the roundabout.

**Example 5.** A point on the drum of a washing machine is $0.25$ m from the
axis. The drum spins at $100$ rad/s. Find the acceleration of the point and the
force on a $0.5$ kg item of clothing at the wall.

$$a = \omega^2 r = 100^2 \times 0.25 = 2500\ \text{m/s}^2$$

$$F = ma = 0.5 \times 2500 = 1250\ \text{N}$$

The drum wall pushes the clothing towards the centre. Without that push the
clothing would travel in a straight line, which is why the water escapes through
the holes.

> **Watch out.** There is no outward force in this model. The force on a body
> moving in a circle points towards the centre, and that is the resultant. A
> common error is to invent a "centrifugal force" pushing outwards and add it to
> the diagram, which leaves two forces in opposite directions and no reason for
> the body to turn. What you feel in a turning car is your own body tending to
> carry on in a straight line while the car turns. A second error is to use the
> diameter in place of the radius. The radius is the distance from the centre,
> half the diameter.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The car of mass $1200$ kg goes round the roundabout
of radius $20$ m at $10$ m/s. You found its angular velocity in Section 7.1.

Find the centripetal acceleration:

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{10^2}{20} = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\ \text{m/s}^2$$

Then find the force towards the centre:

$$F = ma = 1200 \times \underline{\hspace{2cm}} = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\ \text{N}$$

Keep the force. Section 7.3 asks whether the road can supply it, and Section 7.4
asks what happens when the bend is banked instead.

## You should now be able to

- explain why a body moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating;
- state that the centripetal acceleration points towards the centre;
- use $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ and $a = \omega^2 r$;
- use $F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$;
- name the force that supplies the centripetal force in a given situation;
- explain why there is no outward force on the body in this model.
