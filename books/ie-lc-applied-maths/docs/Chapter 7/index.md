# Chapter 7: Circular motion and oscillation

A car going round a roundabout is moving at a steady speed, yet its velocity
changes every moment, because its direction changes. This chapter explains what
produces that change, and how to calculate it. Then it turns to a different kind
of repeated motion: a mass on a spring, moving back and forth.

## The chapter problem

**The car on the roundabout and the banked bend.** A car of mass $1200$ kg
drives round a roundabout of radius $20$ m at a steady $10$ m/s. It then joins a
motorway bend of radius $150$ m, banked at $15^\circ$ to the horizontal. The car
rests on four springs, which together have stiffness $120000$ N/m. Take
$g = 9.8$ m/s².

You cannot answer the questions yet, and you are not expected to. Make a
prediction first.

- Does the car need a force pushing it outwards, or inwards, to go round the
  roundabout?
- On a wet day the road is more slippery. Will the car be able to take the
  roundabout faster, or more slowly?
- Which allows a higher speed at the same radius: a flat bend, or a banked one?

Write your predictions down and keep them. Then the questions:

- What is the car's angular velocity and period on the roundabout?
- What is the car's centripetal acceleration, and what force produces it?
- What is the greatest speed at which the car can take the roundabout if the
  coefficient of friction is $0.8$?
- What is the design speed of the banked bend, the speed at which no friction is
  needed?
- How far do the springs compress under the weight of the car?
- What is the period of the car's bounce on its springs?

By the end of the chapter you can find an angular velocity, use the centripetal
acceleration and force, analyse a bend where friction or banking supplies the
force, apply Hooke's law to a spring, and find the period of a simple harmonic
motion.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- define angular velocity and period, and use $\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T}$;
- convert between an angular velocity and a linear speed with $v = \omega r$;
- explain why motion in a circle at constant speed is still accelerating;
- use $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ and $F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$;
- find the friction force that holds a car on a flat bend, and the greatest
  speed before it slides;
- analyse a particle in a vertical circle, including the condition to stay in
  contact;
- resolve the forces on a conical pendulum and on a car on a banked track;
- find the design speed of a banked track;
- state Hooke's law and use $T = kx$ and $T = \dfrac{\lambda x}{L}$;
- explain why a mass on a spring moves with simple harmonic motion;
- use $a = -\omega^2 x$ and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$;
- find the maximum speed and the maximum acceleration of a simple harmonic
  motion.

## Where it is used

Roundabouts, banked racing tracks and motorway curves; a washing machine on its
spin cycle; a centrifuge in a laboratory; a satellite in orbit; a car crossing a
hump-back bridge; a bucket of water swung in a vertical circle. Springs and
oscillations appear in vehicle suspension, in a bathroom scale, in a clock, in a
seismograph, and in any structure that must absorb a shock and then settle.

## Your turn to connect it

Somewhere in your week, something moves in a circle. A bus turning a corner, a
washing machine on its spin cycle, a door swinging on its hinge, a bicycle
wheel, a ball on a string, a satellite passing overhead.

Choose one. Write down what is moving in the circle, what supplies the force
towards the centre, and what would happen if that force disappeared. You will be
