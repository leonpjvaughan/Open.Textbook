# Chapter 6: Momentum, impulse and collisions

When two objects collide, they push on each other and their motion changes.
This chapter puts numbers on that change. You will meet momentum, the quantity
that survives a collision, and impulse, the push that changes it. Then you will
model a crash test and ask which model of a collision fits the real thing.

## The chapter problem

**What the crash test measures.** A crash-test centre drives a test car of mass
$1200$ kg at $15$ m/s into a stationary car of mass $800$ kg. The centre runs
the same test three times, changing only the bumpers.

- In **test A** the bumpers crumple and the two cars lock together.
- In **test B** the bumpers spring back a little, and the coefficient of
  restitution is $0.5$.
- In **test C** the bumpers are perfectly springy, and the coefficient of
  restitution is $1$. This test is impossible in practice, but it shows the
  limit.

You cannot answer the questions yet, and you are not expected to. Make a
prediction first.

- After the impact, which car moves faster?
- Does the total energy of motion, the kinetic energy, go up, down, or stay
  the same?
- In which test do the cars separate fastest?

Write your predictions down and keep them. Then the questions:

- What is the momentum of the moving car before the impact?
- What is the impulse on each car?
- What is the velocity of each car after the impact in each of the three tests?
- How much kinetic energy is lost in each test?
- Which model fits a real crash better, and why?

By the end of the chapter you can find a momentum, work out an impulse, use
conservation of momentum, apply the coefficient of restitution, compare an
elastic model with an inelastic one, and say which model a real crash test
supports.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- define momentum and impulse, and use the units kg m/s and N s;
- calculate a momentum from a mass and a velocity;
- use $J = F\Delta t$ and $J = \Delta p$ to find an impulse or an average force;
- state and use the law of conservation of momentum;
- solve collisions in one dimension, including bodies that lock together;
- state Newton's experimental law of restitution and find the coefficient $e$;
- use restitution with conservation to find two unknown velocities;
- tell an elastic collision from an inelastic one, and find the kinetic energy
  lost;
- conserve momentum in two directions at once, resolving velocities into
  components;
- find the speeds and heights after a series of rebounds, and the total distance
  travelled.

## Where it is used

Crash testing, airbags and crumple zones; sport, from a cricket bat to a
boxing glove; ballistics and spacecraft docking; the recoil of a rifle; the
design of packaging that protects a dropped phone. Any impact you want to
survive, or to measure, is a momentum problem. Engineers choose materials and
shapes to make the impulse last longer, so the force is smaller.

## Your turn to connect it

Somewhere in your week, something collides. A football kicked, a phone dropped,
a shopping trolley bumped into a shelf, a cyclist braking, a door swinging into
its stop, a seat belt holding you back.

Choose one. Write down the two objects that collide, which one is moving, and
what you would want to measure: the force, the speed after the impact, or the
energy lost. Say whether the collision is elastic, inelastic, or somewhere in
between. You will be able to put numbers on it by the end of the chapter.
