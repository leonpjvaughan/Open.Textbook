# 6.3 Collisions in one dimension — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 6.2, conservation of momentum; Section 6.1, impulse;
Section 4.1, Newton's third law.

**Objectives:** set up a one-dimensional collision; find the impulse on each
body from its change in momentum; use the fact that the two impulses are equal
and opposite; recognise when a second condition is needed.

A collision is a short, violent event. Two bodies touch, push hard on each
other, and separate. Momentum is conserved. The impulse on each body is its
change in momentum, and Newton's third law ties the two impulses together.

## Setting up a collision

For a collision along a line, follow the same steps every time.

1. Draw the two bodies before and after.
2. Choose a positive direction and mark it on the diagram.
3. Write every velocity with its sign.
4. Write the total momentum before equal to the total momentum after.

The impulse on a body is its change in momentum:

$$J_1 = m_1v_1 - m_1u_1$$

Because the force on the first body and the force on the second are equal and
opposite, and act for the same time, the two impulses are equal and opposite:

$$J_1 = -J_2$$

## The impulse in a collision

**Example 1.** In the crash test, a car of mass $800$ kg is at rest and is
struck by another car. The two cars move off together at $9$ m/s. Find the
impulse on the $800$ kg car.

The impulse equals the change in momentum:

$$J = m(v - u) = 800 \times (9 - 0) = 7200\,\text{N s}$$

The impulse points in the direction the struck car moves.

**Example 2.** The car of mass $1200$ kg slows from $15$ m/s to $9$ m/s in the
same collision. Find the impulse on it, taking its original direction as
positive.

$$J = 1200 \times (9 - 15) = 1200 \times (-6) = -7200\,\text{N s}$$

The negative sign means the impulse acts against the original direction of
travel. The two impulses are $+7200$ N s and $-7200$ N s: equal and opposite, as
Newton's third law requires.

**Example 3.** A cricket ball of mass $0.15$ kg approaches a bat at $30$ m/s
and is hit back along the same line at $40$ m/s. Find the magnitude of the
impulse on the ball.

Take the direction back towards the bowler as positive. The velocity changes
from $-30$ m/s to $+40$ m/s, a change of $70$ m/s.

$$J = 0.15 \times 70 = 10.5\,\text{N s}$$

The bat both reverses the ball's momentum and adds to it.

**Example 4.** The bat and ball are in contact for $0.002$ s. Find the average
force on the ball.

$$F = \frac{J}{\Delta t} = \frac{10.5}{0.002} = 5250\,\text{N}$$

The contact is brief, so the average force is large.

## When one equation is not enough

A collision along a line has two final velocities to find. Conservation of
momentum gives one equation. If both final velocities are unknown, one equation
cannot find two unknowns, and a second condition is needed.

Sometimes a question supplies the second condition: one final velocity is given,
or the bodies lock together, so the two final velocities are equal. In the next
section, the coefficient of restitution supplies the second condition for a
general collision.

**Example 5.** A body of mass $2$ kg moving at $6$ m/s to the right meets a
body of mass $3$ kg moving at $4$ m/s to the left. After the collision the
$2$ kg body moves at $3$ m/s to the left. Find the velocity of the $3$ kg body.

Take the right as positive. The momentum before is

$$2 \times 6 + 3 \times (-4) = 12 - 12 = 0$$

After the collision the $2$ kg body has momentum $2 \times (-3) = -6$ kg m/s, so
the $3$ kg body must carry $+6$ kg m/s:

$$3v = 6 \qquad v = 2\,\text{m/s to the right}$$

> **Watch out.** A collision is short, so the forces between the bodies are far
> larger than any friction from the ground. That is why momentum is treated as
> conserved even when the ground is rough. Two errors are common. The first is
> to give a reversed velocity the wrong sign, which makes the change in momentum
> too small. The second is to try to solve for two unknown final velocities with
> only the momentum equation. Count the unknowns before you start.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. In test A the cars lock together and move at
$9$ m/s.

Find the impulse on the $800$ kg car, which starts at rest:

$$J = 800 \times (9 - 0) = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\,\text{N s}$$

Now find the impulse on the $1200$ kg car, which slows from $15$ m/s to $9$ m/s:

$$J = 1200 \times (9 - 15) = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\,\text{N s}$$

The two answers should be equal and opposite. Section 6.4 uses the same collision
with a springier bumper, where the cars do not stay together.

## You should now be able to

- set up a one-dimensional collision with a positive direction and signs;
- find the impulse on a body from its change in momentum;
- explain why the impulses on the two bodies are equal and opposite;
- find an average force from an impulse and a contact time;
- recognise when a second condition is needed to find both final velocities.
