# 6.1 Momentum and impulse — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 3.1, speed and velocity; Section 4.1, Newton's laws;
Section 4.3, using $F = ma$.

**Objectives:** define momentum and impulse; calculate a momentum; use
$J = F\Delta t$ and $J = \Delta p$; use Newton's second law in momentum form.

A moving object carries something that makes it hard to stop. A loaded lorry at
$5$ m/s is harder to stop than a bicycle at $5$ m/s, and a bicycle at $20$ m/s
is harder to stop than one at $5$ m/s. The quantity that measures this is
momentum, and the push that changes it is impulse.

## What momentum is

The **momentum** of an object is its mass times its velocity:

$$p = mv$$

Momentum is a vector, so it points in the direction of the velocity. Its unit is
the kilogram metre per second, kg m/s.

**Example 1.** A test car of mass $1200$ kg moves at $15$ m/s. Find its
momentum.

$$p = mv = 1200 \times 15 = 18000\,\text{kg m/s}$$

The momentum points along the road, in the direction the car is moving.

**Example 2.** A ball of mass $0.25$ kg moves at $8$ m/s. Find its momentum.

$$p = 0.25 \times 8 = 2\,\text{kg m/s}$$

A small mass moving quickly can carry a momentum as large as a much bigger mass
moving slowly. A bullet has a small momentum compared with a lorry, and that is
why a lorry is harder to stop.

## Impulse

A force acting for a time changes an object's momentum. The product of the force
and the time is called the **impulse**:

$$J = F\Delta t$$

The unit of impulse is the newton second, N s. Since a force is a rate of change
of momentum, $1$ N s is the same as $1$ kg m/s.

The impulse equals the change in momentum. This is the **impulse–momentum
theorem**:

$$J = \Delta p = mv - mu$$

where $u$ is the velocity before the impulse and $v$ is the velocity after it.

**Example 3.** A force of $60$ N acts on a trolley for $0.5$ s. Find the
impulse.

$$J = F\Delta t = 60 \times 0.5 = 30\,\text{N s}$$

**Example 4.** A ball of mass $3$ kg moves at $12$ m/s. A keeper catches it and
brings it to rest. Find the impulse on the ball.

The momentum starts at $3 \times 12 = 36$ kg m/s and ends at zero. So

$$J = 36 - 0 = 36\,\text{N s}$$

The impulse acts against the motion, to slow the ball.

**Example 5.** The ball in Example 4 is caught in $0.08$ s. Find the average
force on it.

$$F = \frac{J}{\Delta t} = \frac{36}{0.08} = 450\,\text{N}$$

The same impulse spread over a longer time needs a smaller force. That is the
idea behind a cushion, a glove and a crumple zone.

## Newton's second law in momentum form

Newton's second law can be written with momentum instead of acceleration. The
resultant force is the rate of change of momentum:

$$F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}$$

This form is useful when the momentum changes quickly and the force is large.

**Example 6.** A car of mass $1500$ kg speeds up from $10$ m/s to $25$ m/s in
$5$ s. Find the average resultant force.

The change in momentum is

$$\Delta p = 1500 \times (25 - 10) = 1500 \times 15 = 22500\,\text{kg m/s}$$

Divide by the time:

$$F = \frac{22500}{5} = 4500\,\text{N}$$

The force acts in the direction of travel.

## A rebound changes the sign

When an object bounces back, its velocity reverses, so the change in momentum is
larger than a simple stop.

**Example 7.** A ball of mass $0.5$ kg hits a wall at $6$ m/s and rebounds at
$4$ m/s. Find the impulse on the ball.

Take the direction away from the wall as positive. The velocity before is
$-6$ m/s and after is $+4$ m/s. So

$$J = m(v - u) = 0.5 \times (4 - (-6)) = 0.5 \times 10 = 5\,\text{N s}$$

The impulse is $5$ N s away from the wall. Stopping the ball would have needed
only $3$ N s. A rebound always needs a larger impulse, because the momentum
reverses instead of reaching zero.

> **Watch out.** Momentum and impulse are vectors, so choose a positive
> direction and keep the signs. Two errors are common. The first is to forget
> that a rebound makes the change in velocity larger, because the two speeds
> point in opposite directions. The second is to confuse impulse with force.
> Impulse is force times time, so the same force acting for twice as long
> delivers twice the impulse.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The test car has mass $1200$ kg and travels at
$15$ m/s before the impact.

Find its momentum:

$$p = mv = 1200 \times 15 = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\,\text{kg m/s}$$

Keep that number. Section 6.2 uses it to find the common velocity in test A, and
Section 6.3 uses it to find the impulse on each car.

## You should now be able to

- define momentum as $p = mv$ and give its unit;
- calculate a momentum from a mass and a velocity;
- define impulse as $J = F\Delta t$ and give its unit;
- use the impulse–momentum theorem, $J = \Delta p$;
- find an average force from an impulse and a time;
- use Newton's second law in the form $F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}$;
- handle a rebound by giving the reversed velocity the correct sign.
