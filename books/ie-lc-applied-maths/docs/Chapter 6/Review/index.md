# Chapter 6: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**What the crash test measures.** A test car of mass $1200$ kg travels at
$15$ m/s and hits a stationary car of mass $800$ kg. The centre runs the test
three times with different bumpers. Take the original direction of travel as
positive.

**The momentum before.** The moving car carries momentum $p$:

$$p = mv = 1200 \times 15 = 18000\,\text{kg m/s}$$

The kinetic energy before, $E_k$, is

$$E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 15^2 = 135000\,\text{J}$$

These two numbers are the same in all three tests.

**Test A, $e = 0$.** The cars lock together. Conservation of momentum gives

$$2000v = 18000 \qquad v = 9\,\text{m/s}$$

The impulse on the $800$ kg car is $800 \times (9 - 0) = 7200$ N s. The impulse
on the $1200$ kg car is $1200 \times (9 - 15) = -7200$ N s, equal and opposite.
The kinetic energy after is

$$E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times 2000 \times 9^2 = 81000\,\text{J}$$

so $54000$ J is lost.

**Test B, $e = 0.5$.** Momentum and restitution give

$$1200v_1 + 800v_2 = 18000, \qquad v_2 - v_1 = 7.5$$

Solving, $v_1 = 6$ m/s and $v_2 = 13.5$ m/s. The impulse on the $800$ kg car is
$800 \times 13.5 = 10800$ N s. The kinetic energy after is

$$E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 6^2 + \frac{1}{2} \times 800 \times 13.5^2
= 21600 + 72900 = 94500\,\text{J}$$

so $40500$ J is lost.

**Test C, $e = 1$.** The elastic formulas give $v_1 = 3$ m/s and $v_2 = 18$ m/s.
The impulse on the $800$ kg car is $800 \times 18 = 14400$ N s. The kinetic
energy after is

$$E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 3^2 + \frac{1}{2} \times 800 \times 18^2
= 5400 + 129600 = 135000\,\text{J}$$

No energy is lost, because the collision is perfectly elastic.

**Which model fits a real crash?** In a real crash the metal crumples, and the
coefficient of restitution is small, often between $0$ and $0.3$. The inelastic
model, close to test A, fits far better than the elastic one. The elastic model
overestimates the rebound speed of the struck car and the impulse on it, and it
predicts that no kinetic energy is lost. A real crash always loses energy, into
crumpled metal, sound and heat. Test B lies between the two and is a reasonable
description of a bumper that springs back a little.

**Your predictions.** The struck car moves faster than the moving car in tests B
and C, because it has less mass. The total kinetic energy falls in tests A and
B, and stays the same only in the ideal test C. The cars separate fastest in
test C, where the coefficient of restitution is largest.

**A last change.** Suppose test A took $0.15$ s. The average force on the
$800$ kg car is

$$F = \frac{J}{\Delta t} = \frac{7200}{0.15} = 48000\,\text{N}$$

That is a large force, and it is why a crash-test dummy is built to record it.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which kind of problem
it is** and say why, before you calculate anything. Some of them need a method
from an earlier chapter, and two of them are about choosing the right idea
rather than the right number.

1. A body of mass $5$ kg moves at $4$ m/s. Find its momentum.
2. A force of $25$ N acts for $0.4$ s. Find the impulse.
3. A trolley of mass $2$ kg moves at $4$ m/s to the right and meets a trolley
   of mass $1$ kg moving at $2$ m/s to the left. They lock together. Find their
   common velocity.
4. A gun of mass $3$ kg fires a bullet of mass $0.02$ kg at $300$ m/s. Find the
   recoil speed of the gun.
5. A ball of mass $0.2$ kg hits a wall at $10$ m/s and rebounds at $6$ m/s.
   Find the magnitude of the impulse on it.
6. A ball is dropped from $1.8$ m and rebounds to $1.152$ m. Find the
   coefficient of restitution.
7. A ball of mass $2$ kg moving at $8$ m/s hits a stationary ball of mass $2$
   kg. The coefficient of restitution is $0.5$. Find the velocity of each ball.
8. A ball of mass $2$ kg moving at $6$ m/s hits a stationary ball of mass $2$
   kg and they lock together. Find the kinetic energy lost.
9. A ball of mass $1$ kg moving at $4$ m/s collides elastically with a
   stationary ball of mass $3$ kg. Find the velocity of each ball, and check the
   kinetic energy.
10. A ball of mass $2$ kg moves at $6$ m/s along the $x$-axis and hits a
    stationary ball of mass $1$ kg. After the collision the $2$ kg ball moves at
    $3$ m/s at $60^\circ$ to the $x$-axis. Find the speed and direction of the
    $1$ kg ball. Which two sections does this question join together?
11. A ball is dropped from a height of $4$ m with coefficient of restitution
    $0.5$. Find the height after the second bounce, and the total distance it
    travels.
12. A ball is thrown vertically upwards at $14$ m/s. Find the greatest height
    it reaches. Which chapter does this question belong to, and why?

## What you can now do

- define momentum and impulse, and use their units;
- calculate a momentum, and use $J = F\Delta t$ and $J = \Delta p$;
- state and use the law of conservation of momentum;
- solve a collision in one dimension, including bodies that lock together;
- find the impulse on each body in a collision, and check that the two are equal
  and opposite;
- state Newton's experimental law of restitution and find the coefficient $e$;
- use restitution with conservation to find two unknown velocities;
- tell an elastic collision from an inelastic one and find the kinetic energy
  lost;
- conserve momentum in two directions at once, resolving velocities into
  components;
- find the speed and height after any number of rebounds, and the total distance
  travelled.

## Thinking back

Look again at the collision you chose at the start of the chapter: the football,
the phone, the trolley, the cyclist, the door, or the seat belt.

Name the two objects that collide and which one is moving. Decide whether the
collision is elastic, inelastic, or somewhere in between, and say what
coefficient of restitution you would estimate. Then write down what the model
leaves out: the ground may be rough, the collision may not be along a line, and
the coefficient may not be the same for every impact.

If you cannot put a number on one of the quantities, say so. Deciding what you
can measure and what you cannot is part of modelling, and it is exactly what the
modelling project asks you to do.
