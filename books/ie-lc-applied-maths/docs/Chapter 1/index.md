# Chapter 1: What mathematical modelling is

A model is a simplified picture of a real situation, written in mathematics. It
leaves things out on purpose, so that the question becomes small enough to
answer. This chapter shows how that is done, and how you decide whether the
answer is worth trusting.

## The chapter problem

**How long is the queue?** At 12:30, forty students are queuing at the school
canteen. More students join the back of the queue at a steady rate of five each
minute. One till serves students at a steady rate of ten each minute.

Before you calculate anything, make two predictions and write them down.

- Will the queue get longer or shorter during lunch?
- Will the queue ever be gone?

Now the questions you will be able to answer by the end of the chapter.

- How long does a student who joins at 12:30 wait before being served?
- How long until the queue is gone?
- How long does a student who joins at 12:35 wait?
- What happens if the till serves only five students each minute?
- Which assumption does your answer depend on most?

The predictions are not marked. They are there so that you can compare them with
the model at the end. Many people find that one prediction was right and the
other was not, and the difference between them is the interesting part.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- describe the modelling cycle — formulate, translate, compute, evaluate,
  refine — and say what each stage does;
- turn a vague question into a precise one, and break it into smaller questions;
- state the assumptions a model rests on, and explain what each one leaves out;
- translate a situation into symbols, and check the units of the result;
- compute a solution, round it sensibly, and say what the number means;
- test a model against data, refine one assumption at a time, and report
  honestly;
- write a modelling report that another person can follow and check.

## Where it is used

Every model in this book uses this cycle, from networks to projectiles to
population growth. The modelling project, worth 20% of your mark, is marked on
the same cycle: a clear question, stated assumptions, a worked model, and an
honest evaluation. Outside school, the cycle is how a delivery company plans
routes, how a hospital plans a ward, and how an engineer tests a design.

## Your turn to connect it

Somewhere in your week, a queue or a delay affects you. The canteen at lunch, a
bus that does not come, a checkout at a shop, the wait to charge a phone, a
match that runs over time.

Choose one. Write down what is arriving or building up, what is being cleared
or used, and how fast each of those happens. Then write the question you would
like to be able to answer about it. You will be able to model it by the end of
the chapter, and you will return to it in the Review.
