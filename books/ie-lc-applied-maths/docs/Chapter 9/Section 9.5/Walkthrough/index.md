# 9.5 Communicating the result — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Sections 9.1 to 9.4, a tested and improved model.

**Objectives:** write the six parts of a report; round a number to the precision
the data supports; present a range when the data is uncertain; write a
conclusion that names the answer, its main assumption and its limits.

The project is marked on the report. A correct model with a poor report cannot
earn the marks, because the reader cannot see where the answer came from. The
report is part of the applied mathematics, not a wrapper around it.

## Write for a reader who was not there

Your reader knows the mathematics but not your situation. They did not watch you
collect the data, and they cannot ask you a question. Every choice you made has
to be on the page.

That means three habits.

- **Name the situation** in the first sentence, so the reader can picture it.
- **Define every letter**, with its unit, before you use it.
- **Say what each assumption leaves out**, so the reader can judge whether the
  answer applies to their own situation.

## The six parts

The report has six parts, and they follow the modelling cycle.

1. **The question.** One sentence, with a unit and a moment or place.
2. **The assumptions.** A list, each one ending with what it leaves out.
3. **The model.** Every letter with its unit, and the relationships between them.
4. **The solution.** The substitution, the arithmetic and the units.
5. **The evaluation.** The data, the residuals, and what the refinement changed.
6. **The conclusion.** The answer, the assumption it depends on most, and one
   limit.

## Rounding and precision

Round once, at the end, to the precision the data supports. A measurement taken
to the nearest degree cannot support an answer to five decimal places.

If the measurements vary by 2 °C when you repeat them, then reporting a
prediction of 63.333 °C claims a precision the data does not have. Report
63.3 °C, or report the range the data supports.

$$\text{a range beats a false precision}$$

A range is honest and useful. "The clear time is between 7 and 9 minutes" tells
the reader something true. "The clear time is 7.916666 minutes" tells them
something false about how well you measured.

## Tables and graphs

A table carries the numbers, and a graph shows the pattern. Both need their
units.

- In a table, put the unit in the column header, so every entry in that column
  is in the same unit.
- In a graph, label each axis with its quantity and its unit.
- Draw the measurements as points and the model as a line, so the reader can see
  where they agree and where they part.
- Keep the same number of decimal places down a column.

## The conclusion

The conclusion is the last paragraph, and it has three jobs.

1. **Answer the question**, with its unit.
2. **Name the assumption the answer depends on most.**
3. **State one limit** of the model.

A conclusion that does all three is honest. A conclusion that gives only the
answer sounds more certain than the model can support.

> **Watch out.** The most common report error is to give the answer without the
> assumptions. A reader who cannot see the assumptions cannot tell whether the
> answer applies to their own situation.
>
> The second is false precision. A number with six decimal places does not make a
> model more accurate; it makes the report harder to trust, because the precision
> is not real.
>
> The third is overclaiming. "The queue clears in 8 minutes" is a claim about one
> day. "The queue clears in about 8 minutes on a normal lunchtime, and a heavier
> rush would make it longer" is honest about the limits.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem, **your own model**. Write the six parts of your
report now.

1. **The question**, in one sentence with a unit.
2. **The assumptions**, each one ending with what it leaves out.
3. **The model**, with every letter and its unit.
4. **The solution**, with the substitution and the units.
5. **The evaluation**, with the data and what the refinement changed.
6. **The conclusion**, with the answer, the main assumption and one limit.

Then read it back and check two things: is every number rounded once, and is the
conclusion no more certain than the model allows?

## You should now be able to

- write the six parts of a report, in order;
- round once, to the precision the data supports;
- report a range when the measurements are uncertain;
- put units in the column header of a table and on the axes of a graph;
- write a conclusion that gives the answer, its main assumption and a limit;
- explain why false precision makes a report harder to trust.
