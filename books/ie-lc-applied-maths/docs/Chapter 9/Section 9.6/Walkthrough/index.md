# 9.6 Critical evaluation of models — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Sections 9.1 to 9.5, a tested and reported model.

**Objectives:** state where a model fails; recognise extrapolation and a broken
assumption; explain why a model's answer is not the decision; evaluate another
person's model.

A model is a simplified picture, and every simplification has a price. The last
skill of the project is to say what the model can and cannot do, without
apology.

## The model is not the situation

A map is not the ground. A model of a queue is not the queue, and a model of a
battery is not the battery. The model leaves things out on purpose, and it is
useful because it leaves them out.

The danger is forgetting which one you are holding. A number from a model is an
answer to a question about a simplified world, and it is only as good as the
assumptions that built it.

## Where a model fails

A model fails in three ways, and a good report names them.

- **Outside its range.** A model tested between 0 and 10 minutes may be wrong at
  60 minutes. Using it there is called **extrapolation**, and it is a guess, not
  a prediction.
- **When an assumption breaks.** A model of a battery that assumes a steady
  drain fails when a heavy app is opened. The model is not wrong; the assumption
  no longer holds.
- **When the situation changes.** A model of last year's traffic may not fit
  this year's roadworks. The world moved, and the model did not.

## The answer is not the decision

A model gives a number. A person decides what to do with it. The two are not the
same, and the report should not pretend they are.

Suppose a model says a new till would cut the queue from 8 minutes to 4. The
model has done its job. Whether to buy the till also depends on its cost, the
space it needs and what else the money could do. Those are values and judgement,
not mathematics.

A model can inform a decision. It cannot make one.

## Checking someone else's model

You will read other people's models: in a newspaper, in a report, or in another
student's project. Five questions will find most of the weaknesses.

1. **What question does it answer?** A model with no clear question cannot be
   checked.
2. **What does it assume?** An unstated assumption is the most common fault.
3. **Where did the data come from?** A number with no source cannot be trusted.
4. **Does the arithmetic check?** Put the units in and see whether they balance.
5. **Does the conclusion claim more than the model allows?** A single number for
   an uncertain situation is a warning sign.

> **Watch out.** Two errors are common when a model is evaluated.
>
> The first is trusting a model because it is complicated, or because a computer
> produced it. A complicated model has more assumptions, not fewer, and a
> computer will compute whatever it is told. A wrong assumption at the start
> makes every later number wrong.
>
> The second is using a model outside the range where it was tested. A model that
> fits the data from 0 to 10 minutes says nothing about 60 minutes. Saying so is
> part of the mathematics, not a weakness in it.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem, **your own model**. Evaluate it as if it were
someone else's.

1. State one thing your model cannot tell you.
2. Name one assumption that would break it, and say what would happen.
3. Say whether any part of your answer is an extrapolation.
4. Write one sentence that explains why your model informs a decision without
   making it.

Then read your report from Section 9.5 again and add anything new you noticed.
This is the last step of the project, and it is the one that earns the
evaluation marks.

## You should now be able to

- explain why a model is not the situation it describes;
- name the three ways a model fails;
- recognise extrapolation and say why it is a guess;
- explain why a model's answer is not the decision;
- check someone else's model with five questions;
- explain why a complicated model is not automatically a better one.
