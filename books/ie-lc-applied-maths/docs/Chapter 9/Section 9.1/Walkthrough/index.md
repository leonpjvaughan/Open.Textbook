# 9.1 Reading the brief and choosing a problem — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 1, the modelling cycle and the six parts of a report.

**Objectives:** read the SEC brief for its context and its task words; turn a
broad context into a precise question with a unit; test a candidate problem for
measurability, available data and size.

The first job of the project is not mathematics. It is choosing the right
question. A good question is small enough to answer and real enough to measure,
and finding it is most of the work.

## The brief gives a context, not a question

Each year the State Examinations Commission issues one brief for the modelling
project. The brief is common to Ordinary and Higher level, and it is marked with
different expectations at each level. It sets out a **context**, such as travel,
energy, sport or the environment. It does not hand you a question.

Read the brief three times, each time looking for something different.

- **First reading:** what is the context about? Name the situation in one
  sentence.
- **Second reading:** what are the task words? Words such as *investigate*,
  *compare*, *model* and *recommend* tell you what kind of answer is wanted.
- **Third reading:** what data could you collect, and what data is out of reach?

The brief is a starting point, and your project is what you build inside it. Two
students can read the same brief and produce two completely different projects.

## A question needs a number

A model answers a question with a number, and a number needs a unit. Compare
these two questions.

- "Is the school gate busy in the morning?"
- "How many minutes does it take for the queue of cars at the school gate to
  clear after 08:30?"

The first question cannot be marked, because nobody can say what *busy* means.
The second question names a quantity, the clear time in minutes, and a moment,
08:30. You can collect data for it, and a reader can check your answer.

A precise question has three parts.

1. **A quantity**, such as a time, a distance, a cost or a count.
2. **A unit**, such as minutes, metres, euro or students.
3. **A moment or a place**, so the answer applies to something specific.

## The three tests

Before you commit to a problem, put it through three tests.

- **Measurable.** The answer is a number with a unit, and you can say what would
  count as a good answer.
- **Data available.** You can collect the numbers, or find them in a published
  source, within the time you have.
- **Manageable.** You can finish the model with the mathematics you know, and
  the arithmetic is not so heavy that the project becomes a data entry task.

A problem that fails any one of the three tests will cost you marks, and it is
much easier to change the problem now than after three weeks of work.

## Narrowing a broad context

Most first ideas are far too big. Narrowing is the skill, and it works by adding
detail until the question can be measured.

| Too broad | Narrowed |
|---|---|
| Traffic in the town | The clear time of the school-gate queue after 08:30 |
| The cost of energy | The cost of boiling a full kettle, in cent |
| Sport and scoring | The number of shots a player needs to score, on average |
| Water use at home | The litres a shower uses each minute |

Each narrowed question names a quantity, a unit and a place. Each one can be
answered with a handful of measurements, and each one can be checked by a
reader.

> **Watch out.** Three traps catch most projects at this stage.
>
> The first is a question with no unit. "How good is the bus service?" cannot be
> checked, because two readers would disagree about what *good* means.
>
> The second is a question with no data. If the numbers you need are private, or
> cost money, or take a year to collect, the project cannot be tested.
>
> The third is a problem that is too big. "Model the whole town's traffic" is a
> research programme, not a project. Narrow it until one person can collect the
> data in a few hours.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem, **your own model**. Write your question in one
sentence. It must name a quantity, a unit and a moment or a place.

Then test it against the three tests.

1. **Measurable.** What number will your answer be, and in what unit?
2. **Data available.** Where will the numbers come from, and how long will they
   take to collect?
3. **Manageable.** Which parts of the mathematics from this book will you use?

If your question fails a test, rewrite it now. Keep the rewritten question with
your three notes. Section 9.2 builds the model from it.

## You should now be able to

- describe what the SEC brief provides and what it leaves to you;
- read a brief for its context, its task words and the data it allows;
- write a question that names a quantity, a unit and a moment or a place;
- test a candidate problem for measurability, available data and size;
- narrow a broad context into a question one person can answer.
