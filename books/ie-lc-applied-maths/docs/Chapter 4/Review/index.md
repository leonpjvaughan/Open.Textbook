# Chapter 4: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The crate, the ramp and the pulley.** A crate of mass $20$ kg rests on a
smooth ramp inclined at $30^\circ$. A light rope runs from the crate, up the
ramp and over a smooth pulley at the top, and carries a hanging mass of $15$ kg.
The system is released from rest. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

**Which way does it move?** Two forces compete along the rope.

- The hanging weight is $15 \times 9.8 = 147$ N, pulling the crate up the ramp.
- The component of the crate's weight down the ramp is
  $20 \times 9.8 \times \sin 30^\circ = 196 \times 0.5 = 98$ N.

The hanging weight is larger, so the crate moves up the ramp and the hanging
mass falls. If you predicted that at the start, your reasoning was right.

**The acceleration.** The resultant force is $147 - 98 = 49$ N. Both bodies
move, so the total mass is $20 + 15 = 35$ kg.

$$a = \frac{49}{35} = 1.4\,\text{m/s}^2$$

**The tension.** Use the hanging mass, which accelerates downwards:

$$m_2 g - T = m_2 a$$

$$147 - T = 15 \times 1.4 = 21$$

$$T = 126\,\text{N}$$

Check with the crate, which accelerates up the ramp:

$$T - 98 = 20 \times 1.4 = 28$$

$$T = 126\,\text{N}$$

Both checks agree, so the tension is 126 N.

**The whole picture.** One problem needed four ideas from four sections:
Newton's second law for each body, the component of the weight on a slope, the
tension in a light string, and the fact that both bodies share one acceleration.
The method is always the same: draw each body, choose a positive direction,
write $F = ma$ for each, add the equations, and substitute back.

**A last change.** If the ramp were smooth but horizontal, the component down it
would be zero, and the acceleration would be $\dfrac{147}{35} = 4.2$ m/s². A
steeper ramp would reduce the acceleration, and at $30^\circ$ with a hanging mass
of 10 kg the system would balance.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which kind of problem it
is** and say why, before you calculate anything. Some of them need a method from
an earlier chapter, and two of them are deliberately about choosing the right
idea rather than the right number.

1. A box of mass $8$ kg rests on a smooth horizontal floor. A force of $30$ N
   pulls it horizontally. Find its acceleration and the normal reaction.
2. Two forces of $9$ N and $40$ N act at right angles. Find the resultant, and
   the angle it makes with the 40 N force.
3. A block of mass $6$ kg slides down a smooth plane inclined at $30^\circ$.
   Find its acceleration and the normal reaction.
4. Masses of $4$ kg and $7$ kg hang from a light string over a smooth pulley.
   Find the acceleration and the tension.
5. A mass of $10$ kg rests on a smooth table. A string from it passes over a
   smooth pulley and carries a hanging mass of $5$ kg. Find the acceleration and
   the tension.
6. A crate of mass $12$ kg rests on a smooth plane inclined at $30^\circ$. A
   string from it runs up the plane, over a smooth pulley, and carries a hanging
   mass of $8$ kg. State which way the system moves, then find the acceleration.
7. A car of mass $1500$ kg tows a trailer of mass $500$ kg on a level road with
   a driving force of $4000$ N. Find the acceleration and the tension in the tow
   bar.
8. A force of $(4\mathbf{i} + 7\mathbf{j})$ N acts on a particle of mass $2$ kg.
   Find the acceleration as a vector, and its magnitude.
9. A particle is acted on by $(6\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j})$ N and
   $(-2\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j})$ N. Find the third force that keeps it in
   equilibrium.
10. A ball is thrown vertically upwards at $14$ m/s. Using $a = -g$ and the
    equations of motion from Chapter 3, find the greatest height it reaches.
    Then say which chapter's method you used, and why this question is not a
    forces question.
11. A block of mass $5$ kg slides down a smooth plane and reaches a speed of
    $7$ m/s after $2$ s. Find its acceleration, and hence the angle of the
    plane. Which two chapters does this question join together?
12. A student says: "The tension in a string is always equal to the weight
    hanging on it." Give one situation in this chapter where that is true, and
    one where it is false, with a reason for each.

## What you can now do

- describe a force, tell mass from weight, and state Newton's three laws;
- find a resultant along a line and at right angles, and find its direction;
- resolve a force, and use the equilibrium condition;
- find the normal reaction and the acceleration on a smooth horizontal plane;
- resolve the weight on an inclined plane and find the acceleration down it;
- write $F = ma$ for connected particles, find the tension, and check it;
- solve pulley problems with a mass on a table or a slope joined to a hanging
  mass;
- write forces in vector form and use $F = ma$ component by component.

## Thinking back

Look again at the object you chose at the start of the chapter: the door, the
shopping bag, the wheelbarrow, the bicycle on the hill, or the person on the
ladder.

Draw its free-body diagram. Name each force, give its direction, and mark which
of them you can estimate. Then decide which question you are asking. Is the
object in equilibrium, so that the forces balance? Or is it accelerating, so that
the resultant force is not zero?

Write down your answer and your reasoning. If you cannot put a number on one of
the forces, say so. Deciding what you can and cannot measure is part of modelling,
and it is exactly what the modelling project asks you to do.
