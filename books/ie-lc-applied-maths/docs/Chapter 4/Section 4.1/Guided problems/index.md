# 4.1 Forces and Newton's laws — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Work out $6 \times 9.8$.
2. A car speeds up from $0$ m/s to $12$ m/s in $4$ s. Find its acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $58.8$. This is the kind of multiplication you use to turn a mass in kg into
   a weight in N.
2. $a = \dfrac{v - u}{t} = \dfrac{12 - 0}{4} = 3$ m/s². In this chapter the
   acceleration usually comes from a force instead of a stopwatch, but the
   meaning is the same.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Find the weight of a box of mass $12$ kg. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $W = mg$. The mass is in kg and the weight comes out in N.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$W = 12 \times 9.8$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mg = 12 \times 9.8 = 117.6\,\text{N}$$

The box has a mass of 12 kg and a weight of 117.6 N. The weight is a force, so
it is measured in newtons.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A sack weighs $98$ N. Find its mass. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start from $W = mg$ and divide both sides by $g$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$m = \frac{W}{g} = \frac{98}{9.8}$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$m = \frac{W}{g} = \frac{98}{9.8} = 10\,\text{kg}$$

The sack has a mass of 10 kg. A weight of 98 N is the weight of a 10 kg mass
near the surface of the Earth.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A resultant force of $45$ N acts on a mass of $9$ kg. Find the
acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $F = ma$ and make $a$ the subject.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$45 = 9a$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{45}{9} = 5\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The acceleration is 5 m/s² in the direction of the resultant force.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A trolley of mass $0.4$ kg accelerates at $5$ m/s². Find the resultant
force on it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The second law gives the force directly when you know the mass and the
acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$F = 0.4 \times 5$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = ma = 0.4 \times 5 = 2\,\text{N}$$

A small mass and a modest acceleration need only a small force.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A box is pushed with $20$ N to the right and $8$ N to the left. Write
down the resultant force, and the direction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The forces are along the same line but in opposite directions, so subtract the
smaller from the larger.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$20 - 8$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$20 - 8 = 12\,\text{N} \text{ to the right}$$

The resultant points to the right, because the larger force points that way.
Only the resultant goes into $F = ma$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A student writes: "A 2 kg book weighs 2 N, because the mass and the
weight are the same." Explain what is wrong, and give the correct weight. Take
$g = 9.8$ m/s².

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Mass is measured in kg and weight is a force, measured in N.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Use $W = mg$ with $m = 2$ kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Mass and weight are different quantities. The mass is 2 kg, and it stays 2 kg
wherever the book is. The weight is the force of gravity on the book, and it is
found from $W = mg$.

$$W = 2 \times 9.8 = 19.6\,\text{N}$$

The weight is 19.6 N, not 2 N. The number 2 belongs to the mass, and the unit
tells you which quantity it is.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** A swimmer pushes the water backwards with her hands. State the pair of
forces in Newton's third law, and say which one moves the swimmer forwards.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The two forces act on two different bodies.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The swimmer pushes the water; the water pushes the swimmer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The swimmer pushes the water backwards. By the third law, the water pushes the
swimmer forwards with a force of the same size and the opposite direction.

The force that moves the swimmer is the push of the water on the swimmer. The
other force of the pair acts on the water. The two forces are equal and
opposite, but they act on different bodies, so they do not cancel.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 1

**8.** In Chapter 1 a model was judged by what it leaves out. This section uses
one model of gravity: every object near the Earth has $g = 9.8$ m/s², whatever
its mass. State one thing this model leaves out, and one question it can still
answer well.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about where the object is, and how high it is.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The value of $g$ is not exactly the same everywhere.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The model leaves out the way $g$ changes with height and with position on the
Earth. The value 9.8 m/s² is an average near the surface, and it is slightly
smaller on a mountain and slightly larger near the poles. It also leaves out air
resistance, which is a separate force on a falling object.

The model still answers a useful question: the weight of an object of known
mass, to two significant figures, anywhere near the ground. For a school
calculation that is close enough, and the small variation of $g$ would not
change the answer at the level of accuracy the data support.

</details>
</details>
</details>
