# 4.1 Forces and Newton's laws — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a push or a pull, and multiplying and dividing
whole numbers and decimals.

**Objectives:** describe a force and its unit; tell mass from weight; find a
weight from a mass; state Newton's three laws; use $F = ma$; draw a free-body
diagram.

Every later section of this chapter uses the three laws and the word **force**.
This section sets both up, and shows the two mistakes that cause most of the
errors in the rest of the chapter.

## What a force is

A **force** is a push or a pull on an object. Force is a vector, so it has a
size and a direction. The SI unit of force is the **newton**, written N. One
newton is the force that gives a mass of one kilogram an acceleration of one
metre per second squared.

Forces come in a few common kinds.

- **Weight** is the pull of gravity on an object. It acts downwards, towards the
  centre of the Earth.
- The **normal reaction** is the push of a surface on an object that rests on
  it. It acts at right angles to the surface.
- **Tension** is the pull in a string, rope or cable.
- **Friction** is a force along a surface that opposes sliding. A surface is
  called **smooth** when friction is small enough to ignore.
- **Thrust** is a push from an engine or a motor.

## Mass and weight

Mass and weight are different quantities, and the difference matters.

- The **mass** of an object is the amount of matter in it. It is measured in
  kilograms, kg, and it does not change from place to place.
- The **weight** of an object is the force of gravity on it. It is measured in
  newtons, N.

The link between them is

$$W = mg$$

where $m$ is the mass in kg, $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity, and $W$ is
the weight in N. Near the surface of the Earth, $g = 9.8$ m/s².

**Example 1.** Find the weight of a bag of mass $5$ kg.

Write down the formula and substitute:

$$W = mg$$

$$W = 5 \times 9.8$$

$$W = 49\,\text{N}$$

The bag has a mass of 5 kg and a weight of 49 N. The mass stays 5 kg on the
Moon, but the weight there is smaller, because $g$ is smaller.

## Newton's first law

> **Newton's first law.** An object stays at rest, or keeps a constant velocity,
> unless a resultant force acts on it.

A **resultant force** is the single force that has the same effect as all the
forces on the object added together. The first law says that if the resultant is
zero, the velocity does not change. The object may be still, and it may be
moving in a straight line at a steady speed. Both are allowed.

## Newton's second law

> **Newton's second law.** The resultant force on an object equals its mass
> times its acceleration.
>
>
>
> $$F = ma$$

Here $F$ is the resultant force in N, $m$ is the mass in kg, and $a$ is the
acceleration in m/s². The acceleration is in the same direction as the resultant
force.

**Example 2.** A resultant force of $20$ N acts on a mass of $4$ kg. Find the
acceleration.

Start from the second law and make $a$ the subject:

$$F = ma$$

$$20 = 4a$$

$$a = \frac{20}{4}$$

$$a = 5\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The acceleration is 5 m/s² in the direction of the force.

**Example 3.** A car travels along a straight road at a constant velocity of
$15$ m/s. What is the resultant force on it?

The velocity is constant, so the acceleration is zero:

$$a = 0$$

By the second law,

$$F = ma = m \times 0 = 0\,\text{N}$$

The resultant force is zero. The engine thrust and the resistance balance, even
though both forces are present.

## Newton's third law

> **Newton's third law.** If object $A$ pushes or pulls object $B$, then $B$
> pushes or pulls $A$ with a force of the same size and the opposite direction.

The two forces are equal and opposite, but they act on **different objects**.
That is why they do not cancel. When you push a wall, the wall pushes back on
you with the same size of force, and it is the force on you that changes your
motion.

## Free-body diagrams

A **free-body diagram** shows one object on its own, with every force acting on
it drawn as an arrow. The diagram does not show the forces the object exerts on
other things.

Draw one before you calculate anything.

1. Choose the object you are studying.
2. Draw the object as a dot or a small box.
3. Draw an arrow for each force on it, from the object outwards.
4. Label each arrow with the force and its size, where you know it.
5. Mark the direction of the acceleration, if there is one.

> **Watch out.** Two errors cause most of the trouble in this chapter. The first
> is treating mass and weight as the same thing: a 5 kg bag has a mass of 5 kg
> everywhere, but its weight is 49 N only because $g = 9.8$ m/s². The second is
> putting a single force into $F = ma$ instead of the **resultant** force. If two
> forces act, add them first, with their directions. A common sign of the second
> error is an acceleration that does not match the way the object moves.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The crate has a mass of $20$ kg and the hanging
mass is $15$ kg.

Use $W = mg$ to write down the weight of each one.

- The weight of the crate is $20 \times 9.8 = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}$ N.
- The weight of the hanging mass is $15 \times 9.8 =
  \underline{\hspace{2cm}}$ N.

The hanging weight is larger than the whole weight of the crate. The crate's
weight, however, does not pull straight down the ramp. Section 4.2 works out how
much of it does, and that decides which way the system moves.

## You should now be able to

- describe a force, and name the common kinds: weight, normal reaction, tension,
  friction and thrust;
- tell mass from weight, and find a weight from a mass using $W = mg$;
- state Newton's three laws, and use the first law to explain a constant
  velocity;
- use $F = ma$ to find a force, a mass or an acceleration;
- draw a free-body diagram for a simple object.
