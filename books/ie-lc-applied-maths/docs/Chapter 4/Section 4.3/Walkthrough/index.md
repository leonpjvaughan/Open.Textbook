# 4.3 Motion on a smooth horizontal plane — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Sections 4.1 and 4.2, including $F = ma$ and the equilibrium
condition.

**Objectives:** explain what a smooth surface means; find the normal reaction on
a horizontal surface; find the horizontal resultant and the acceleration.

A block on a table is the simplest force problem there is. It also shows the
method you use in every later section: split the forces into two directions, and
treat each direction on its own.

## Smooth means no friction

A surface is **smooth** when friction along it is small enough to ignore. On a
smooth horizontal surface the only forces on a block are:

- its weight, $mg$, acting downwards;
- the normal reaction, $R$, acting upwards from the surface;
- any pulling or pushing force given in the question, acting horizontally.

## Two directions, two equations

The block does not move vertically, so the vertical forces are in equilibrium:

$$R = mg$$

The block may move horizontally, so the horizontal forces produce an
acceleration. Write $F = ma$ for the horizontal direction only.

The two directions do not mix. The normal reaction never appears in the
horizontal equation, because it acts vertically.

**Example 1.** A block of mass $10$ kg rests on a smooth horizontal floor. A
horizontal force of $25$ N pulls it. Find the normal reaction and the
acceleration. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

Vertical direction: the block does not move up or down, so the forces balance.

$$R = mg$$

$$R = 10 \times 9.8$$

$$R = 98\,\text{N}$$

Horizontal direction: the only horizontal force is the 25 N pull, so it is the
resultant.

$$F = ma$$

$$25 = 10a$$

$$a = \frac{25}{10}$$

$$a = 2.5\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The normal reaction is 98 N upwards, and the acceleration is 2.5 m/s² in the
direction of the pull.

**Example 2.** A crate of mass $6$ kg is pushed with $30$ N to the right and
$12$ N to the left on a smooth floor. Find the acceleration.

The forces are opposite, so subtract to find the resultant:

$$R = 30 - 12$$

$$R = 18\,\text{N}$$

Then use the second law with that resultant:

$$18 = 6a$$

$$a = \frac{18}{6}$$

$$a = 3\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The acceleration is 3 m/s² to the right. Notice that the 30 N and the 12 N are
not each put into $F = ma$; only their resultant is.

**Example 3.** A puck of mass $4$ kg slides on smooth ice. A force of $6$ N
acts against its motion. Find the acceleration, taking the direction of motion
as positive.

The force opposes the motion, so in the chosen positive direction its value is
$-6$ N.

$$F = ma$$

$$-6 = 4a$$

$$a = \frac{-6}{4}$$

$$a = -1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The negative sign means the acceleration points backwards, so the puck slows
down. Its speed falls by 1.5 m/s each second.

> **Watch out.** Do not put the normal reaction into the horizontal equation.
> The normal reaction is vertical, so it has no effect on horizontal motion. A
> second error is to use one of several forces instead of their resultant: with
> 30 N one way and 12 N the other, $F$ is 18 N, not 30 N and not 12 N. Before
> you substitute, draw the free-body diagram and mark which direction is
> positive.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. Suppose the ramp were removed and the crate were
placed on a smooth horizontal floor instead. The hanging mass of 15 kg still
pulls the rope, which now runs horizontally to the pulley.

The crate has a mass of $20$ kg and the hanging mass is $15$ kg. They move
together, so the mass being accelerated is $20 + 15 = 35$ kg, and the driving
force is the hanging weight, $15 \times 9.8 = 147$ N.

Find the acceleration on the horizontal floor:

$$a = \frac{147}{35} = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\,\text{m/s}^2$$

Keep that number. Section 4.6 does the same calculation with the crate back on
the ramp, and you can compare the two answers.

## You should now be able to

- say what a smooth surface means, and list the forces on a block on one;
- find the normal reaction on a horizontal surface from $R = mg$;
- find the resultant horizontal force, using the correct signs;
- use $F = ma$ in the horizontal direction to find an acceleration;
- read the sign of an acceleration and say whether the object speeds up or
  slows down.
