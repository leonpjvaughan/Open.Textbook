# 4.2 Resultant force and equilibrium — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 4.1, including $F = ma$; right-angled triangles and
Pythagoras' theorem; the sine and cosine of an angle.

**Objectives:** add forces along a line and at right angles; find the direction
of a resultant; use the equilibrium condition; resolve a force into two
perpendicular components.

Most real objects have more than one force on them. To use $F = ma$ you need the
**resultant**, so this section is about combining forces. It also introduces the
opposite idea: what it means for forces to cancel completely.

## Forces along a line

If two forces act along the same line, their resultant is found by adding or
subtracting, depending on their directions.

- Same direction: add the sizes.
- Opposite directions: subtract the smaller size from the larger. The resultant
  points the way the larger force points.

**Example 1.** Forces of $9$ N and $4$ N act on a particle in the same
direction. A third force of $6$ N acts in the opposite direction. Find the
resultant.

Add the two forces in the first direction, then subtract the third:

$$R = (9 + 4) - 6$$

$$R = 13 - 6$$

$$R = 7\,\text{N}$$

The resultant is 7 N in the direction of the 9 N and 4 N forces.

## Forces at right angles

Forces at right angles do not add like ordinary numbers. They form two sides of a
right-angled triangle, and the resultant is the hypotenuse.

$$R = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2}$$

**Example 2.** Forces of $6$ N and $8$ N act at right angles. Find the size of
the resultant.

Substitute into the formula:

$$R = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2}$$

$$R = \sqrt{36 + 64}$$

$$R = \sqrt{100}$$

$$R = 10\,\text{N}$$

To find the **direction**, use the tangent of the angle $\theta$ between the
resultant and one of the forces.

$$\tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite force}}{\text{adjacent force}}$$

**Example 3.** For the same two forces, find the angle between the resultant and
the 8 N force.

$$\tan\theta = \frac{6}{8}$$

$$\tan\theta = 0.75$$

$$\theta = 36.9^\circ$$

The resultant is 10 N, at 36.9° to the 8 N force.

## Equilibrium

A particle is in **equilibrium** when the resultant force on it is zero. By
Newton's second law, its acceleration is then zero. It stays at rest, or it
keeps a constant velocity.

For equilibrium, the forces must cancel in every direction. If three forces act
and two of them are known, the third must be equal and opposite to their
resultant.

## Resolving a force into components

**Resolving** a force means writing it as two perpendicular forces that have the
same effect. The two components replace the single force.

A force $F$ at an angle $\theta$ to a direction has a component along that
direction and a component at right angles to it.

$$F_x = F\cos\theta$$

$$F_y = F\sin\theta$$

where $F_x$ is the component along the direction, and $F_y$ is the component at
right angles to it.

**Example 4.** A force of $20$ N acts at $30^\circ$ above the horizontal.
Resolve it into a horizontal and a vertical component.

The horizontal component uses cosine:

$$F_x = 20\cos 30^\circ$$

$$F_x = 20 \times 0.8660$$

$$F_x = 17.32\,\text{N}$$

The vertical component uses sine:

$$F_y = 20\sin 30^\circ$$

$$F_y = 20 \times 0.5$$

$$F_y = 10\,\text{N}$$

The single 20 N force has the same effect as 17.32 N along the horizontal and
10 N vertically. Check with Pythagoras:
$\sqrt{17.32^2 + 10^2} = 20$ N.

> **Watch out.** The component **along** the angle uses cosine, and the
> component **at right angles** to it uses sine. Swapping them is the most
> common error with resolving. A quick check catches it: if the angle is small,
> the force points mostly along the direction, so the component along it should
> be the larger of the two. At 30°, the horizontal component is 17.32 N and the
> vertical is 10 N, so cosine gives the larger value. If your two components
> come out the other way round, you have swapped sine and cosine.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The crate of mass $20$ kg sits on a ramp inclined
at $30^\circ$. Its weight is $20 \times 9.8 = 196$ N, and it acts straight down.

The component of the weight down the ramp is $mg\sin\theta$:

$$20 \times 9.8 \times \sin 30^\circ = 196 \times 0.5 = 98\,\text{N}$$

The hanging mass of $15$ kg has a weight of $15 \times 9.8 = 147$ N. The rope
pulls the crate up the ramp with that weight.

Compare the two forces: 147 N up the ramp against 98 N down the ramp. The
hanging weight is larger, so the system will move with the crate going up the
ramp. Write that down next to your prediction from the start of the chapter. The
acceleration comes in Section 4.4.

## You should now be able to

- add forces along a line, using the correct signs;
- find the size and direction of the resultant of two forces at right angles;
- state the condition for equilibrium and find a missing third force;
- resolve a force into two perpendicular components using sine and cosine;
- check a resolution by combining the components with Pythagoras.
