# Chapter 4: Forces and Newton's laws

A force is a push or a pull. Newton's three laws turn forces into a model of
motion, and that model answers questions about things that slide, hang and are
pulled along. In this chapter you apply the laws to particles on planes and to
objects joined by strings.

## The chapter problem

**The crate, the ramp and the pulley.** A crate of mass $20$ kg rests on a
smooth ramp inclined at $30^\circ$ to the horizontal. A light rope is tied to the
crate. The rope runs up the ramp, parallel to the surface, over a smooth pulley
at the top, and then hangs straight down with a mass of $15$ kg on the end. The
system is released from rest.

Three questions follow from that one picture.

- Which way does the system move?
- What is the acceleration?
- What is the tension in the rope?

You cannot answer any of them yet, and you are not expected to. You can make a
first guess. The crate's weight has a component pulling it down the ramp. The
hanging mass has a weight pulling it down. Predict which of the two is larger,
and so which way the system will move. Write your prediction down and keep it.

By the end of the chapter you can resolve the crate's weight, compare the two
forces, write Newton's second law for each body, and find both the acceleration
and the tension. Then you can check your prediction.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- describe a force, and state Newton's three laws of motion;
- find the weight of an object from its mass, and tell mass from weight;
- find the resultant of forces along a line and of forces at right angles;
- use the equilibrium condition and resolve a force into two perpendicular
  components;
- find the acceleration of a particle on a smooth horizontal plane;
- resolve the weight of a particle on an inclined plane and find its
  acceleration;
- write Newton's second law for connected particles and find the tension;
- solve pulley problems, including a mass on a plane joined to a hanging mass;
- write forces in vector form and use Newton's second law component by
  component.

## Where it is used

Every machine that lifts, tows or holds something is a study in forces. A crane
holds a load on a cable; a car pulls a trailer; a ramp lets you raise a load with
less force than lifting it. Engineers choose the angle of a ramp, the strength of
a rope and the power of an engine by the same calculation you meet here. The
same laws keep a satellite in orbit and a bridge in place.

## Your turn to connect it

Somewhere in your day something is pulled, pushed or held in position: a door on
its hinge, a shopping bag, a wheelbarrow, a bicycle being pushed up a hill, a
person standing on a ladder.

Choose one of these. Write down the forces you can name, and the direction of
each one. You will be able to draw them as a free-body diagram, and to work out
what happens, by the end of the chapter.
