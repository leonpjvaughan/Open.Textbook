# 16.3 Lenz's law and conservation of energy — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write down the equation for the force on a straight current-carrying wire at
   right angles to a magnetic field, and state what each symbol means.
2. A coil has an induced emf of 3.0 V and a resistance of 6.0 Ω. Calculate the
   current induced in it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $F = BIL$, where $B$ is the flux density in teslas, $I$ is the current in
   amperes and $L$ is the length of wire in the field in metres. Lenz's law uses
   this force to explain why the motion is opposed.
2. $I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{3.0}{6.0} = 0.50\,\text{A}$. The induced emf drives
   this current, and the current in a magnetic field feels a force.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** The north pole of a bar magnet is pushed towards a coil. State the polarity
of the face of the coil nearest the magnet, and explain your answer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The flux through the coil is increasing. What must the induced current do to oppose
that?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The induced current must make the near face repel the approaching magnet.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

As the magnet approaches, the flux through the coil increases. By Lenz's law the
induced current opposes that increase, so it must try to push the magnet away.

The near face of the coil therefore becomes a **north pole**. Two north poles
repel, so the coil pushes back on the approaching magnet.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** The same magnet is now pulled away from the coil. State the polarity of the
near face, and explain the difference.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The flux is now decreasing.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The induced current must try to hold the magnet back.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

As the magnet moves away, the flux through the coil decreases. The induced current
opposes that decrease, so it tries to keep the flux up by attracting the magnet
back.

The near face of the coil becomes a **south pole**. Two unlike poles attract, so
the coil pulls on the receding magnet.

The two answers differ because the change is in the opposite direction. The induced
current always opposes the change, never the magnet.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A strong magnet is dropped down a vertical copper tube. The magnet falls
much more slowly than it would in air. Explain why, and say where the energy goes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Copper is not magnetic, but it is a conductor. A moving magnet induces currents in
a conductor.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The induced currents oppose the motion.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

As the magnet falls, the flux through each part of the copper changes. This induces
eddy currents in the copper. By Lenz's law the eddy currents flow in a direction
that opposes the change, and so they exert an upward force on the falling magnet.

The magnet still falls, but it reaches a steady slow speed where the upward
magnetic force balances its weight.

The gravitational potential energy the magnet loses is transferred to internal
energy in the copper, so the tube warms slightly. Energy is conserved.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A side of a generator coil is 0.20 m long and lies at right angles to a
field of flux density 0.15 T. The induced current in the side is 0.40 A. Calculate
the force on the side.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $F = BIL$ from Chapter 15.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = 0.15 \times 0.40 \times 0.20$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = BIL = 0.15 \times 0.40 \times 0.20 = 0.012\,\text{N}$$

The force opposes the motion of the coil. The turbine must do work against this
force to keep the coil turning, and that work becomes electrical energy.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain why a generator needs a continuous supply of energy to keep turning
while it supplies a current.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

There is a current in a magnetic field. What force does that produce?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The induced current is in a magnetic field, so each side of the coil feels a
force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The induced current flows in the coil, and the coil sits in a magnetic field. Each
side of the coil therefore feels a force, $F = BIL$.

By Lenz's law that force opposes the motion of the coil. The turbine must do work
against it to keep the coil turning. Without a continuous energy supply the coil
would slow down and stop.

The work done against the magnetic force is the energy that becomes electrical
energy in the circuit. This is why the falling water must keep flowing.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain why the coil of a generator becomes warm while it supplies a large
current.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The coil is made of wire, and wire has resistance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The induced current flows through the resistance of the coil.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The induced current flows through the wire of the coil, and the wire has
resistance. The current transfers energy to the wire at a rate $P = I^2R$, so the
coil warms up.

That energy comes from the work the turbine does against the opposing force. Some
of the mechanical energy from the water becomes electrical energy in the circuit,
and some becomes internal energy in the coil. This is one reason a real generator
is not perfectly efficient.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 15

**7.** A straight wire of length 0.25 m carries a current of 1.5 A at right angles
to a magnetic field of flux density 0.40 T. Calculate the force on the wire.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $F = BIL$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = 0.40 \times 1.5 \times 0.25$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = BIL = 0.40 \times 1.5 \times 0.25 = 0.15\,\text{N}$$

This is the same force that appears in a motor. In this chapter the force acts the
other way round: an induced current in a field feels a force that opposes the
motion, and that is Lenz's law.
</details>
</details>
</details>
