# 16.1 Magnetic flux and flux density — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A straight wire of length 0.20 m carries a current of 3.0 A at right angles to
   a magnetic field of flux density 0.15 T. Calculate the force on the wire.
2. A rectangle is 0.20 m long and 0.10 m wide. Calculate its area in square
   metres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $F = BIL = 0.15 \times 3.0 \times 0.20 = 0.090\,\text{N}$. The flux density
   $B$ is what Chapter 15 used to find a force. In this section the same $B$ is
   used to find a flux, which is a different quantity.
2. $A = 0.20 \times 0.10 = 0.020\,\text{m}^2$. Keep the area in square metres.
   A coil of 200 cm² has an area of 0.020 m², because
   $1\,\text{m}^2 = 1 \times 10^{4}\,\text{cm}^2$.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A coil of area 0.050 m² lies at right angles to a field of flux density
0.20 T. Calculate the flux through the coil.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You are given $B$ and $A$, and asked for $\Phi$. Use $\Phi = BA$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Phi = 0.20 \times 0.050$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\Phi = BA = 0.20 \times 0.050 = 0.010\,\text{Wb}$$

The unit works out too: $\text{T} \times \text{m}^2 = \text{Wb}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** The flux through a surface of area 0.080 m² is 0.024 Wb. Calculate the flux
density of the field.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $\Phi = BA$ for $B$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$B = \dfrac{\Phi}{A} = \dfrac{0.024}{0.080}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$B = \frac{\Phi}{A} = \frac{0.024}{0.080} = 0.30\,\text{T}$$

Check the sense of it: a flux of 0.024 Wb spread over 0.080 m² is 0.30 Wb in each
square metre, so the flux density is 0.30 T.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A coil of 150 turns, each of area 0.040 m², lies at right angles to a field
of flux density 0.25 T. Calculate the flux through one turn and the flux linkage
of the coil.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the flux through one turn first, then multiply by the number of turns.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Phi = 0.25 \times 0.040 = 0.010$ Wb, then $N\Phi = 150 \times 0.010$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\Phi = BA = 0.25 \times 0.040 = 0.010\,\text{Wb}$$

$$N\Phi = 150 \times 0.010 = 1.5\,\text{Wb}$$

The flux linkage is 150 times larger than the flux through one turn, because the
same field passes through all 150 turns.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A coil of 250 turns has a flux of $4.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Wb through each
turn. Calculate the flux linkage of the coil.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You are given $N$ and $\Phi$, and asked for $N\Phi$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$N\Phi = 250 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-3}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$N\Phi = 250 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-3} = 1.0\,\text{Wb}$$

The flux linkage is the quantity that Faraday's law uses, so it is worth finding
before Section 16.2 begins.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A coil is turned from a position where its plane is at right angles to the
field to a position where its plane is parallel to the field. Describe what
happens to the flux through the coil, and state its value in the second position.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At right angles, the field passes straight through the coil. What happens when the
field runs along the coil instead?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The flux starts at its largest value, $BA$, and falls as the coil turns.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

In the first position the plane of the coil is at right angles to the field, so
the flux has its largest value, $\Phi = BA$. As the coil turns, less of the field
passes through it, so the flux falls steadily. In the second position the field
runs along the plane of the coil, so no field passes through it and the flux is
zero.

This change is the reason a generator works. A coil turning steadily in a constant
field has a flux that rises and falls, and it is the change that drives the
current.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A field of flux density 0.45 T passes through a coil of area 0.020 m². The
coil is turned so that the plane is no longer at right angles to the field.
Explain why the flux through the coil is now smaller than 9.0 mWb.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The formula $\Phi = BA$ gives the largest possible value, for one particular
orientation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Turning the coil changes how much of the field passes through it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

At right angles, the flux is $\Phi = BA = 0.45 \times 0.020 = 9.0 \times 10^{-3}$
Wb, which is 9.0 mWb. That is the largest value the flux can have for this coil in
this field.

When the coil turns away from that position, the field meets the coil at an angle
and less of it passes through. The flux therefore falls below 9.0 mWb. It reaches
zero when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 14

**7.** A resistor of resistance 15 Ω has a potential difference of 6.0 V across
it. Calculate the current through it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use Ohm's law, $V = IR$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{6.0}{15}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{6.0}{15} = 0.40\,\text{A}$$

This matters here because an induced voltage drives a current through the
resistance of the coil and the circuit. Once you have the induced voltage, Ohm's
law gives the current.
</details>
</details>
</details>
