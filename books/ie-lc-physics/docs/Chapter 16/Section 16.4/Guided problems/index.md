# 16.4 A.C. and D.C. generators — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the unit of frequency?
2. A coil turns at 25 revolutions each second. Calculate the time for one complete
   revolution.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The hertz, Hz. One hertz is one complete cycle each second.
2. $T = \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{25} = 0.040\,\text{s}$. A quarter of a
   revolution takes $0.010$ s, which is the time between a peak and the next zero
   of the output.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A generator coil turns at 40 revolutions each second. Calculate the time
for one complete revolution and the time for one quarter of a revolution.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The period is the reciprocal of the frequency. A quarter turn is one quarter of
that time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$T = \dfrac{1}{40}$, then divide by 4.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$T = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{40} = 0.025\,\text{s}$$

$$\frac{T}{4} = \frac{0.025}{4} = 6.3 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{s}$$

In a quarter of a revolution the coil turns from the position of largest flux to
the position of zero flux, which is where the emf reaches its peak.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** The peak output of an a.c. generator is 20 V. Calculate its r.m.s. value.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $V_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{20}{\sqrt{2}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{20}{1.414} = 14\,\text{V}$$

The r.m.s. value is smaller than the peak, because the voltage spends most of its
time below the peak.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** An a.c. supply has an r.m.s. voltage of 6.0 V. Calculate its peak voltage.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $V_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}$ for $V_0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V_0 = \sqrt{2} \times 6.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V_0 = \sqrt{2}\,V_{\text{rms}} = 1.414 \times 6.0 = 8.5\,\text{V}$$

The peak is larger than the r.m.s. value by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$, which is about
1.41.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Sketch the graph of output voltage against time for an a.c. generator, and
mark the positions where the flux through the coil is at its largest.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The emf follows the rate of change of the flux, not the flux itself.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The graph is a sine curve. The flux is largest where the emf is zero.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The graph is a sine curve through the origin. It rises to a peak, falls back
through zero, reaches a peak in the opposite direction, and returns to zero once
per revolution.

Mark the positions of largest flux at the points where the curve **crosses the
axis**, not at the peaks. At those moments the coil is at right angles to the
field, the flux is at its largest, and the flux is changing most slowly, so the emf
is zero.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Describe the difference between the slip rings of an a.c. generator and the
split-ring commutator of a d.c. generator, and state the effect of the difference
on the output.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about whether the connections to the outside circuit swap over during a
revolution.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Slip rings keep the connections fixed. The commutator swaps them every half
revolution.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

In an a.c. generator, each end of the coil is joined to its own slip ring, and a
brush presses on each ring. The connections to the outside circuit never swap
over, so the current in the outside circuit reverses every half revolution. The
output alternates.

In a d.c. generator, the ring is cut into two halves. The brushes swap from one
half to the other every half revolution, so the connections to the outside circuit
swap over. The current in the outside circuit keeps the same direction. The output
still rises and falls, but it does not reverse.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** The peak output of a generator is 170 V. Calculate its r.m.s. value.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the peak by $\sqrt{2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{170}{1.414}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{170}{1.414} = 120\,\text{V}$$

An r.m.s. value of 120 V is a common supply voltage, and it is the value printed
on a label. The peak of 170 V is what an oscilloscope would show.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 14

**7.** An appliance has a power of 60 W when it is connected to a 12 V supply.
Calculate the current through it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $P = VI$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I = \dfrac{P}{V} = \dfrac{60}{12}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{60}{12} = 5.0\,\text{A}$$

This matters here because a generator has to supply that current. The larger the
current, the larger the force opposing the turning of the coil, and the more work
the turbine must do.
</details>
</details>
</details>
