# Chapter 16: Electromagnetic induction

Move a magnet near a coil and a current appears. No battery is connected, and
nothing touches the coil. The current is made by the **change** itself. That one
idea runs the generator in a power station and the transformer on a pole outside
your house.

## The chapter problem

**The stream that lights the village.** A stream in the hills turns a small
turbine. The turbine spins the coil of a generator, and the generator sends
electrical energy along a cable to a village below.

These are the facts to use all the way through the chapter.

- The generator coil has **180 turns**, and each turn encloses an area of
  **0.020 m²**.
- The coil turns in a magnetic field of flux density **0.30 T**.
- The coil rotates **50 times each second**, so the output alternates at 50 Hz.
- The peak value of the induced voltage is **340 V**, measured on an
  oscilloscope.
- The generator delivers a power of **24 kW**.
- The transmission cable has a total resistance of **4.0 Ω**.
- A step-up transformer raises the voltage to **24 kV** before the cable, and a
  step-down transformer lowers it again at the village.

By the end of the chapter you can find the magnetic flux through the coil and
its flux linkage, find the average voltage induced as the coil turns, explain
why the induced current opposes the change, work out the r.m.s. voltage, choose
the turns ratio of each transformer, and show how much energy the cable would
waste if the voltage were not raised first.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- define magnetic flux and magnetic flux density, and use $\Phi = BA$ with the
  units weber and tesla;
- use flux linkage $N\Phi$ and Faraday's law, $E = -N\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$,
  to find an induced voltage;
- state Lenz's law and use it to explain why an induced current opposes the
  change that produced it, and why energy is conserved;
- describe the parts of an a.c. generator and a d.c. generator, and sketch the
  output voltage against time for each;
- use the transformer relation $\dfrac{V_s}{V_p} = \dfrac{N_s}{N_p}$ and the
  efficiency of an ideal transformer;
- explain why electrical energy is transmitted at a high voltage, and calculate
  the energy wasted in a transmission cable;
<!-- tier:h -->
- define the r.m.s. value of an alternating current or voltage, and use
  $V_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $I_{\text{rms}} =
  \dfrac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}$;
<!-- /tier:h -->

## Where it is used

Almost every large power station works by induction. Falling water, steam or wind
turns a coil in a magnetic field, and the changing flux drives the current. The
same physics runs a bicycle dynamo, a wireless phone charger and an electric
toothbrush. The national grid exists because a transformer can raise a voltage for
the journey and lower it again for the socket on the wall.
