# 16.2 Faraday's law of induction — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the unit of magnetic flux?
2. A coil of 400 turns has a flux of $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Wb through each turn.
   Calculate the flux linkage of the coil.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The weber, Wb. One weber is the flux through one square metre of a field of
   flux density one tesla.
2. $N\Phi = 400 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3} = 0.80\,\text{Wb}$. Faraday's law uses
   the flux linkage, so this number is the one that changes.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** The flux through one turn of a coil of 60 turns changes by 0.020 Wb in
0.10 s. Calculate the average induced emf.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E = N\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$. You are given $N$, $\Delta\Phi$ and
$\Delta t$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 60 \times \dfrac{0.020}{0.10}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = 60 \times \frac{0.020}{0.10} = 60 \times 0.20$$

$$E = 12\,\text{V}$$

The flux changes by 0.20 Wb each second in every turn, and the coil has 60 turns,
so the emf is 60 times 0.20 V.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** The flux through one turn of a coil of 150 turns rises from
$3.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Wb to $9.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Wb in 0.030 s. Calculate the
average induced emf.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the **change** in flux first, then divide by the time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Delta\Phi = 9.0 \times 10^{-3} - 3.0 \times 10^{-3} = 6.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Wb.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\Delta\Phi = 9.0 \times 10^{-3} - 3.0 \times 10^{-3} = 6.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{Wb}$$

$$E = N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = 150 \times \frac{6.0 \times 10^{-3}}{0.030}$$

$$E = 150 \times 0.20 = 30\,\text{V}$$

Using the final flux of $9.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Wb instead of the change would give a
much larger answer. The change is what matters.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A single loop of area 0.050 m² lies at right angles to a field whose flux
density rises from 0.20 T to 0.60 T in 0.20 s. Calculate the average induced emf.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The area does not change, so the change in flux comes from the change in flux
density: $\Delta\Phi = A\,\Delta B$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Delta\Phi = 0.050 \times (0.60 - 0.20) = 0.050 \times 0.40$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\Delta\Phi = A\,\Delta B = 0.050 \times 0.40 = 0.020\,\text{Wb}$$

There is one loop, so $N = 1$:

$$E = N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = 1 \times \frac{0.020}{0.20} = 0.10\,\text{V}$$

A single loop gives a small emf. That is why a generator coil has many turns.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A coil of 250 turns has a flux change of $8.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Wb through
each turn. The average induced emf is 20 V. Calculate the time taken for the
change.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $E = N\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$ for $\Delta t$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Delta t = \dfrac{N\,\Delta\Phi}{E} = \dfrac{250 \times 8.0 \times 10^{-3}}{20}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\Delta t = \frac{N\,\Delta\Phi}{E} = \frac{250 \times 8.0 \times 10^{-3}}{20}$$

$$\Delta t = \frac{2.0}{20} = 0.10\,\text{s}$$

A change of 2.0 Wb of flux linkage in 0.10 s gives 20 V, because 2.0 divided by
0.10 is 20.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A student pushes a bar magnet into a coil slowly, and then pushes the same
magnet in quickly. The magnet travels the same distance each time. Explain why the
induced emf is larger the second time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The change in flux is the same both times. What is different?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The same change happens in a shorter time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The magnet starts outside the coil and finishes in the same position inside it, so
the change in flux linkage is the same both times.

The second push takes less time, so the **rate** of change of flux linkage is
larger. Faraday's law says the induced emf is proportional to that rate, so the
emf is larger the second time.

Doubling the speed of the magnet doubles the emf, even though the change in flux
is unchanged.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A coil of 100 turns has a flux linkage that changes from 0.20 Wb to
0.80 Wb in 0.040 s. Calculate the average induced emf.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You are given the flux **linkage**, $N\Phi$, so you do not multiply by $N$ again.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Delta(N\Phi) = 0.80 - 0.20 = 0.60$ Wb, then divide by 0.040 s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The change in flux linkage is

$$\Delta(N\Phi) = 0.80 - 0.20 = 0.60\,\text{Wb}$$

The induced emf is the rate of change of the flux linkage:

$$E = \frac{\Delta(N\Phi)}{\Delta t} = \frac{0.60}{0.040} = 15\,\text{V}$$

The number of turns is already included in the flux linkage. Multiplying by 100
again would give 1500 V, which is a hundred times too large.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 14

**7.** An induced emf of 6.0 V drives a current around a coil of resistance 3.0 Ω.
Calculate the current.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use Ohm's law, $V = IR$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{6.0}{3.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{6.0}{3.0} = 2.0\,\text{A}$$

This matters here because an induced emf behaves like the emf of a battery. Once
the emf is known, the current follows from the resistance of the circuit, and the
power transferred follows from $P = VI$.
</details>
</details>
</details>
