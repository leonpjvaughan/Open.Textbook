# 16.5 Transformers and the transmission of electricity — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A resistor of resistance 5.0 Ω carries a current of 3.0 A. Calculate the power
   transferred to it.
2. What kind of supply must a transformer be connected to?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $P = I^2R = 3.0^2 \times 5.0 = 9.0 \times 5.0 = 45\,\text{W}$. This is the
   equation that explains the waste in a transmission cable.
2. An alternating supply. A steady direct current makes a steady flux, and a steady
   flux induces no emf in the secondary coil.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A transformer has 200 turns on the primary and 800 turns on the secondary.
The primary voltage is 12 V. Calculate the secondary voltage.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $\dfrac{V_s}{V_p} = \dfrac{N_s}{N_p}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V_s = 12 \times \dfrac{800}{200}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V_s = V_p \times \frac{N_s}{N_p} = 12 \times \frac{800}{200} = 12 \times 4 = 48\,\text{V}$$

The secondary has more turns than the primary, so the voltage is stepped up.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A transformer has 1000 turns on the primary and 100 turns on the secondary.
The primary voltage is 230 V. Calculate the secondary voltage.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The ratio of the turns is now less than one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V_s = 230 \times \dfrac{100}{1000}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V_s = V_p \times \frac{N_s}{N_p} = 230 \times \frac{100}{1000} = 230 \times 0.10 = 23\,\text{V}$$

This is a step-down transformer. The secondary voltage is ten times smaller than
the primary voltage, because it has one tenth of the turns.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A transformer steps a voltage of 240 V down to 12 V. The primary coil has
500 turns. Calculate the number of turns on the secondary.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange the transformer equation for $N_s$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$N_s = N_p \times \dfrac{V_s}{V_p} = 500 \times \dfrac{12}{240}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$N_s = N_p \times \frac{V_s}{V_p} = 500 \times \frac{12}{240} = 500 \times 0.050 = 25$$

The secondary coil has 25 turns. Fewer turns give a smaller voltage.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** An ideal transformer has a primary voltage of 12 V and a primary current of
2.0 A. The secondary voltage is 48 V. Calculate the secondary current.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

An ideal transformer transfers all the power, so $V_pI_p = V_sI_s$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I_s = \dfrac{V_pI_p}{V_s} = \dfrac{12 \times 2.0}{48}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I_s = \frac{V_pI_p}{V_s} = \frac{12 \times 2.0}{48} = \frac{24}{48} = 0.50\,\text{A}$$

The power is 24 W on both sides: $12 \times 2.0 = 24$ W and $48 \times 0.50 = 24$
W. The voltage went up four times, so the current went down four times.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A transformer has an input power of 800 W and an output power of 720 W.
Calculate its efficiency.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Efficiency is the output power divided by the input power, as a percentage.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\text{efficiency} = \dfrac{720}{800} \times 100\%$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{720}{800} \times 100\% = 90\%$$

The missing 80 W is transferred to the core and the windings of the transformer,
which warm up a little.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A power station delivers 10 kW along a cable of resistance 5.0 Ω. Calculate
the power lost in the cable when the transmission voltage is 10 kV. Then calculate
the power lost when the same power is sent at 500 V, and compare the two answers.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the current first, using $I = \dfrac{P}{V}$. Then use $P_{\text{lost}} = I^2R$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

At 10 kV, $I = \dfrac{10\,000}{10\,000} = 1.0$ A. At 500 V,
$I = \dfrac{10\,000}{500} = 20$ A.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

At 10 kV:

$$I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{10\,000}{10\,000} = 1.0\,\text{A}$$

$$P_{\text{lost}} = I^2R = 1.0^2 \times 5.0 = 5.0\,\text{W}$$

At 500 V:

$$I = \frac{10\,000}{500} = 20\,\text{A}$$

$$P_{\text{lost}} = I^2R = 20^2 \times 5.0 = 400 \times 5.0 = 2000\,\text{W}$$

At 500 V the cable wastes 2000 W, which is 2.0 kW out of the 10 kW sent. At 10 kV
it wastes only 5.0 W. The current is 20 times smaller, and the loss depends on the
square of the current, so the waste falls by a factor of 400.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 14

**7.** A 24 kW supply delivers its power at 240 V. Calculate the current.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $P = VI$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I = \dfrac{P}{V} = \dfrac{24\,000}{240}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{24\,000}{240} = 100\,\text{A}$$

A current of 100 A needs a very thick cable and wastes a lot of energy in it. This
is exactly why the grid raises the voltage first: the same power at 24 kV needs a
current of only 1.0 A.
</details>
</details>
</details>
