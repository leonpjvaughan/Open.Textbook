# 16.6 R.M.S. values of alternating current — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A resistor of resistance 10 Ω carries a steady current of 2.0 A. Calculate the
   power transferred to it.
2. The r.m.s. value of an alternating voltage is 10 V. Calculate its peak value.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $P = I^2R = 2.0^2 \times 10 = 4.0 \times 10 = 40\,\text{W}$. The r.m.s. value
   of an alternating current is the steady current that gives this same power.
2. $V_0 = \sqrt{2}\,V_{\text{rms}} = 1.414 \times 10 = 14\,\text{V}$. The peak is
   larger than the r.m.s. value by a factor of about 1.41.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** An alternating current has a peak value of 8.0 A. Calculate its r.m.s.
value.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $I_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{8.0}{1.414}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{8.0}{1.414} = 5.7\,\text{A}$$

The r.m.s. value is smaller than the peak, and it is the value an a.c. ammeter
would show.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** An alternating current has an r.m.s. value of 4.0 A. Calculate its peak
value.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $I_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}$ for $I_0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I_0 = \sqrt{2} \times 4.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I_0 = \sqrt{2}\,I_{\text{rms}} = 1.414 \times 4.0 = 5.7\,\text{A}$$

The peak and the r.m.s. value differ by the factor $\sqrt{2}$. Doubling one doubles
the other.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A heater of resistance 48 Ω is connected to an r.m.s. voltage of 240 V.
Calculate the r.m.s. current and the average power transferred to the heater.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use Ohm's law with the r.m.s. values, then $P = I_{\text{rms}}^2R$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{240}{48} = 5.0$ A, then $P = 5.0^2 \times 48$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}}{R} = \frac{240}{48} = 5.0\,\text{A}$$

$$P = I_{\text{rms}}^2R = 5.0^2 \times 48 = 25 \times 48 = 1200\,\text{W}$$

The same answer comes from $P = \dfrac{V_{\text{rms}}^2}{R} = \dfrac{240^2}{48} =
1200$ W. The r.m.s. values give the average power, which is the rate at which the
heater transfers energy.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** The peak value of a mains voltage is 325 V. Calculate its r.m.s. value.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the peak by $\sqrt{2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{325}{1.414}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{325}{1.414} = 230\,\text{V}$$

This is the value printed on a mains label. The peak of 325 V is what an
oscilloscope would show, and it is the value the insulation has to withstand.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain why the mean value of an alternating current is not used to find the
energy it transfers, and state what is used instead.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The current is positive for half a cycle and negative for the other half. What is
the mean of those values? What happens to the energy in a resistor?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The energy depends on the square of the current, and a square is never negative.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Over a full cycle the current spends as long negative as positive, so its mean
value is zero. A mean of zero would predict no energy transferred, but a heater on
an alternating supply gets hot.

The energy transferred in a resistor depends on $I^2$, and a square is never
negative, so the square of the current has a positive mean. Taking the square root
of that mean gives the **r.m.s.** value.

The r.m.s. value is the steady direct current that transfers energy at the same
average rate. It is the value used in $P = I_{\text{rms}}^2R$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A lamp is rated at 60 W when it is connected to an r.m.s. voltage of 230 V.
Calculate the r.m.s. current and the peak current through the lamp.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $P = V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}$ for the r.m.s. current, then multiply by
$\sqrt{2}$ for the peak.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{60}{230}$, then $I_0 = \sqrt{2}\,I_{\text{rms}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{P}{V_{\text{rms}}} = \frac{60}{230} = 0.26\,\text{A}$$

$$I_0 = \sqrt{2}\,I_{\text{rms}} = 1.414 \times 0.26 = 0.37\,\text{A}$$

The power rating is an average, so it uses the r.m.s. current. The peak current is
about 41% larger.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 14

**7.** An appliance of power 2.0 kW is connected to a 230 V supply. Calculate the
current it draws.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $P = VI$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I = \dfrac{P}{V} = \dfrac{2000}{230}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{2000}{230} = 8.7\,\text{A}$$

This matters here because the 230 V is an r.m.s. value, so the current of 8.7 A is
an r.m.s. current. The power of 2.0 kW is the average power, and the r.m.s. values
are the ones that give it.
</details>
</details>
</details>
