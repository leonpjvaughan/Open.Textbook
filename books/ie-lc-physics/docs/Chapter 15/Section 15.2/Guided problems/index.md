# 15.2 The magnetic field of a current — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

Answer these from memory, then check.

1. State the unit of electric current and the unit of charge, and say how the two are
   related.
2. In Section 15.1 you drew the field lines of a bar magnet. State which way the lines
   run outside the magnet, and what the spacing of the lines shows.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. Current is measured in amperes, A, and charge in coulombs, C. One ampere is one
   coulomb per second, so $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$. A current is a flow of charge, and a flow
   of charge is what makes a magnetic field.
2. Outside a bar magnet the field lines run from the north pole to the south pole.
   Closer lines mean a stronger field, so the field is strongest at the poles. Both
   rules apply to the field of a current as well.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A straight wire carries a current vertically upwards. State the direction of the
magnetic field at a point to the left of the wire.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the right-hand grip rule. Point the thumb along the current, then read the
direction your fingers curl at the point you are asked about.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The thumb points upwards. The fingers curl around the wire, so on one side of the wire
they point into the page and on the other side they point out of the page.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

The field at a point to the left of the wire points **out of the page**. To the right of
the wire it points into the page. The field lines are circles around the wire, so the
field direction turns as you move around the wire.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A straight wire carries a current into the page. Describe the shape of the field
and state its direction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A current into the page is drawn with a cross. Point your thumb into the page.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The field lines are circles in the plane of the page, centred on the wire.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

The field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire, in the plane of the page.
With the thumb pointing into the page, the fingers curl clockwise, so the field circles
the wire clockwise. The field is strongest close to the wire and weaker further away.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A solenoid is wound so that, seen from the right-hand end, the current flows
clockwise around the coil. State which end is the north pole.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the right-hand grip rule for a solenoid: fingers follow the current, thumb points
to the north end.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Curl the fingers of the right hand clockwise as seen from the right-hand end.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

Curl the fingers clockwise as seen from the right. The thumb points away from the
viewer, to the left, so the **left-hand end** of the solenoid is its north pole and the
right-hand end is its south pole.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A student winds a coil around a plastic tube and connects it to a battery. She
then slides a soft iron rod inside the tube. Describe what happens to the magnetic
field, and explain why.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The rod is a magnetic material. Think about its domains.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The field of the coil lines up the domains in the iron.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

The field becomes much stronger. The soft iron is a magnetic material, so the field of
the coil lines up its domains. The aligned iron then adds its own field to the field of
the coil. The iron is a temporary magnet, so the whole field disappears when the
current is switched off.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** State two ways to make the magnetic field of an electromagnet stronger, and
explain each one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the current and about the coil itself.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

More current means a stronger field, and more turns mean more fields adding together.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

Increase the current, because the field of a current gets stronger as the current gets
larger. Increase the number of turns on the coil, because the field of every turn adds
to the field of the others. A third way is to keep the soft iron core, which adds its
own field to the field of the coil.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 14

**6.** A current of 0.40 A flows through a wire for 30 s. Calculate the charge that
passes, and state what else the moving charge produces.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $Q = It$, then think back to the start of this section.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$Q = 0.40 \times 30$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

$Q = It = 0.40 \times 30 = 12$ C. The moving charge also produces a magnetic field
around the wire, which is the effect Oersted noticed when a compass needle near the
wire twitched.

</details>
</details>
</details>
