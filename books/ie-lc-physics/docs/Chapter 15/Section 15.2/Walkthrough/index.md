# 15.2 The magnetic field of a current — Learn

**Prerequisites:** magnetic fields and field lines from Section 15.1; electric
current from Chapter 14.

**Objectives:** describe the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying wire;
use the right-hand grip rule; describe the field of a solenoid and find its poles;
explain the effect of a soft iron core; define magnetic flux density.

By the end of this section, you can work out the direction of the field a current
makes, and say how to make that field stronger.

## A current makes a magnetic field

In 1820 the Danish physicist Hans Christian Oersted was teaching when he noticed
something odd. A compass needle lying near a wire twitched whenever the current was
switched on. The needle was not near a magnet, so the current itself had to be making
a magnetic field.

That single observation joined two subjects that had been separate. Electricity and
magnetism are two parts of one idea: **a moving charge makes a magnetic field**.

The effect is not a small one. A current of a few amperes makes a field strong enough
to move a compass needle across a room, and the same field is what turns every
electric motor.

## The field around a straight wire

The field around a long straight wire has a simple shape.

- The field lines are **concentric circles** around the wire, in a plane at right
  angles to the wire.
- The field is **stronger close to the wire** and weaker further away.
- A **larger current** makes the field stronger everywhere around the wire.

To find the direction, use the **right-hand grip rule**: point the thumb of your right
hand along the conventional current, and your fingers curl in the direction of the
field. For a current coming out of the page, the field circles **anticlockwise** as you
look at the page.

The strength of a magnetic field is measured by its **magnetic flux density**, $B$. Its
unit is the **tesla**, T. A field of 1 T is a strong field: the Earth's field is about
$5 \times 10^{-5}$ T, and the field in an MRI scanner is a few tesla.

**Example 1.** A straight wire carries a current vertically upwards. Find the direction
of the magnetic field at a point to the right of the wire.

Point the thumb of the right hand upwards, along the current. The fingers curl around
the wire. At a point to the right of the wire, the fingers point **into the page**, so
that is the direction of the field there.

**Example 2.** A straight wire carries a current into the page. Find the direction of
the field above the wire.

Point the thumb into the page. The fingers curl clockwise as you look at the page, so
above the wire the field points to the **right**.

## The field of a solenoid

Wind a wire into a coil, or **solenoid**, and the fields of all the turns add together.
The result is a field that looks just like the field of a bar magnet.

- Outside the solenoid, the field lines run from one end to the other, curving round
  like the lines of a bar magnet.
- Inside the solenoid, the field lines are close together and almost straight, so the
  field there is strong and uniform.
- One end of the solenoid acts as a **north pole** and the other as a **south pole**.

The **right-hand grip rule for a solenoid** finds the poles: curl the fingers of your
right hand in the direction of the current around the coil, and your thumb points to the
**north end**.

**Example 3.** A solenoid is wound so that, seen from the left, the current flows
anticlockwise around the coil. Which end is the north pole?

Curl the fingers of the right hand anticlockwise as seen from the left. The thumb points
to the **left**, so the left end of the solenoid is its north pole.

## The effect of a soft iron core

Slide a piece of soft iron inside the solenoid and the field becomes much stronger,
often hundreds of times stronger. Iron is a magnetic material, so the field of the coil
lines up its domains, and the iron then adds its own field to the field of the coil.

The iron is a temporary magnet, so the effect lasts only while the current flows. Switch
the current off and the field disappears. A solenoid with a soft iron core is called an
**electromagnet**, and it is the reason a crane can lift a car and then drop it.

Three things make the field of an electromagnet stronger:

- a larger current;
- more turns on the coil;
- a soft iron core.

> **Watch out.** The right-hand grip rule gives the direction of the **field**, not the
> direction of the force. It also uses the **conventional current**, which flows from the
> positive terminal to the negative terminal, the opposite way to the electrons. A
> second trap is to think that the field of a solenoid points along the wire. The field
> circles each turn of the wire; the turns together make a field that runs along the
> axis of the coil.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The fan's coil carries a current of 0.25 A. The current
flows one way around the top of the coil and the opposite way around the bottom.

Use the right-hand grip rule for a solenoid to work out which end of the coil becomes
its north pole. Then write one sentence saying what a soft iron core would do to the
strength of the field. Keep your answer: Section 15.3 uses the field of the magnet and

## You should now be able to

- describe the field around a straight current-carrying wire, and use the right-hand
  grip rule to find its direction;
- describe how the field changes with the size of the current and with the distance
  from the wire;
- describe the field of a solenoid, and find its north and south ends with the
  right-hand grip rule;
- explain what a soft iron core does, and why it works;
- state what magnetic flux density is and give its unit;
- name three ways to make the field of an electromagnet stronger.
