# Chapter 15: Magnetism and the force on a current

A magnet can push and pull without touching anything. So can a wire carrying a
current: hold it near a compass and the needle turns. This chapter follows that idea
from the field around a magnet to the force that turns a motor.

## The chapter problem

**What makes the fan turn?** A handheld fan runs from two 1.5 V cells. Inside it is a
small motor: a coil of wire that spins between the poles of a permanent magnet. These
are the facts to use all the way through the chapter.

- the coil has **50 turns**;
- the side of the coil that sits in the magnetic field is **2.0 cm** long;
- the magnetic flux density of the magnet is **0.15 T**;
- the coil carries a current of **0.25 A**.

By the end of the chapter you can find the force on one side of the coil, explain why
the two sides are pushed in opposite directions, and say why the commutator has to
reverse the current every half turn. You can also say what happens to the force when
the magnet is stronger, the current is larger, or the coil has more turns.

At Higher level you can also explain how that force comes from the force on each

## By the end of this chapter you can

- describe the magnetic field of a bar magnet, and use field lines to show its
  direction and its strength;
- describe the interaction of two magnetic fields, and tell a permanent magnet from a
  temporary one;
- describe the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying wire and around a
  solenoid, and use the right-hand grip rule to find its direction;
- explain what a soft iron core does to the field of a solenoid;
- use $F = BIl$ to find the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field;
- use Fleming's left-hand rule to find the direction of that force;
<!-- tier:h -->
- use $F = Bqv$ to find the force on a moving charge, and describe the circular path it
  produces.
<!-- /tier:h -->
- explain how a simple direct-current motor turns, and what the split-ring commutator
  does.

## Where it is used

Every electric motor uses the force in this chapter: the fan in a laptop, the pump in
a washing machine, the starter motor in a car and the wheels of an electric bike. The
same force moves the cone of a loudspeaker and the needle of an older ammeter.
Electromagnets lift scrap metal, lock a door and open a valve, and every one of them
is a coil with a current in it.

## Your turn to connect it

Where have you felt a magnet push or pull today? A fridge door, a phone case, a pair
of headphones, a magnetic charger, the catch on a bag.

Choose one. Write down which part is the magnet and which part is the magnetic
material. Keep your answer. By the end of the chapter you can explain what the field
does to the material, and why a current in a coil can do the same job.
