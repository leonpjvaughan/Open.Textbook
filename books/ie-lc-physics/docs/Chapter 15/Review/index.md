# Chapter 15: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**What makes the fan turn?** A handheld fan runs from two 1.5 V cells. Its motor has a
coil of 50 turns, the side of the coil in the magnetic field is 2.0 cm long, the flux
density is 0.15 T, and the coil carries a current of 0.25 A.

In these formulas $F$ is a force, $B$ the magnetic flux density, $I$ the current,
$l$ the length of wire in the field, $q$ a charge and $v$ its speed.

**Step 1: the force on one side of one turn.** The side of the coil is at right angles
to the field, so

$$F = BIl = 0.15 \times 0.25 \times 0.020 = 7.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{N}$$

The length must be in metres: 2.0 cm is 0.020 m.

**Step 2: the force on the whole coil.** Every turn carries the same current, so the
50 turns add their forces:

$$F_{\text{total}} = 50 \times 7.5 \times 10^{-4} = 3.75 \times 10^{-2}\,\text{N}$$

That is $3.8 \times 10^{-2}$ N to two significant figures.

**Step 3: why the coil turns.** The current flows one way along one side of the coil
and the opposite way along the other side. The field is the same for both, so the force
on each side is the same size but in the opposite direction. The two opposite forces
act on opposite sides of the axis, so together they produce a turning effect. The coil
turns.

**Step 4: why the commutator is needed.** Without a commutator the forces keep pointing
the same way in space, so after half a turn the coil reaches the position where its
plane is at right angles to the field. The forces then act along the same line through
the axis and produce no turning effect, so the coil stops. The split-ring commutator
reverses the current every half turn, which reverses the force on each side, so the
coil keeps turning the same way.

**Step 5: the force on one electron.** The force on the wire is the sum of the forces
on the moving electrons inside it. Each electron drifts at about $1.0 \times 10^{-4}$
m/s, so

$$F = Bqv = 0.15 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.0 \times 10^{-4} = 2.4 \times 10^{-24}\,\text{N}$$

One electron feels almost nothing, but a copper wire holds an enormous number of them,
and their forces add up to the $3.8 \times 10^{-2}$ N on the coil.

**The answer.** The force on one side of one turn is $7.5 \times 10^{-4}$ N, and the
force on that side of the 50-turn coil is $3.8 \times 10^{-2}$ N. The other side is
pushed the opposite way, so the coil turns. The commutator reverses the current every
half turn so the coil keeps turning one way. A stronger magnet, a larger current, more
turns or a longer side in the field would each make the fan turn faster.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies**
and say why. Three of them come from earlier chapters, and one of them asks you to
choose between the force on a wire and the force on a single particle.

1. A wire of length 0.30 m carries a current of 4.0 A at right angles to a field of
   flux density 0.25 T. Calculate the force on the wire.
2. A wire lies in a field that points from left to right, and the current flows
   downwards. State the direction of the force on the wire.
3. A straight wire carries a current towards the top of the page. State the direction
   of the magnetic field at a point to the right of the wire.
4. A solenoid is wound so that, seen from the left-hand end, the current flows
   clockwise around the coil. State which end is the north pole.
5. A coil of 30 turns has a side of length 4.0 cm in a field of 0.50 T, and carries a
   current of 0.20 A. Calculate the total force on one side of the coil.
6. A charged particle moves at right angles to a magnetic field. Would you use
   $F = BIl$ or $F = Bqv$ to find the force on it? Explain how you decide.
7. A 12 Ω resistor has a current of 0.50 A through it. Calculate the potential
   difference across it and the power it transfers.
8. Two charges of $+3.0\ \mu\text{C}$ and $+2.0\ \mu\text{C}$ are $0.15$ m apart in
   air. Calculate the force between them. Take $k = 9.0 \times 10^{9}$ N m$^{2}$
   C$^{-2}$.
9. A spring of spring constant 150 N/m is stretched by 0.20 m. Calculate the energy
   stored in it.

## What you can now do

- describe the magnetic field of a bar magnet, and use field lines to show its
  direction and its strength;
- describe the interaction of two magnetic fields, and use the domain model to explain
  magnetisation;
- tell a permanent magnet from a temporary magnet;
- describe the field around a straight current-carrying wire and around a solenoid, and
  use the right-hand grip rule;
- explain what a soft iron core does to the field of a solenoid, and name three ways to
  make an electromagnet stronger;
- use $F = BIl$ to find the force on a current-carrying conductor;
- use Fleming's left-hand rule to find the direction of that force;

- use $F = Bqv$ to find the force on a moving charge, and describe the circular path it
  follows;
- explain why the magnetic force does no work on a moving charge.

  does;
- name four changes that increase the turning effect on a motor coil, and describe the
  energy transfers in a motor.

## Thinking back

Look at the magnet you chose at the start of the chapter — a fridge door, a phone case,
a pair of headphones, a magnetic charger.

You can now say what its field does to the magnetic material nearby, and why the force
falls off with distance. You can also estimate the force on a current in a field, and
say which way it points.

Write down one number from your own choice: a flux density, a current or a length. Then
work out what would happen to the force if that number doubled. If your estimate is
rough, write that down too. Saying how rough an estimate is belongs in the physics, and
it is exactly what the Physics In Practice Investigation asks you to do.
