# 15.1 Magnetic fields and magnetic materials — Learn

**Prerequisites:** forces and Newton's third law from Chapter 3; the idea of a field
from Chapter 13.

**Objectives:** describe the poles of a magnet and the force between them; describe a
magnetic field with field lines; describe the interaction of two magnetic fields;
explain magnetisation with the domain model; tell a permanent magnet from a temporary
one.

By the end of this section, you can describe the field around a magnet and explain what
makes a material magnetic.

## Poles and the force between them

A **magnet** has two ends, called poles. One is the **north pole** and the other is the
**south pole**. A freely turning magnet points north and south, and that is how the
poles are named.

The force between two poles is simple to state.

- Like poles **repel**: north pushes north away, and south pushes south away.
- Unlike poles **attract**: a north pole pulls a south pole towards it.

A magnetic material is also attracted to either pole of a magnet. Iron filings, a
paper clip and a steel screw are all pulled towards a magnet, whichever pole comes
near them. That attraction is not a pole-to-pole force, and it is the first clue that
a magnetic material behaves differently from a magnet.

## Magnetic fields and field lines

The force between two magnets acts without them touching. The region around a magnet
in which it exerts a force is its **magnetic field**.

A field is drawn with **field lines**. Three rules describe them.

- Outside the magnet, the lines run from the **north pole to the south pole**. An arrow
  on a line shows the direction of the force on a north pole placed there.
- The lines are drawn **closer together where the field is stronger**. The field is
  strongest at the poles.
- The lines **never cross**, because the field has only one direction at any point.

**Example 1.** Sketch the field lines between the north pole of one magnet and the
south pole of another.

The two poles attract, so the lines run from the north pole of the first magnet
straight across to the south pole of the second. Near the gap the lines are close
together, because the field is strong there. Away from the gap they curve round to the
far poles.

**Example 2.** Sketch the field lines between two north poles facing each other.

The two poles repel. The lines leave each north pole and bend away from the gap, so
the two sets of lines push apart. In the middle of the gap there is a point where the
field is very weak, because the two fields cancel there.

The second example is the **interaction of magnetic fields**: where two fields overlap,
they add as vectors, and the shape of the combined field is what the reader sees in the
pattern of the lines.

## The Earth's magnetic field

A compass needle is a small magnet that turns freely. It comes to rest pointing north
and south, so the Earth must have a magnetic field.

The Earth behaves as if it has a giant bar magnet inside it. The magnetic pole near the
geographic north is a **magnetic south pole**, because the north end of a compass
needle is attracted to it. The field is weak compared with a laboratory magnet, but it
is enough to steer a compass.

## Magnetic materials and domains

Only a few materials are strongly magnetic. They are **iron, nickel and cobalt**, and
alloys of these metals, such as **steel**. Copper, aluminium and plastic are not
magnetic, and a magnet does not attract them.

Why does iron become magnetic when a magnet comes near it? The answer is a model. A
magnetic material is made of many tiny regions called **domains**, and each domain
behaves like a small magnet of its own.

- In an **unmagnetised** piece, the domains point in random directions. Their fields
  cancel, so the piece has no overall field.
- In a **magnetised** piece, the domains point the same way. Their fields add, so the
  piece has a north pole and a south pole.

Bring a magnet near a piece of soft iron and the field lines up the domains with it, so
the iron becomes a magnet and is attracted. Take the magnet away and the domains
return to random directions, so the iron loses its magnetism. That is why a paper clip
sticks to a magnet while the magnet is there and falls away when it is removed.

> **Watch out.** A magnet always has two poles. Cut a bar magnet in half and you do not
> get a north piece and a south piece: you get two smaller magnets, each with its own
> north and south pole. The domain model explains this, because every domain is itself
> a small magnet with two poles. Also, a magnet attracts a magnetic material with
> either pole, so attraction alone does not tell you which pole you are holding.

## Permanent and temporary magnets

Two kinds of magnet are worth telling apart.

- A **permanent magnet**, such as steel, keeps its magnetism when the magnetising field
  is removed. A fridge magnet and the magnet in a loudspeaker are permanent magnets.
- A **temporary magnet**, such as soft iron, is strongly magnetised while it is in a
  field but loses most of its magnetism when the field is removed. The core of an
  electromagnet is a temporary magnet.

Steel is hard to magnetise but keeps its magnetism; soft iron magnetises readily but
loses it again. That difference is why a permanent magnet is made from steel and the
core of an electromagnet is made from soft iron.

## You should now be able to

- describe the force between two poles, and between a magnet and a magnetic material;
- draw and read field lines, and say what their direction and spacing mean;
- describe what happens where two magnetic fields overlap;
- explain magnetisation and demagnetisation with the domain model;
- tell a permanent magnet from a temporary magnet, and say what each is used for;
- say which materials are strongly magnetic and which are not.
