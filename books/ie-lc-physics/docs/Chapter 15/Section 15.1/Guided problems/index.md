# 15.1 Magnetic fields and magnetic materials — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

Answer these from memory, then check.

1. State Newton's third law, and say what it tells you about the two forces in an
   interaction.
2. In Chapter 13 you drew electric field lines. State what a field line shows, and
   which way the lines point around a positive charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. If object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts a force of the same size on A
   in the opposite direction. The two forces act on different objects, so they do not
   cancel. A magnet exerts a force on another magnet in the same way, at a distance.
2. A field line shows the direction of the force on a small positive charge placed at
   that point. Around a positive charge the lines point outwards. The magnetic field
   lines in this section work in the same way, but the test object is a north pole.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Two bar magnets are placed with their south poles facing each other. Describe
the force between them and the pattern of the field lines in the gap.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start from the rule for two poles of the same kind, then think about which way the
field lines have to bend.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Like poles repel, so the two south poles push each other away. Each magnet sends out
field lines that end on its own south pole, so the lines near the gap point into each
magnet.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

Like poles repel, so each magnet is pushed away from the other. The field lines from
the two south poles curve away from the gap, and the field in the middle of the gap is
weak because the two fields cancel there.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A student says that the field lines around a bar magnet are closest together at
the ends. Explain what that tells you about the field.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Field lines are drawn closer together where the field is stronger.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The ends of a bar magnet are its poles.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

The field is strongest at the poles. Close field lines mean a strong field, and at the
ends of the magnet the lines crowd together, so the poles are where the field is
strongest. In the middle of the magnet, and far away from it, the lines are further
apart and the field is weaker.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A steel paper clip is picked up by a magnet. Explain, using the domain model,
why the clip is attracted to the magnet.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The clip is a magnetic material, not a magnet to begin with.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

In an unmagnetised piece of steel the domains point in random directions, so their
fields cancel.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

Steel is a magnetic material, so it is made of domains that each behave like a small
magnet. In the unmagnetised clip the domains point in random directions and their
fields cancel. When the magnet comes near, the field lines up the domains with it, so
the clip becomes a magnet with its own north and south poles. The unlike poles of the
clip and the magnet attract, so the clip is pulled towards the magnet.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A bar magnet is broken in half. Describe what the two pieces are, and explain
why.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the smallest unit of magnetism in the domain model.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Every domain is itself a small magnet with a north end and a south end.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

The two pieces are each a complete magnet, with its own north pole and south pole. The
domains inside the steel are small magnets, and breaking the bar does not separate a
north pole from a south pole. It separates one set of complete domains from another,
so each piece still has both poles.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain why a permanent magnet is made from steel but the core of an
electromagnet is made from soft iron.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about what happens to the domains when the magnetising field is removed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Steel keeps its magnetism; soft iron loses most of its magnetism.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

Steel is hard to magnetise, but once its domains are lined up they stay lined up, so
steel keeps its magnetism and makes a good permanent magnet. Soft iron magnetises
readily, because its domains line up with only a small field, but they return to random
directions when the field is removed. That is what an electromagnet needs, because it
has to switch on and off with the current.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 13

**6.** An electric field is drawn with field lines that start on positive charge and
end on negative charge. A magnetic field is drawn with lines that run from a north
pole to a south pole. State one way in which the two fields are alike and one way in
which they differ.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Both are fields, so both are drawn with lines that show direction and strength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Think about the test object you would use to find the direction of each field.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full answer</summary>

Both fields are drawn with lines whose direction shows the force on a test object and
whose spacing shows the strength of the field. They differ in the test object: the
electric field line shows the force on a small positive charge, and the magnetic field
line shows the force on a north pole. A magnetic field line always closes on itself,
because a magnet has no single pole, while an electric field line can start and end on
separate charges.

</details>
</details>
</details>
