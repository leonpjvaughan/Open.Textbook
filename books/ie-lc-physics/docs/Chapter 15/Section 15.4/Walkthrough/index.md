# 15.4 The force on a moving charge — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the force on a current-carrying conductor from Section 15.3; charge
and current from Chapters 13 and 14.

**Objectives:** use $F = Bqv$ to find the force on a moving charge; describe the
circular path of a charged particle in a magnetic field; explain why the magnetic force
does no work on the particle.

By the end of this section, you can find the force on a single moving charge and
describe the path it follows.

## One moving charge

A current in a wire is a flow of charge. Each charge in that flow is a moving charge,
and the field pushes on it. So the force on a wire is really the sum of the forces on
all the charges moving inside it.

Take a single charged particle and send it through a magnetic field. When its velocity
is at right angles to the field, the force on it is

$$F = Bqv$$

- $F$ is the force on the particle, in newtons (N);
- $B$ is the magnetic flux density, in tesla (T);
- $q$ is the charge on the particle, in coulombs (C);
- $v$ is the speed of the particle, in metres per second (m/s).

This is the same physics as the force on a wire. If the particle is not moving, there is
no force, which is why a stationary charge feels nothing from a magnetic field.

**Example 1.** An electron of charge $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C moves at
$2.0 \times 10^{6}$ m/s at right angles to a field of flux density 0.30 T. Calculate the
force on the electron.

$$
F = Bqv
$$

$$
F = 0.30 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{6}
$$

$$
F = 9.6 \times 10^{-14}\,\text{N}
$$

**Example 2.** A proton of charge $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C moves at
$5.0 \times 10^{5}$ m/s at right angles to a field of 0.20 T. Calculate the force on
the proton.

$$
F = Bqv = 0.20 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 5.0 \times 10^{5} = 1.6 \times 10^{-14}\,\text{N}
$$

**Example 3.** A charged particle of charge $3.2 \times 10^{-19}$ C moves at
$1.0 \times 10^{6}$ m/s at right angles to a magnetic field and experiences a force of
$2.4 \times 10^{-14}$ N. Calculate the magnetic flux density.

Rearranging $F = Bqv$ for the flux density:

$$
B = \frac{F}{qv} = \frac{2.4 \times 10^{-14}}{3.2 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.0 \times 10^{6}} = 0.075\,\text{T}
$$

## The direction of the force

The force is at right angles to the velocity and at right angles to the field. Use
Fleming's left-hand rule, with the second finger along the velocity of a **positive**
charge.

- For a **positive** charge, the thumb gives the direction of the force directly.
- For a **negative** charge, such as an electron, the force is in the **opposite**
  direction to the thumb.

The direction of the force is always at right angles to the velocity. That is the key
to the shape of the path.

## Why the path is a circle

Because the force is always at right angles to the velocity, it changes the direction
of the velocity but not its size. The particle is pulled sideways the whole time, so it
travels in a **circle**.

The magnetic force does **no work** on the particle. Work is force multiplied by the
distance moved in the direction of the force, and here that distance is zero, because
the motion is always at right angles to the force. No work means no change in kinetic
energy, so the speed stays the same. Only the direction changes.

The radius of the circle grows with the speed and shrinks with the field:

- a **faster** particle makes a **bigger** circle;
- a **stronger** field makes a **smaller** circle.

This is how a mass spectrometer separates ions, and how the old cathode-ray tube in a
television steered its electron beam.

> **Watch out.** Two things catch students here. First, the force is at right angles to
> the velocity, so the particle speeds up or slows down **not at all**; only its
> direction changes. A magnetic field cannot change the speed of a charged particle.
> Second, the direction rule is for a positive charge. For an electron the force is
> reversed, so the electron curves the other way. Also, a stationary charge feels no
> magnetic force at all.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The force on one side of the fan's coil is
$3.8 \times 10^{-2}$ N. The electrons in the copper wire drift along at about
$1.0 \times 10^{-4}$ m/s, and the flux density is 0.15 T.

Calculate the force on one drifting electron. Then write one sentence explaining how
the force on the whole wire is built from the forces on all the moving electrons.

## You should now be able to

- use $F = Bqv$ to find a force, a charge, a speed or a flux density;
- find the direction of the force with Fleming's left-hand rule, reversing it for a
  negative charge;
- describe the path of a charged particle moving at right angles to a magnetic field,
  and say why it is a circle;
- explain why the magnetic force does no work, and why the speed stays constant;
- explain how the force on a current-carrying wire follows from the force on each
  moving charge.
