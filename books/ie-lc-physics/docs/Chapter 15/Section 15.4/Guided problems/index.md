# 15.4 The force on a moving charge — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

Answer these from memory, then check.

1. State the charge on an electron, and say whether it is positive or negative.
2. State the equation for the kinetic energy of a moving object, and give the unit of
   energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The charge on an electron is $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C, and it is negative. That
   negative sign is why an electron curves the opposite way to a positive charge in the
   same magnetic field.
2. $E_k = \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, measured in joules, J. In this section the magnetic force
   does no work, so the kinetic energy and the speed of the particle stay the same.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** An electron of charge $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C moves at $3.0 \times 10^{6}$ m/s at
right angles to a magnetic field of flux density 0.20 T. Calculate the force on the
electron.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Write down which quantities you are given, then use $F = Bqv$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = 0.20 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 3.0 \times 10^{6}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = Bqv = 0.20 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 3.0 \times 10^{6} = 9.6 \times 10^{-14}\,\text{N}$$

The force is tiny by everyday standards, but an electron is very light, so it still
curves the path noticeably.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A charged particle of charge $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C moves at
$5.0 \times 10^{6}$ m/s at right angles to a magnetic field and experiences a force of
$1.2 \times 10^{-14}$ N. Calculate the magnetic flux density.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $F = Bqv$ for $B$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$B = \dfrac{F}{qv} = \dfrac{1.2 \times 10^{-14}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 5.0 \times 10^{6}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$B = \frac{F}{qv} = \frac{1.2 \times 10^{-14}}{8.0 \times 10^{-13}} = 0.015\,\text{T}$$

If you wrote 0.15 T, you lost a factor of ten in the powers of ten. Write the
denominator out in full before you divide.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A particle of charge $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C moves at right angles to a field of
flux density 0.40 T and experiences a force of $3.2 \times 10^{-14}$ N. Calculate the
speed of the particle.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $F = Bqv$ for $v$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \dfrac{F}{qB} = \dfrac{3.2 \times 10^{-14}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.40}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \frac{F}{qB} = \frac{3.2 \times 10^{-14}}{6.4 \times 10^{-20}} = 5.0 \times 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$$

The unit checks out: newtons divided by coulomb-tesla give metres per second.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Explain why a magnetic field changes the direction of a moving charged particle
but not its speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start from the direction of the force compared with the velocity.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work is force multiplied by the distance moved in the direction of the force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The magnetic force is always at right angles to the velocity of the particle. Work is
force multiplied by the distance moved in the direction of the force, and that distance
is zero here, because the motion is at right angles to the force. The force therefore
does no work. With no work done there is no change in kinetic energy, so the speed
stays the same and only the direction of the velocity changes.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A negative particle and a positive particle move in the same direction through the
same magnetic field. State how the forces on them compare, and explain how you know.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Fleming's left-hand rule gives the direction for a positive charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The force depends on the sign of the charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The two forces are equal in size and opposite in direction. The size depends on the
size of the charge, the speed and the field, which are the same for both particles. The
direction from Fleming's left-hand rule is for a positive charge, and for a negative
charge the force is reversed.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 13

**6.** A body gains $2.0 \times 10^{13}$ electrons. Calculate the size of its charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $q = ne$, with the charge on one electron.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$q = 2.0 \times 10^{13} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$q = ne = 2.0 \times 10^{13} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.2 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{C}$$

That is $3.2\ \mu\text{C}$. The charge matters here because the force on a moving
particle is proportional to the size of its charge.

</details>
</details>
</details>
