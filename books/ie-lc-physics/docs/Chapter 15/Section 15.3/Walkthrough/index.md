# 15.3 The force on a current-carrying conductor — Learn

**Prerequisites:** magnetic fields from Section 15.1; the field of a current from
Section 15.2; current and force from Chapters 14 and 3.

**Objectives:** describe the motor effect; use $F = BIl$; use Fleming's left-hand rule
to find the direction of the force.

By the end of this section, you can find the force on a wire in a magnetic field and
say which way it points.

## The motor effect

A wire carrying a current has its own magnetic field. Put that wire in the field of a
magnet and the two fields overlap. Where they overlap, the combined field is stronger
on one side of the wire than on the other, and the wire is pushed from the strong side
towards the weak side.

The result is a force on the wire. It is called the **motor effect**, because it is the
effect that turns every electric motor.

Three things have to be true for the force to be as large as possible.

- The wire carries a current.
- The wire is in a magnetic field.
- The current is at right angles to the field.

## The force equation

When the current is at right angles to the field, the size of the force is

$$F = BIl$$

- $F$ is the force on the wire, in newtons (N);
- $B$ is the magnetic flux density, in tesla (T);
- $I$ is the current in the wire, in amperes (A);
- $l$ is the length of wire that is **in the field**, in metres (m).

The equation shows how to make the force larger: use a stronger magnet, a larger
current, or a longer piece of wire in the field. Doubling any one of the three doubles
the force.

If the current is **parallel** to the field, the force is zero. The force depends only
on the part of the current that is at right angles to the field.

**Example 1.** A wire of length 0.20 m carries a current of 3.0 A at right angles to a
field of flux density 0.40 T. Calculate the force on the wire.

$$
F = BIl
$$

$$
F = 0.40 \times 3.0 \times 0.20
$$

$$
F = 0.24\,\text{N}
$$

**Example 2.** A wire of length 0.15 m in a field of 0.25 T experiences a force of
0.030 N. Calculate the current.

Rearranging $F = BIl$ for the current:

$$
I = \frac{F}{Bl}
$$

$$
I = \frac{0.030}{0.25 \times 0.15} = 0.80\,\text{A}
$$

**Example 3.** A wire of length 0.25 m carries a current of 2.0 A at right angles to a
field of 0.60 T. Calculate the force.

$$
F = BIl = 0.60 \times 2.0 \times 0.25 = 0.30\,\text{N}
$$

## The direction of the force

The force is at right angles to **both** the current and the field. That gives three
directions to keep track of, so there is a rule for it.

**Fleming's left-hand rule.** Hold your left hand with the first finger, the second
finger and the thumb at right angles to each other.

- The **first finger** points in the direction of the magnetic field, from north to
  south.
- The **second finger** points in the direction of the conventional current.
- The **thumb** points in the direction of the force on the conductor.

A useful check is that the force is always at right angles to the wire and to the
field. If your thumb points along the wire or along the field, you have the rule wrong.

**Example 4.** A wire lies in a field that points from left to right, and the current
flows upwards. Find the direction of the force.

First finger points to the right, along the field. Second finger points upwards, along
the current. The thumb then points **out of the page**, so the force on the wire is out
of the page.

> **Watch out.** The letter $l$ in $F = BIl$ is the length of wire that is **inside the
> field**, not the whole length of the wire. If a 1.0 m wire crosses a 0.20 m magnet
> gap, then $l = 0.20$ m. A second trap is the direction: the force is not along the
> wire and not along the field, but at right angles to both. Also remember that the
> force is zero when the current is parallel to the field, however large the current
> is.

2.0 cm long, the flux density is 0.15 T, and the current is 0.25 A.

Calculate the force on one side of one turn, then the total force on that side of the
50-turn coil. Then state the direction of the force on the opposite side of the coil.

## You should now be able to

- describe the motor effect, and say when the force is largest and when it is zero;
- use $F = BIl$ to find a force, a current, a length or a flux density;
- use Fleming's left-hand rule to find the direction of the force;
- explain what happens to the force when the current, the field or the length changes;
- say why the length in the equation is the length in the field.
