# 4.3 Conservation of energy — Learn

**Prerequisites:** work, kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy (Sections
4.1 and 4.2).

**Objectives:** state the principle of conservation of energy; use it to convert
gravitational potential energy into kinetic energy; account for energy transferred to
the surroundings.

By the end of this section, you can follow energy from one store to another and say
where the rest has gone.

## Energy is conserved

The **principle of conservation of energy** says that energy cannot be created and
cannot be destroyed. It can be transferred from one store to another, or from one
object to another, but the total amount stays the same.

That gives a powerful method. Instead of tracking forces and accelerations, you write
down the energy at the start and the energy at the end, and set them equal.

$$\text{total energy at the start} = \text{total energy at the end}$$

## From height to speed

A falling object loses gravitational potential energy and gains kinetic energy. If no
energy is transferred to the surroundings, the two are equal:

$$mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

The mass appears on both sides, so it cancels. That gives a useful result for an object
falling through a height $h$:

$$v = \sqrt{2gh}$$

A heavy object and a light object fall with the same speed, because the mass cancels.
This is the energy version of the result from Section 2.4.

**Example 1.** A stone is dropped from rest from a height of 5.0 m. Calculate its speed
just before it lands. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

$$
v = \sqrt{2gh}
$$

$$
v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 5.0}
$$

$$
v = 9.9\,\text{m/s}
$$

**Example 2.** A skateboarder starts from rest at the top of a ramp and reaches the
bottom at 7.0 m/s. Ignoring friction, calculate the height of the ramp.

Rearranging $mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ for $h$:

$$
h = \frac{v^2}{2g}
$$

$$
h = \frac{7.0^2}{2 \times 9.8}
$$

$$
h = 2.5\,\text{m}
$$

## Where the energy goes

In real situations some energy is always transferred to the surroundings. A falling
stone pushes air aside; a sliding box rubs against the floor. That energy warms the
air, the floor and the object. It is called **thermal energy**, and it is still energy:
it has not been destroyed.

So the full statement is

$$\text{energy at the start} = \text{useful energy at the end} + \text{energy transferred to the surroundings}$$

**Example 3.** A ball is dropped from a height of 2.0 m and bounces back up to 1.2 m.
Calculate the percentage of the energy lost in the bounce.

The gravitational potential energy is proportional to the height, so the ball keeps
$\dfrac{1.2}{2.0} = 0.60$ of its energy. It loses 40%, transferred to the floor and the
ball as thermal energy.

> **Watch out.** "Energy lost" does not mean destroyed. It means transferred to the
> surroundings in a form you cannot use. Say where it went, and use the words
> **thermal energy** or **warms the surroundings**. An answer that says the energy
> disappeared has lost the physics and the mark.

## Predict, then test

Here is a prediction you can check with arithmetic.

**Predict.** A ball is dropped from a height of 1.8 m. Before you calculate, write down
whether you expect its landing speed to be nearer 3 m/s, 6 m/s or 12 m/s. Give a reason
for your choice.

**Test.** Use $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ to calculate the speed. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

$$
v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 1.8} = 5.9\,\text{m/s}
$$

The true landing speed, measured in a laboratory, is a little lower, about 5.7 m/s.

**Explain.** Write two sentences saying why the measured speed is lower than the
predicted speed. Name the force that transfers energy to the surroundings, and say what
happens to that energy.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The water in the station falls 300 m to the turbines.

Assuming no losses, use $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ to calculate its speed at the turbines. You
should find about 77 m/s.

In the station the water actually arrives at about 60 m/s. Write down where the missing
kinetic energy has gone, and name the store it is now in. Keep your answer: Section 4.4
uses the same water to work out the power.

## You should now be able to

- state the principle of conservation of energy;
- use $mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ to convert between a height and a speed;
- explain why the mass cancels, so all objects fall with the same speed;
- account for energy transferred to the surroundings as thermal energy.
