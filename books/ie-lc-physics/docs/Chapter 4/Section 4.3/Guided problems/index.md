# 4.3 Conservation of energy — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Calculate the kinetic energy of a 2.0 kg object moving at 3.0 m/s.
2. Calculate the gravitational potential energy of a 5.0 kg object 4.0 m above the
   ground. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 0.5 \times 2.0 \times 3.0^2 = 9.0\,\text{J}$.
2. $E_p = mgh = 5.0 \times 9.8 \times 4.0 = 196\,\text{J}$, so about
   $2.0 \times 10^2\,\text{J}$. Both stores are measured in joules, which is what lets
   you set them equal.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A ball is dropped from rest from a height of 3.2 m. Ignoring air resistance,
calculate its speed just before it lands. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Set the potential energy lost equal to the kinetic energy gained. Use
$v = \sqrt{2gh}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 3.2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 3.2} = \sqrt{62.7} = 7.9\,\text{m/s}$$

The mass does not appear, so the answer is the same for any mass dropped from 3.2 m.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A skateboarder starts from rest at the top of a ramp 1.8 m high. Ignoring
friction, calculate the speed at the bottom. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The ramp height is the drop. Use $mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 1.8}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 1.8} = \sqrt{35.3} = 5.9\,\text{m/s}$$

The shape of the ramp does not matter, only the height dropped. A long shallow ramp and
a short steep one give the same speed at the bottom.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A ball is dropped from a height of 1.5 m and bounces back up to 0.90 m. Calculate
the percentage of the energy lost in the bounce.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The potential energy is proportional to the height, so compare the two heights.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The ball keeps $\dfrac{0.90}{1.5}$ of its energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{0.90}{1.5} = 0.60$$

The ball keeps 60% of its energy, so it loses **40%**. The lost energy warmed the ball
and the floor as thermal energy. It has not been destroyed.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A 0.25 kg ball is dropped from a height of 1.8 m. Ignoring air resistance,
calculate its kinetic energy just before it lands. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You do not need the speed. The kinetic energy gained equals the potential energy lost.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E_k = E_p = mgh = 0.25 \times 9.8 \times 1.8$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_k = mgh = 0.25 \times 9.8 \times 1.8 = 4.4\,\text{J}$$

Working in energy rather than in speed saves a square root. This is the habit that
Section 4.5 turns into a method.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A 60 kg cyclist freewheels down a hill, dropping 5.0 m, and reaches 8.0 m/s at
the bottom. Calculate the energy transferred to the surroundings. Take
$g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the potential energy lost and the kinetic energy gained. The difference went to the
surroundings.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E_p = 60 \times 9.8 \times 5.0$ and $E_k = 0.5 \times 60 \times 8.0^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_p = mgh = 60 \times 9.8 \times 5.0 = 2940\,\text{J}$$

$$E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 0.5 \times 60 \times 8.0^2 = 1920\,\text{J}$$

Energy transferred to the surroundings $= 2940 - 1920 = 1020\,\text{J}$, which is about
$1.0 \times 10^3\,\text{J}$. Friction and air resistance transferred it as thermal
energy.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A ball is dropped from the top of a tower. Explain why its landing speed does not
depend on its mass, when air resistance is ignored.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Write down the potential energy lost and the kinetic energy gained, and set them equal.
What happens to the mass?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$. Divide both sides by $m$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The potential energy lost is $mgh$, which is proportional to the mass. The kinetic
energy gained is $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$. Setting them equal:

$$mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

The mass $m$ appears on both sides, so it cancels:

$$v = \sqrt{2gh}$$

The landing speed depends only on the height and on $g$. A heavy ball and a light ball
released from the same height land with the same speed. This is the energy route to the
result you met in Section 2.4.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 4.2

**7.** A roller coaster car of mass 400 kg is released from rest at the top of a 12 m
drop. Ignoring friction, calculate its speed at the bottom. Take
$g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The kinetic energy at the bottom equals the potential energy at the top.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 12}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 12} = \sqrt{235} = 15\,\text{m/s}$$

To two significant figures the speed is 15 m/s. The 400 kg does not appear. A 40 kg car
on the same drop would reach the same speed.

</details>
</details>
</details>
