# 4.5 Solving problems with the work-energy model — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. State the work-energy model in words.
2. A 1000 kg car travels at 12 m/s. Calculate its kinetic energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The work done by the net force on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy.
   In symbols, $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta E_k$.
2. $E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 0.5 \times 1000 \times 12^2 = 72000\,\text{J}$, which is
   $7.2 \times 10^4\,\text{J}$.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A car of mass 900 kg travels at 15 m/s. The brakes apply a force of 4500 N.
Calculate the braking distance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The brakes remove all the kinetic energy. Set $Fd = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$d = \dfrac{0.5 \times 900 \times 15^2}{4500}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$Fd = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

$$d = \frac{0.5 \times 900 \times 15^2}{4500} = \frac{101250}{4500} = 22.5\,\text{m}$$

The distance is 22.5 m. Doubling the speed would quadruple this distance, because the
kinetic energy depends on $v^2$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A ball of mass 0.30 kg is thrown at 18 m/s and caught. Calculate the work done on
the ball by the catch.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The work done equals the kinetic energy the ball loses. It comes to rest.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 0.5 \times 0.30 \times 18^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = \Delta E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 0.5 \times 0.30 \times 18^2 = 48.6\,\text{J}$$

That is about 49 J. The hands do work on the ball, and the ball's kinetic energy becomes
thermal energy in the hands.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A van's engine delivers 45 kW at a steady 25 m/s. Calculate the total resistive
force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At a steady speed, $P = Fv$. Convert the power to watts first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = \dfrac{45000}{25}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = \frac{P}{v} = \frac{45000}{25} = 1800\,\text{N}$$

At a steady speed the driving force equals the resistive force, so the resistive force
is 1800 N.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A 4.0 kg box slides 6.0 m along a rough floor and slows from 5.0 m/s to 2.0 m/s.
Calculate the friction force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the change in kinetic energy first. That is the work done against friction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Delta E_k = 0.5 \times 4.0 \times (5.0^2 - 2.0^2)$, then $F = \dfrac{\Delta E_k}{6.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\Delta E_k = \frac{1}{2}m(v^2 - u^2) = 0.5 \times 4.0 \times (25 - 4) = 42\,\text{J}$$

$$F_f = \frac{\Delta E_k}{d} = \frac{42}{6.0} = 7.0\,\text{N}$$

The friction force is 7.0 N, opposing the motion. The 42 J became thermal energy in the
floor and the box.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A 75 kg runner accelerates from rest to 8.0 m/s over a distance of 20 m. Using the
work-energy model, calculate the average net force on the runner.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The net force does work $Fd$, and that work equals the kinetic energy gained.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = \dfrac{0.5 \times 75 \times 8.0^2}{20}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$Fd = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

$$F = \frac{0.5 \times 75 \times 8.0^2}{20} = \frac{2400}{20} = 120\,\text{N}$$

The average net force is 120 N. This is the resultant after the driving force and the
resistive forces have been combined, which is why it is called the net force.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A question gives the force on an object, its mass and the distance it moves, and
asks for its final speed. Explain which method you would use and why.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which quantities does the energy method need? Which does it avoid?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The energy method uses force, mass and distance. It never needs the time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Use the **work-energy model**, because the work done by the net force equals the change
in kinetic energy:

$$Fd = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2$$

This route never needs the time or the acceleration, so it avoids two quantities the
question does not give. The equations of motion would also reach the answer, but only
after finding the acceleration from Newton's second law, so the energy route is shorter
and has fewer steps to go wrong.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 4.4

**7.** A pump lifts 250 kg of water 15 m in 30 s. The power input to the pump is 1600 W.
Calculate the useful power output and the efficiency of the pump. Take
$g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The useful work is the work done against gravity. Divide it by the time for the output
power, then divide by the input power.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 250 \times 9.8 \times 15$, then $P_{\text{out}} = \dfrac{W}{30}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mgh = 250 \times 9.8 \times 15 = 36750\,\text{J}$$

$$P_{\text{out}} = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{36750}{30} = 1225\,\text{W}$$

That is about $1.2 \times 10^3\,\text{W}$. The efficiency is

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{1225}{1600} \times 100\% = 77\%$$

The rest of the input, about 23%, was transferred to the surroundings as thermal energy
by friction in the pump and the pipes.

</details>
</details>
</details>
