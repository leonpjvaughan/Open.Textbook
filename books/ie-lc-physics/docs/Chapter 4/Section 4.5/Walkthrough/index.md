# 4.5 Solving problems with the work-energy model — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the whole of Chapter 4, and the equations of motion (Section 2.3).

**Objectives:** use the work-energy model to relate a force and a distance to a change
in kinetic energy; include the work done against friction; choose between the energy
method and the equations of motion; use $P = Fv$.

By the end of this section, you can choose the shortest route through a mechanics
problem and give a reason for the choice.

## The work-energy model

The work done by the **net force** on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy:

$$W_{\text{net}} = \Delta E_k$$

This is the **work-energy model**. It joins ideas you already have: work transfers
energy, and a change in kinetic energy is a change in the store of motion.

The model is most useful when a force acts over a distance. The work is then $Fd$, and
the change in kinetic energy is $\frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2$.

**Example 1.** A car of mass 1200 kg travels at 20 m/s. The brakes apply a force of
6000 N. Calculate the braking distance.

The brakes do work $Fd$ and remove all the kinetic energy:

$$
Fd = \frac{1}{2}mv^2
$$

$$
d = \frac{\frac{1}{2}mv^2}{F}
$$

$$
d = \frac{0.5 \times 1200 \times 20^2}{6000}
$$

$$
d = 40\,\text{m}
$$

No time and no acceleration appears. The energy route reached the answer in one step.

## Work done against friction

Friction opposes the motion, so it removes kinetic energy and transfers it to the
surroundings as thermal energy. The work done against friction is $F_f d$, where $F_f$
is the friction force and $d$ is the distance moved.

You include friction by adding its work to the energy account:

$$\text{energy at the start} = \text{useful energy at the end} + F_f d$$

**Example 2.** A cyclist of total mass 70 kg freewheels down a slope and drops 8.0 m. At
the bottom the cyclist is moving at 10 m/s. Calculate the energy transferred to the
surroundings by friction and air resistance. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

Potential energy lost:

$$
mgh = 70 \times 9.8 \times 8.0 = 5490\,\text{J}
$$

Kinetic energy gained:

$$
\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 0.5 \times 70 \times 10^2 = 3500\,\text{J}
$$

Energy transferred to the surroundings:

$$
5490 - 3500 = 1990\,\text{J}
$$

That is about $2.0 \times 10^3\,\text{J}$.

## Choosing the method

Both the work-energy model and the equations of motion are correct. The question decides
which is quicker.

- Use the **work-energy model** when you know forces and distances and you want a speed.
  It avoids time and acceleration.
- Use the **equations of motion** when you know times and accelerations and you want a
  distance or a speed.
- If a question gives a force, a mass and a distance, the energy route is usually one
  step.

Write down your reason as well as your answer. "Which method, and why?" is a question in
its own right.

## Power and force at a steady speed

At a steady speed the driving force balances the resistive forces. The power delivered
is then

$$P = Fv$$

where $F$ is the driving force and $v$ is the steady speed.

**Example 3.** A car's engine delivers 30 kW while the car travels at a steady 15 m/s.
Calculate the total resistive force.

$$
F = \frac{P}{v}
$$

$$
F = \frac{30000}{15}
$$

$$
F = 2000\,\text{N}
$$

> **Watch out.** Two traps in this section. First, the work-energy model uses the
> **net** force. If you use the driving force alone, you must account for the friction
> work separately, and using both double-counts it. Second, $P = Fv$ gives the force
> only at a **steady** speed, and it needs the speed in metres per second. While a car
> accelerates, the driving force is larger than the resistive force.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The station takes 24 MW from the grid while pumping, and it
delivers 16 MW while generating. Both happen at the same rate of water flow.

Calculate the overall efficiency of the store, as a percentage. You should find about
67%.

Then write three or four sentences explaining why the station is still worth building,
even though it gives back only about two thirds of the energy. Use the words **stored
