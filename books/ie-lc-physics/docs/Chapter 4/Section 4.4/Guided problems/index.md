# 4.4 Power and efficiency — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Calculate the work done lifting a 4.0 kg mass 3.0 m. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.
2. What does it mean to say that a lamp has a power of 60 W?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $W = mgh = 4.0 \times 9.8 \times 3.0 = 117.6\,\text{J}$, so about
   $1.2 \times 10^2\,\text{J}$.
2. The lamp transfers 60 J of energy every second. Power is a rate, so it is an amount
   of energy divided by a time.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A motor does 900 J of work in 3.0 s. Calculate its power.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Power is work divided by time. Check that the time is in seconds.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$P = \dfrac{900}{3.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$P = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{900}{3.0} = 300\,\text{W}$$

The unit works out too: $\text{J}/\text{s} = \text{W}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A crane lifts a 500 kg load 8.0 m in 16 s. Calculate the useful power output.
Take $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the useful work first, then divide by the time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = mgh = 500 \times 9.8 \times 8.0$, then $P = \dfrac{W}{16}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mgh = 500 \times 9.8 \times 8.0 = 39200\,\text{J}$$

$$P = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{39200}{16} = 2450\,\text{W}$$

That is about $2.5 \times 10^3\,\text{W}$, or 2.5 kW. The crane does the same work
whatever the time; a shorter lift means a larger power.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A 1.5 kW motor runs for 40 s. Calculate the energy it transfers.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Convert kilowatts to watts first. Then use $E = Pt$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 1500 \times 40$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = Pt = 1500 \times 40 = 60000\,\text{J}$$

That is $6.0 \times 10^4\,\text{J}$. If you left the power as 1.5 kW, the answer would
be in kilojoules, so keep the units consistent.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A machine is supplied with 4000 J of energy and transfers 3200 J usefully.
Calculate its efficiency.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the useful output by the total input, then multiply by 100%.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{3200}{4000} \times 100\%$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{3200}{4000} \times 100\% = 80\%$$

The machine transfers 80% of the input usefully. The other 20% went to the surroundings
as thermal energy.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** An electric motor has an efficiency of 75% and is supplied with 8000 J of energy.
Calculate the useful energy output.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

75% as a decimal is 0.75. Multiply the input by it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E_{\text{useful}} = 0.75 \times 8000$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_{\text{useful}} = 0.75 \times 8000 = 6000\,\text{J}$$

The remaining 2000 J was transferred to the surroundings. Check it: $6000 + 2000 = 8000$,
which is the input.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A pump lifts 300 kg of water 12 m in 20 s. Calculate the useful power output, and
explain why the power input to the pump must be larger. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The useful work is the work done against gravity. What happens to some of the input
energy?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 300 \times 9.8 \times 12$, then $P = \dfrac{W}{20}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mgh = 300 \times 9.8 \times 12 = 35280\,\text{J}$$

$$P = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{35280}{20} = 1760\,\text{W}$$

That is about $1.8 \times 10^3\,\text{W}$.

The power input must be larger, because friction in the pump and in the pipes transfers
some energy to the surroundings as thermal energy. The efficiency is the useful output
divided by that larger input.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 4.3

**7.** A 0.50 kg ball is dropped from a height of 2.0 m. Ignoring air resistance,
calculate its kinetic energy just before it lands, and the time it takes to fall. Hence
calculate the average rate at which potential energy becomes kinetic energy. Take
$g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The kinetic energy equals the potential energy lost. For the time, use
$s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$ from Chapter 2.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E_k = mgh = 0.50 \times 9.8 \times 2.0$, and
$t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 2.0}{9.8}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_k = mgh = 0.50 \times 9.8 \times 2.0 = 9.8\,\text{J}$$

$$t = \sqrt{\frac{2s}{g}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 2.0}{9.8}} = 0.64\,\text{s}$$

The average rate at which potential energy becomes kinetic energy is

$$P = \frac{E}{t} = \frac{9.8}{0.64} = 15\,\text{W}$$

The rate is not constant: the ball moves fastest at the end, so the energy is
transferred fastest there. The 15 W is an average over the fall.

</details>
</details>
</details>
