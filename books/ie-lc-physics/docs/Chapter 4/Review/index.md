# Chapter 4: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The station that stores energy.** A pumped-storage station stores electricity by
pumping water up to a high reservoir. The upper reservoir is 300 m above the lower one.
When the station generates, $4.0 \times 10^5$ kg of water passes through the turbines
every minute, and the electrical output is 16 MW. When it pumps, the pumps take 24 MW
from the grid and lift the same mass of water back up each minute.

In these formulas $W$ is the work done, $E_p$ the potential energy and $E_k$ the
kinetic energy. Also $m$ is the mass, $g$ the gravitational field strength, $h$ the
height, $v$ the speed, $P$ the power, $E$ the energy transferred and $t$ the time.

**The work done lifting the water.** The pumps lift against the weight of the water,
$mg$. For $1.0 \times 10^5$ kg lifted through 300 m:

$$W = mgh = 1.0 \times 10^5 \times 9.8 \times 300 = 2.9 \times 10^8\,\text{J}$$

**The energy stored.** The water in the upper reservoir has gravitational potential
energy. For the full minute of flow, $4.0 \times 10^5$ kg:

$$E_p = mgh = 4.0 \times 10^5 \times 9.8 \times 300 = 1.2 \times 10^9\,\text{J}$$

**The speed at the turbines.** If no energy were transferred to the surroundings, all of
that potential energy would become kinetic energy:

$$mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \qquad v = \sqrt{2gh}$$

$$v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 300} = 77\,\text{m/s}$$

The mass cancels, so the answer does not depend on how much water flows.

**Where the rest of the energy goes.** In the station the water arrives at about
60 m/s. At that speed its kinetic energy is

$$E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 0.5 \times 4.0 \times 10^5 \times 60^2 = 7.2 \times 10^8\,\text{J}$$

That is less than the $1.2 \times 10^9\,\text{J}$ it lost in height. The difference,
about $4.6 \times 10^8\,\text{J}$, was transferred by friction and air resistance as the
water moved through the pipe. It warmed the water and the pipe, and spread into the
surroundings as thermal energy. The total energy is unchanged: energy is conserved.

**The power.** The rate at which potential energy reaches the turbines is

$$P = \frac{E}{t} = \frac{1.2 \times 10^9}{60} = 2.0 \times 10^7\,\text{W}$$

That is 20 MW.

**The efficiency while generating.** The station delivers 16 MW from an input of
19.6 MW:

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{16}{19.6} \times 100\% = 82\%$$

**The overall efficiency.** While pumping, the station takes 24 MW from the grid and
stores 19.6 MW in the water. The pump efficiency is also about 82%. Over a full cycle of
pumping and generating, the energy out divided by the energy in is

$$\text{overall efficiency} = \frac{16}{24} \times 100\% = 67\%$$

**Why the station is still worth building.** It gives back about two thirds of the
energy it takes in, and it never makes energy. Its value is in the **time**, not in the
amount. It stores energy when **demand** is low and electricity is cheap, and it returns
energy when demand is high. The missing third became **thermal energy** in the pumps,
the pipes and the turbines. A store that keeps two thirds of what it is given is useful
when the alternative is to lose the energy altogether.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies** and
say why. One of them asks you to choose between the work-energy model and the equations
of motion.

1. A force of 80 N moves a trolley 6.0 m in the direction of the force. Calculate the
   work done.
2. A 0.20 kg ball moves at 25 m/s. Calculate its kinetic energy.
3. A stone is dropped from rest from a height of 20 m. Ignoring air resistance,
   calculate its landing speed.
4. A motor does 4500 J of work in 15 s. Calculate its power.
5. A 1200 kg car travelling at 25 m/s brakes to rest over 50 m. Calculate the average
   braking force.
6. A crane lifts a 400 kg load 15 m in 25 s. Calculate the useful power output. Take
   $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.
7. A ball is dropped from a height of 4.0 m and bounces back up to 2.8 m. Calculate the
   percentage of the energy lost in the bounce.
8. A car travels at a steady 30 m/s and the engine delivers 60 kW. Calculate the total
   resistive force.
9. A 2.0 kg stone is thrown straight up at 12 m/s. Calculate the maximum height it
   reaches. State which method you used, and why it is the shorter route.
10. A cyclist of total mass 85 kg freewheels down a 6.0 m drop and reaches 9.0 m/s at
    the bottom. Calculate the energy transferred to the surroundings.

## What you can now do

- calculate the work done by a force, and say when a force does no work;
- calculate kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy, and rearrange both
  formulas;
- use conservation of energy to convert a height into a speed, and explain why the mass
  cancels;
- account for energy transferred to the surroundings as thermal energy;
- calculate power from work and time, and energy from power and time;
- calculate the efficiency of a machine, and explain why it is less than 100%;
- use the work-energy model, include the work done against friction, and choose between
  the energy method and the equations of motion.

## Thinking back

Return to the object you chose at the start of the chapter — a dam, a trampoline, a
phone battery, a bungee rope, or a water tank in a roof.

You can now put numbers on it. Write down where the energy comes from, the store it
passes through, and what it becomes when it is released. Estimate the energy stored in
joules, and the power while it is being transferred. Then answer the question you
started with: where does the rest of the energy go?

If your estimate is rough, write that down too. Saying how rough an estimate is part of
the physics, not an apology for it.
