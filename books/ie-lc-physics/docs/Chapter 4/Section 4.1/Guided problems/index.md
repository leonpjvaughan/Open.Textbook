# 4.1 Work and energy — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the unit of force, and what is the unit of energy?
2. A mass of 2.0 kg hangs on a spring and stretches it. What is the weight of the mass?
   Take $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. Force is measured in newtons, N. Energy and work are measured in joules, J. One joule
   is one newton metre.
2. $W = mg = 2.0 \times 9.8 = 19.6\,\text{N}$, so about 20 N. A mass is not a force.
   Convert it with $W = mg$ before you use it in a force calculation.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A force of 25 N moves a crate 4.0 m in the direction of the force. Calculate the
work done.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You are given the force and the distance. Multiply them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = Fd = 25 \times 4.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = Fd = 25 \times 4.0 = 100\,\text{J}$$

The unit works out too: $\text{N} \times \text{m} = \text{J}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A student lifts a 6.0 kg bag 0.80 m onto a table. Calculate the work done
against gravity. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Lifting means working against the weight, $mg$. Use $W = mgh$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 6.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.80$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mgh = 6.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.80 = 47\,\text{J}$$

To two significant figures the work is $47\,\text{J}$. The bag is lifted slowly, so all
the work goes into raising it, not into speeding it up.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A force does 90 J of work while moving a trolley 3.0 m. Calculate the force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $W = Fd$ for $F$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = \dfrac{90}{3.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = \frac{W}{d} = \frac{90}{3.0} = 30\,\text{N}$$

Check the sense of it: 30 N over 3.0 m is $30 \times 3.0 = 90\,\text{J}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A car's brakes exert a force of 3500 N, and the car travels 25 m while braking.
Calculate the work done by the brakes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The braking force and the movement are in opposite directions. The size of the work is
still $Fd$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 3500 \times 25$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = Fd = 3500 \times 25 = 87500\,\text{J}$$

That is $8.8 \times 10^4\,\text{J}$ to two significant figures. The brakes remove this
energy from the car and transfer it to the brake discs and the air as thermal energy.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A student carries a 5.0 kg bag 12 m across a platform at a steady height. State
the work done against the weight of the bag, and explain your answer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which direction does the weight act in, and which direction does the bag move in?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The weight acts downwards; the bag moves horizontally. The two are perpendicular.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The work done against the weight is **zero**.

The weight acts downwards and the bag moves horizontally, so the force and the movement
are perpendicular. There is no movement in the direction of the weight, so the weight
does no work on the bag.

The student still gets tired, because the muscles in the arm transfer energy and warm
up. That effort is real, but it is not work done on the bag against gravity.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A crane lifts a 250 kg load 12 m. Calculate the work done against gravity. Take
$g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the weight first, then multiply by the height. Or use $W = mgh$ in one step.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 250 \times 9.8 \times 12$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mgh = 250 \times 9.8 \times 12 = 29400\,\text{J}$$

That is $2.9 \times 10^4\,\text{J}$ to two significant figures. The same work would be
done whether the load is lifted slowly or quickly; speed changes the power, not the
work.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 3

**7.** A box of mass 8.0 kg is pulled along a floor at a steady speed by a horizontal
force of 20 N. The box moves 5.0 m.

(a) State the size of the friction force on the box. (b) Calculate the work done by the
pulling force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A steady speed means the forces balance. The pulling force and friction are the only
horizontal forces.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Friction balances the 20 N pull, so friction is 20 N. Then use $W = Fd$ with the pull.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

**(a)** A steady speed means the net force is zero, so the friction force is **20 N**,
equal and opposite to the pull.

**(b)** The work done by the pulling force is

$$W = Fd = 20 \times 5.0 = 100\,\text{J}$$

The kinetic energy of the box does not change, because the speed is steady. So the
100 J from the pull is transferred straight to the surroundings by friction as thermal
energy. Section 4.5 makes this accounting exact.

</details>
</details>
</details>
