# 4.1 Work and energy — Learn

**Prerequisites:** force in newtons, and the idea that a force can move an object.

**Objectives:** define work; use $W = Fd$; use $W = mgh$ when lifting a mass; say when
a force does no work.

By the end of this section, you can calculate the work done by a force and say when no
work is done.

## What work is

Work is done when a force moves an object through a distance. The size of the work
depends on two things: how big the force is, and how far the object moves.

$$W = Fd$$

- $W$ is the work done, in joules (J);
- $F$ is the force, in newtons (N);
- $d$ is the distance moved **in the direction of the force**, in metres (m).

The joule is the unit of both work and energy. One joule is the work done when a force
of one newton moves an object one metre.

$$1\,\text{J} = 1\,\text{N m} = 1\,\text{kg m}^2/\text{s}^2$$

**Example 1.** A box is pushed 3.0 m along a floor by a horizontal force of 12 N.
Calculate the work done.

$$
W = Fd
$$

$$
W = 12 \times 3.0
$$

$$
W = 36\,\text{J}
$$

## Work done against gravity

Lifting an object means doing work against its weight. The weight of a mass $m$ is
$mg$, so the force you lift against is $mg$. If you lift the mass through a height $h$,
the work done is

$$W = mgh$$

**Example 2.** A box of mass 20 kg is lifted 1.5 m onto a shelf. Calculate the work
done against gravity. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

$$
W = mgh
$$

$$
W = 20 \times 9.8 \times 1.5
$$

$$
W = 294\,\text{J}
$$

That is about $2.9 \times 10^2\,\text{J}$.

**Example 3.** A force does 240 J of work while moving a crate 6.0 m in the direction
of the force. Calculate the size of the force.

Rearranging $W = Fd$ for $F$:

$$
F = \frac{W}{d}
$$

$$
F = \frac{240}{6.0}
$$

$$
F = 40\,\text{N}
$$

## When a force does no work

The distance in $W = Fd$ is the distance moved **in the direction of the force**. If the
object moves at right angles to the force, the force does no work on it.

Carry a box across a room at a steady height. The weight of the box acts downwards, and
the box moves horizontally. The two are perpendicular, so the weight does no work on
the box.

> **Watch out.** Carrying a box across a room is tiring, so it can feel like work. Your
> muscles do transfer energy, and they warm up as you go. But the work done **on the
> box** against its weight is zero, because the box does not move upwards or downwards.
> Keep the effort you feel and the work the force does as two separate ideas.

## Work and energy together

Work is a way of transferring energy. When you do work on an object, you give it
energy. When an object does work on you, it gives energy to you. The unit is the same,
the joule, because work and energy are two views of one idea.

The box you lift onto a shelf now stores energy because of its height. Section 4.2
works out how much, and Section 4.3 follows it as the box falls.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The pumped-storage station lifts $1.0 \times 10^5$ kg of
water through 300 m.

Write down the force the pumps lift against, and then the work done. Take
$g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$. You should find a force of about $9.8 \times 10^5\,\text{N}$ and
a work of about $2.9 \times 10^8\,\text{J}$.

Keep your answer. Section 4.2 shows where that energy is stored, and Section 4.4
divides it by the time to find the power.

## You should now be able to

- state what work is, and write its unit;
- calculate work from a force and the distance moved in the direction of the force;
- calculate the work done lifting a mass through a height;
- explain why a force perpendicular to the movement does no work on the object.
