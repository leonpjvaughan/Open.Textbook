# Chapter 18: The photoelectric effect and X-rays

Light carries energy. The wave model describes reflection, refraction and
interference well. In this chapter you meet a second model, the photon, and you find
the experiments that forced it on us.

## The chapter problem

**The sensor that ignores red light.** An automatic door uses a photocell. Its metal
cathode has a work function of 2.0 eV. A red lamp of wavelength 700 nm shines on the
cathode, and the door stays shut, however bright the lamp is. A dim blue lamp of
wavelength 450 nm opens it at once.

By the end of the chapter you can find the energy of a photon from each lamp, show
why only the blue photons can free an electron, find the maximum kinetic energy and
the stopping voltage for the blue light, and explain how the same photon picture sets
the shortest wavelength an X-ray tube can produce.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- explain what a photon is, and use $E = hf$ and $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ to find a
  photon energy;
- describe the photoelectric effect, and the observations that the wave model cannot
  explain;
- define the threshold frequency and the work function, and relate them with
  $\Phi = hf_0$;
- use Einstein's equation $hf = \Phi + \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\text{max}}^2$ to find a work
  function, a maximum kinetic energy or a stopping voltage;
- explain the graph of maximum kinetic energy against frequency, and say why its
  gradient is the Planck constant;
- describe how X-rays are produced, and find the shortest wavelength from the tube
  voltage.

## Where it is used

Automatic doors, burglar alarms, light sensors and solar cells all use the
photoelectric effect. Hospitals and airport security use X-ray tubes, which work by
the same exchange of energy between one electron and one photon.
