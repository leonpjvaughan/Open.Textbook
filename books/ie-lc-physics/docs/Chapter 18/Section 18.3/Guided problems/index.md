# 18.3 Einstein's photoelectric equation — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write down the equation for the kinetic energy of a moving object.
2. Calculate the energy of a photon of frequency $8.0 \times 10^{14}$ Hz. Take
   $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, where $m$ is the mass in kilograms and $v$ is the speed
   in metres per second.
2. $E = hf = 6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 8.0 \times 10^{14} = 5.28 \times 10^{-19}$
   J, which is 3.3 eV. Compare that with the work function in the questions below.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Light of wavelength 500 nm falls on a metal of work function
$3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted
electrons. Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the photon energy first, then subtract the work function.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The photon energy is $3.96 \times 10^{-19}$ J, so $E_k = 3.96 \times 10^{-19} -
3.3 \times 10^{-19}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{5.0
\times 10^{-7}} = 3.96 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$

$$E_k = hf - \Phi = 3.96 \times 10^{-19} - 3.3 \times 10^{-19} = 6.6 \times 10^{-20}\,\text{J}$$

Check the sense of it: the photon energy is a little above the work function, so the
electrons come out slowly.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A photoelectron has a maximum kinetic energy of $1.2 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
Calculate its maximum speed. Take $m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start from $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ and rearrange for $v$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 1.2 \times 10^{-19}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2E_k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.2 \times 10^{-19}}{9.1 \times
10^{-31}}} = 5.1 \times 10^{5}\,\text{m/s}$$

That is about 0.2% of the speed of light, so the non-relativistic kinetic energy
formula is a good model here.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A photoelectron has a maximum kinetic energy of $1.2 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
Calculate the stopping voltage. Take $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The stopping voltage removes all the kinetic energy: $eV_s = E_k$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V_s = \dfrac{1.2 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V_s = \frac{E_k}{e} = \frac{1.2 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 0.75\,\text{V}$$

At this reverse potential difference the current falls to zero, because even the
fastest electrons are turned back before they reach the anode.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Light of frequency $8.0 \times 10^{14}$ Hz releases electrons from a metal
with a maximum kinetic energy of $1.98 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Calculate the work
function of the metal. Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $hf = \Phi + E_k$ for $\Phi$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Phi = 6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 8.0 \times 10^{14} - 1.98 \times 10^{-19}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\Phi = hf - E_k = 6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 8.0 \times 10^{14} - 1.98 \times
10^{-19} = 3.3 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$

That is about 2.1 eV, so this metal sits near the middle of the visible range.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Light of frequency $8.0 \times 10^{14}$ Hz falls on a metal of work function
2.1 eV. Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons, in
electronvolts. Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and
$1\,\text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work in electronvolts throughout. Find the photon energy in eV first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$hf = 5.28 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 3.3$ eV, so $E_k = 3.3 - 2.1$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$hf = 6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 8.0 \times 10^{14} = 5.28 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$

$$\frac{5.28 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.3\,\text{eV}$$

$$E_k = 3.3 - 2.1 = 1.2\,\text{eV}$$

Working in electronvolts saves a conversion at every step, because the photon energy
and the work function are then in the same unit.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A student plots the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons against the
frequency of the light. Describe the graph, and say what the gradient and each
intercept give you.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare $E_k = hf - \Phi$ with $y = mx + c$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The gradient is $h$ and the vertical intercept is $-\Phi$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The graph is a straight line that does not pass through the origin. Its gradient is
the Planck constant $h$, so the line is steep.

The intercept on the vertical axis is $-\Phi$, so its size gives the work function
of the metal. The intercept on the frequency axis is the threshold frequency $f_0$,
because there the maximum kinetic energy is zero and $hf_0 = \Phi$.

Every metal gives a line with the same gradient, because $h$ is a constant. Only the
intercept changes, because the work function depends on the metal.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 4

**7.** A ball of mass 0.20 kg moves at 3.0 m/s. Calculate its kinetic energy. Then
compare it with the kinetic energy of a photoelectron that leaves a metal with
$1.2 \times 10^{-19}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ for the ball.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.20 \times 3.0^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.20 \times 3.0^2 = 0.90\,\text{J}$$

The photoelectron carries $1.2 \times 10^{-19}$ J. The ball carries about
$10^{19}$ times more energy, because it is enormously heavier. This is why the
photoelectric effect is measured one electron at a time, with a stopping voltage,
rather than by weighing anything.

</details>
</details>
</details>
