# Chapter 18: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The sensor that ignores red light.** An automatic door uses a photocell. Its metal
cathode has a work function of 2.0 eV. A red lamp of wavelength 700 nm shines on the
cathode, and the door stays shut, however bright the lamp is. A dim blue lamp of
wavelength 450 nm opens it at once.

**Step 1: the work function in joules.** One electronvolt is $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J,
so

$$\Phi = 2.0 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.2 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$

**Step 2: the photon energy of each lamp.** Use $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, with
$h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.

For the red lamp, $\lambda = 7.0 \times 10^{-7}$ m:

$$E_{\text{red}} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{7.0
\times 10^{-7}} = 2.8 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 1.8\,\text{eV}$$

For the blue lamp, $\lambda = 4.5 \times 10^{-7}$ m:

$$E_{\text{blue}} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{4.5
\times 10^{-7}} = 4.4 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 2.8\,\text{eV}$$

**Step 3: why red fails and blue works.** Each red photon carries 1.8 eV, which is
less than the work function of 2.0 eV. One photon gives all its energy to one
electron, so that electron cannot escape. Making the red lamp brighter adds more
photons, and every one of them is still too weak. The blue photon carries 2.8 eV,
which is more than 2.0 eV, so an electron can escape at once, even in dim light.

**Step 4: the energy the electrons leave with.** For the blue light, Einstein's
equation gives

$$E_k = hf - \Phi = 4.4 \times 10^{-19} - 3.2 \times 10^{-19} = 1.2 \times
10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 0.75\,\text{eV}$$

The stopping voltage is $V_s = \dfrac{E_k}{e} = \dfrac{1.2 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6
\times 10^{-19}} = 0.75$ V.

**Step 5: the same picture in an X-ray tube.** The threshold frequency is
$f_0 = \dfrac{\Phi}{h} = \dfrac{3.2 \times 10^{-19}}{6.6 \times 10^{-34}} = 4.8
\times 10^{14}$ Hz, which is a wavelength of 620 nm. Light of longer wavelength than
that cannot open the door. In an X-ray tube the exchange runs the other way: one
fast electron gives its energy to one photon. A tube at 60 kV gives its electrons
$eV = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 6.0 \times 10^{4} = 9.6 \times 10^{-15}$ J each, so
the shortest wavelength is

$$\lambda_{\text{min}} = \frac{hc}{eV} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0
\times 10^{8}}{9.6 \times 10^{-15}} = 2.1 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{m}$$

One photon, one electron, and conservation of energy: the same idea answers both
parts of the problem.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies**
and say why. Some of them join this chapter to the electron from Chapter 17.

1. Calculate the energy of a photon of frequency $6.0 \times 10^{14}$ Hz. Take
   $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s.
2. Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on a metal of work function $3.3 \times 10^{-19}$
   J. Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons.
3. A metal has a threshold wavelength of 500 nm. Calculate its work function in
   joules and in electronvolts.
4. An X-ray tube runs at 80 kV. Calculate the shortest wavelength of the X-rays it
   produces.
5. An electron is accelerated from rest through 2000 V in an electron gun. Calculate
   its kinetic energy in joules and its speed. Take $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C and
   $m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.
6. The same electron strikes a metal target and gives all its energy to one photon.
   Calculate the shortest wavelength of the X-rays produced. Compare it with the
   answer to question 4 and explain the difference.
7. A photocell is lit with light above its threshold frequency. Describe what
   happens to the current and to the maximum kinetic energy when the intensity is
   doubled.
8. A student plots maximum kinetic energy against frequency for a metal. Describe
   the graph, and say what the gradient and each intercept give you.
9. Explain why the wave model predicts that very bright red light would release
   electrons, and why the experiment shows that it does not.
10. You must find the Planck constant in a school laboratory. Would you use a
    photocell and a set of coloured lamps, or a spring and a set of masses? Say why,
    and name the quantity each experiment actually measures.

## What you can now do

- explain what a photon is, and use $E = hf$ and $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ to find a
  photon energy;
- describe the photoelectric effect, and the observations that the wave model cannot
  explain;
- define the threshold frequency and the work function, and relate them with
  $\Phi = hf_0$;
- use Einstein's equation $hf = \Phi + \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\text{max}}^2$ to find a work
  function, a maximum kinetic energy or a stopping voltage;
- read a graph of maximum kinetic energy against frequency, and say what its
  gradient and intercepts mean;
- describe how X-rays are produced, and find the shortest wavelength from the tube
  voltage;
- compare X-ray production with the photoelectric effect and say how they are
  related.

## Thinking back

Look at the device you chose at the start of the chapter — an automatic door, a phone
camera, a street lamp, a solar panel, a burglar alarm.

You can now say something quantitative about it. Find or estimate the wavelength of
the light it uses, and work out the energy of one photon in electronvolts. Then
answer the question you started with: how does the light change what the device does?

If your estimate is rough, write that down too. Saying how rough an estimate is
belongs in the physics, and it is exactly what the Physics In Practice Investigation
asks you to do.
