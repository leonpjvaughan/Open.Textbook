# 18.1 The photon and the quantum idea — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A wave has a wavelength of 0.50 m and travels at 3.0 m/s. Calculate its frequency.
2. How many joules are there in 2.0 eV? Take $1\,\text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $v = f\lambda$, so $f = \dfrac{v}{\lambda} = \dfrac{3.0}{0.50} = 6.0$ Hz. This is
   the wave equation from Chapter 9, and you use the same link between frequency and
   wavelength for a photon.
2. $2.0 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.2 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Photon energies are
   often quoted in electronvolts, because the numbers are then small.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Calculate the energy of a photon of frequency $5.0 \times 10^{14}$ Hz. Take
$h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E = hf$. You are given $h$ and $f$, and asked for $E$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 5.0 \times 10^{14}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = hf = 6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 5.0 \times 10^{14} = 3.3 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$

The unit works out too: $\text{J s} \times \text{Hz} = \text{J s} \times
\text{s}^{-1} = \text{J}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Calculate the energy of a photon of wavelength 500 nm. Take
$h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

First change the wavelength to metres, then use $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$500\,\text{nm} = 5.0 \times 10^{-7}$ m, so $E = \dfrac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times
3.0 \times 10^{8}}{5.0 \times 10^{-7}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{5.0
\times 10^{-7}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$

Check the sense of it: this is a green photon, and its energy sits between the red
and blue photons in the chapter problem.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A photon has an energy of $4.0 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Calculate its frequency.
Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $E = hf$ for $f$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$f = \dfrac{E}{h} = \dfrac{4.0 \times 10^{-19}}{6.6 \times 10^{-34}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$f = \frac{E}{h} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-19}}{6.6 \times 10^{-34}} = 6.1 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$$

That frequency is in the visible range, so the photon could come from a lamp.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A lamp gives out light of wavelength 500 nm at a power of
$2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ W. Each photon carries $4.0 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Calculate the
number of photons the lamp emits each second.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Power is energy each second. Divide the energy each second by the energy of one
photon.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$N = \dfrac{2.0 \times 10^{-3}}{4.0 \times 10^{-19}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$N = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-3}}{4.0 \times 10^{-19}} = 5.0 \times 10^{15}\,\text{photons each second}$$

A very large number, which is why the light looks steady. The energy still arrives
in whole photons.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A photon has an energy of 2.1 eV. Calculate its energy in joules. Take
$1\,\text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply the number of electronvolts by the number of joules in one electronvolt.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$2.1 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$2.1 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.4 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$

To two significant figures the answer is $3.4 \times 10^{-19}$ J. The exact value
is $3.36 \times 10^{-19}$ J.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain what a photon is, and why the wave model of light could not explain
the photoelectric effect on its own.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What does the wave model predict for brighter light? What does the experiment show?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A photon is a packet of energy, and its energy is $E = hf$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A photon is a packet, or quantum, of light energy. The energy of one photon is
$E = hf$, so it depends on the frequency of the light.

The wave model spreads the light energy over the whole wavefront. It therefore
predicts that brighter light of any colour would release electrons, and that very
dim light would take time to release any. The experiment shows that the frequency
decides whether electrons are released at all, and that emission starts at once.
A photon giving all its energy to one electron explains both observations.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 9

**7.** A radio station broadcasts at a frequency of 100 MHz. Calculate the
wavelength of the radio waves, and then the energy of one photon. Take
$c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s and $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $c = f\lambda$ for the wavelength, then $E = hf$ for the photon.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$f = 1.0 \times 10^{8}$ Hz, so $\lambda = \dfrac{3.0 \times 10^{8}}{1.0 \times
10^{8}} = 3.0$ m.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{8}}{1.0 \times 10^{8}} = 3.0\,\text{m}$$

$$E = hf = 6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 1.0 \times 10^{8} = 6.6 \times 10^{-26}\,\text{J}$$

This photon carries far less energy than a visible photon, because its frequency is
far lower. That is why a radio wave passes through a room without releasing
electrons from anything in it.

</details>
</details>
</details>
