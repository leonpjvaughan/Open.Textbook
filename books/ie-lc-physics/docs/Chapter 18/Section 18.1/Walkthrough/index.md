# 18.1 The photon and the quantum idea — Learn

**Prerequisites:** waves, wavelength and frequency from Chapter 9; energy and the
electronvolt from Chapter 4; the electron from Chapter 17.

**Objectives:** explain what a photon is; use $E = hf$ and $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$
to find the energy of a photon; find the number of photons a source emits each
second.

By the end of this section, you can find the energy of a single photon from its
frequency or its wavelength, and you can say why the wave model was not enough.

## Light as a wave, and where the wave model runs out

Light behaves as a wave in many experiments. It reflects, it refracts, and it forms
interference patterns. Those patterns let you measure its wavelength, and the wave
model predicts them well.

One experiment refuses to fit the wave model. Shine light on a clean metal surface
and electrons can come off it. That is the **photoelectric effect**, and Section 18.2
describes it. The puzzling part is this: the colour of the light decides whether any
electron comes off, and the brightness does not. The wave model predicts the
opposite, because a brighter wave carries more energy.

## The quantum idea

In 1900 Max Planck was working on the light given out by hot objects. He found that
he could explain the measurements only if he treated the energy of the light as
coming in fixed packets. He called each packet a quantum, and its energy is
proportional to the frequency of the light.

Albert Einstein took the idea further in 1905. He said that light itself is made of
these packets, and that a packet can give all of its energy to one electron. Each
packet is now called a **photon**.

> **The photon model.** Light is made of photons. Each photon carries energy
> $E = hf$, where $f$ is the frequency of the light and $h$ is the Planck constant.

$$E = hf$$

- $E$ is the energy of one photon, in joules (J);
- $h$ is the **Planck constant**, $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s;
- $f$ is the frequency of the light, in hertz (Hz).

Because $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$, the same energy can be written in terms of the
wavelength:

$$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

- $c$ is the speed of light in a vacuum, $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s;
- $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the light, in metres (m).

**Example 1.** Calculate the energy of a photon of frequency $6.0 \times 10^{14}$ Hz.

$$
E = hf
$$

$$
E = 6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 6.0 \times 10^{14}
$$

$$
E = 4.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}
$$

**Example 2.** Calculate the energy of a photon of wavelength 600 nm.

The wavelength is $600\,\text{nm} = 6.0 \times 10^{-7}$ m. Then

$$
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

$$
E = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{6.0 \times 10^{-7}}
$$

$$
E = 3.3 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}
$$

**Example 3.** A lamp gives out light of wavelength 600 nm at a power of
$3.0 \times 10^{-3}$ W. Calculate the number of photons it emits each second.

Each photon carries $3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J, from Example 2. The lamp gives out
$3.0 \times 10^{-3}$ J each second, so

$$
N = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{-3}}{3.3 \times 10^{-19}}
$$

Here $N$ is the number of photons the lamp emits each second.

$$
N = 9.1 \times 10^{15}\,\text{photons each second}
$$

That is a very large number, and it is why a lamp looks continuous to your eye. The
energy still arrives in whole photons.

> **Watch out.** The energy of a photon depends on the **frequency**, not on how
> bright the light is. A brighter lamp sends more photons each second, but every
> photon carries the same energy as before. A longer wavelength means a lower
> frequency, so a red photon carries less energy than a blue one. The unit of $h$ is
> the joule second, so $hf$ comes out in joules.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The red lamp has a wavelength of 700 nm and the blue
lamp has a wavelength of 450 nm.

Write down the equation $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, then calculate the energy of one
photon from each lamp. Keep both answers in joules and in electronvolts, because
Section 18.2 compares them with the work function of the metal.

## You should now be able to

- explain what a photon is, and why the wave model could not explain the
  photoelectric effect;
- use $E = hf$ to find the energy of a photon from its frequency;
- use $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ to find the energy of a photon from its wavelength;
- convert a photon energy between joules and electronvolts;
- find the number of photons a source emits each second from its power.
