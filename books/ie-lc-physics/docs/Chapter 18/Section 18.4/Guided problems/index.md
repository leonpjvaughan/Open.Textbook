# 18.4 X-ray production — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is thermionic emission?
2. An electron is accelerated through a potential difference of 100 V. What energy
   does it gain, in electronvolts?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. Thermionic emission is the release of electrons from the surface of a hot metal.
   The electrons need at least the work function to escape, and heating gives them
   that energy. The filament of an X-ray tube works in the same way.
2. 100 eV. One electronvolt is the energy gained by an electron accelerated through
   one volt, so the number of electronvolts equals the voltage in volts. To turn it
   into joules, multiply by $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** An X-ray tube runs at 40 kV. Calculate the shortest wavelength of the X-rays
it produces. Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s and
$e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The electron energy is $eV$, and all of it can go to one photon: $eV =
\dfrac{hc}{\lambda_{\text{min}}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$40\,\text{kV} = 4.0 \times 10^{4}$ V, so $\lambda_{\text{min}} = \dfrac{6.6 \times
10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.0 \times 10^{4}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda_{\text{min}} = \frac{hc}{eV} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times
10^{8}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.0 \times 10^{4}} = 3.1 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{m}$$

This is about a tenth of the diameter of an atom, which is why X-rays pass through
soft tissue.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** An X-ray tube runs at 40 kV. Calculate the highest frequency of the X-rays it
produces. Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The highest frequency goes with the shortest wavelength, so $hf_{\text{max}} = eV$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$f_{\text{max}} = \dfrac{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.0 \times 10^{4}}{6.6 \times
10^{-34}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$f_{\text{max}} = \frac{eV}{h} = \frac{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.0 \times
10^{4}}{6.6 \times 10^{-34}} = 9.7 \times 10^{18}\,\text{Hz}$$

Check the sense of it: $c = f\lambda$, so the highest frequency belongs with the
shortest wavelength.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** An X-ray tube must produce X-rays with a shortest wavelength of
$2.0 \times 10^{-11}$ m. Calculate the tube voltage needed. Take
$h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s and
$e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $eV = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda_{\text{min}}}$ for $V$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V = \dfrac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}
\times 2.0 \times 10^{-11}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V = \frac{hc}{e\lambda_{\text{min}}} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times
10^{8}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-11}} = 6.2 \times 10^{4}\,\text{V}$$

That is 62 kV. A shorter wavelength always needs a higher voltage.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** An X-ray photon has a wavelength of $1.0 \times 10^{-10}$ m. Calculate its
energy. Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = \dfrac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{1.0 \times 10^{-10}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{1.0
\times 10^{-10}} = 2.0 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$$

That is about ten thousand times the energy of a visible photon, which is why
X-rays can pass through soft tissue while visible light cannot.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** In an X-ray tube, electrons are accelerated through 40 kV. Calculate the
kinetic energy each electron gains. Take $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The work done on each electron is $eV$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.0 \times 10^{4}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = eV = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.0 \times 10^{4} = 6.4 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{J}$$

That is 40 keV. This is the energy one electron can hand to one photon, so it is the
energy of the photon at the shortest wavelength.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Describe how X-rays are produced in an X-ray tube.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start at the filament, follow the electron to the target, and say what leaves.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The electron gains energy $eV$ and loses it at the target.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A current heats a filament, and thermionic emission releases electrons from it. A
high potential difference between the filament and the target accelerates the
electrons, so each one gains kinetic energy $eV$.

The electrons strike a heavy metal target and slow down very quickly. The energy
they lose leaves the tube as X-ray photons. The tube is evacuated, so the electrons
are not scattered by air on the way to the target.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 17

**7.** An electron is accelerated from rest through 20 kV. Calculate its speed, then
find the shortest wavelength of the X-rays it could produce when it hits a target.
Take $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C, $m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg,
$h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $eV = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ for the speed, then $eV = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda_{\text{min}}}$
for the wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{4}}{9.1 \times
10^{-31}}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0
\times 10^{4}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}}} = 8.4 \times 10^{7}\,\text{m/s}$$

Then, if the electron gives all its energy to one photon,

$$\lambda_{\text{min}} = \frac{hc}{eV} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times
10^{8}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{4}} = 6.2 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{m}$$

The speed belongs to the electron from Chapter 17. The wavelength belongs to the
X-ray tube, and it is set by the same energy $eV$. Notice that the speed is not
needed to find the wavelength, because the energy already carries the information.

</details>
</details>
</details>
