# 18.2 The photoelectric effect — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the photon and $E = hf$ from Section 18.1; the electron from
Chapter 17.

**Objectives:** describe the photoelectric effect and the observations that the wave
model cannot explain; define the threshold frequency and the work function; relate
them with $\Phi = hf_0$.

By the end of this section, you can explain why the colour of the light, and not its
brightness, decides whether electrons are released from a metal.

## Electrons released by light

A clean metal surface can release electrons when light falls on it. The electrons
that come off are called **photoelectrons**, and the effect is the **photoelectric
effect**.

The effect was a puzzle, because the measurements did not match the wave model of
light. Four observations matter.

1. **There is a threshold frequency.** Below a certain frequency, no electrons are
   released at all, however intense the light is. Above it, electrons are released
   even in very dim light.
2. **The maximum kinetic energy depends on the frequency.** Light of a higher
   frequency gives photoelectrons with more kinetic energy.
3. **The intensity changes the number, not the energy.** Brighter light of the same
   frequency releases more electrons each second, but the maximum kinetic energy of
   each electron stays the same.
4. **Emission starts at once.** Even in very dim light, the electrons appear without
   any measurable delay.

## Why the wave model fails

The wave model says that light carries energy spread out over the wavefront. On that
picture, a more intense wave carries more energy, so brighter light should release
electrons of higher energy, and enough dim light should eventually release electrons
too.

The experiment says otherwise. A very bright red lamp releases nothing, and a very
dim blue lamp releases electrons at once. The colour of the light is the thing that
matters, and the wave model has no place for a threshold set by colour.

## The photon explanation

The photon model explains all four observations with one idea: **one photon gives all
of its energy to one electron**. The electron uses some of that energy to escape from
the metal, and keeps the rest as kinetic energy.

- If the photon carries less energy than the electron needs to escape, nothing
  happens. More photons of the same colour cannot help, because each one is still too
  weak. That is the threshold.
- If the photon carries more, the electron escapes at once and keeps the surplus as
  kinetic energy. A higher frequency means a more energetic photon, so the electron
  leaves faster.
- A brighter beam has more photons, so it releases more electrons each second, while
  each photon still carries the same energy.

## The threshold frequency and the work function

The least energy an electron needs to escape from a metal surface is the **work
function** of that metal, written $\Phi$. It is measured in joules, or in
electronvolts for convenience.

The frequency at which a photon carries exactly the work function is the **threshold
frequency**, $f_0$. At that frequency the electron escapes with no kinetic energy
left. Below it, no electron escapes at all.

$$\Phi = hf_0$$

Because $f_0 = \dfrac{c}{\lambda_0}$, the work function also fixes a **threshold
wavelength**, $\lambda_0$. Light of longer wavelength than that cannot release
electrons from the metal.

**Example 1.** A metal has a work function of $3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Calculate its
threshold frequency.

Rearranging $\Phi = hf_0$ for $f_0$:

$$
f_0 = \frac{\Phi}{h}
$$

$$
f_0 = \frac{3.3 \times 10^{-19}}{6.6 \times 10^{-34}}
$$

$$
f_0 = 5.0 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}
$$

**Example 2.** A metal has a threshold wavelength of 600 nm. Calculate its work
function.

$$
\Phi = \frac{hc}{\lambda_0}
$$

$$
\Phi = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{6.0 \times 10^{-7}}
$$

$$
\Phi = 3.3 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}
$$

That is about 2.1 eV.

**Example 3.** Red light of wavelength 700 nm falls on the same metal. Show whether
electrons are released.

$$
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{7.0
\times 10^{-7}} = 2.8 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}
$$

The photon energy is $2.8 \times 10^{-19}$ J and the work function is
$3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Each photon carries less than the electron needs to escape,
so no electrons are released. Making the red light brighter adds more photons, and
every one of them is still too weak.

> **Watch out.** A brighter light of the same colour does **not** give the
> photoelectrons more energy. It releases more of them each second. The energy of
> each photoelectron is set by the frequency of the light. Also keep the work
> function and the threshold frequency apart: the work function is an energy, in
> joules, and the threshold is a frequency, in hertz.
