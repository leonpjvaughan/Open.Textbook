# 18.2 The photoelectric effect — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the charge on one electron, in coulombs?
2. Calculate the energy of a photon of wavelength 600 nm. Take
   $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C, and it is negative. The magnitude is written $e$.
2. $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times
   10^{8}}{6.0 \times 10^{-7}} = 3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J. This is the energy a photon
   must carry to reach the threshold in the questions below.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A metal has a work function of $3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Calculate its
threshold frequency. Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At the threshold, $hf_0 = \Phi$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$f_0 = \dfrac{3.3 \times 10^{-19}}{6.6 \times 10^{-34}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$f_0 = \frac{\Phi}{h} = \frac{3.3 \times 10^{-19}}{6.6 \times 10^{-34}} = 5.0 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$$

Light of this frequency or higher releases electrons. Light of lower frequency does
not, however bright it is.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A metal has a threshold wavelength of 600 nm. Calculate its work function.
Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At the threshold the photon energy equals the work function, and the wavelength is
600 nm $= 6.0 \times 10^{-7}$ m.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Phi = \dfrac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{6.0 \times 10^{-7}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\Phi = \frac{hc}{\lambda_0} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times
10^{8}}{6.0 \times 10^{-7}} = 3.3 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$

That is about 2.1 eV, so a visible photon can free an electron from this metal.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A work function is $3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Calculate it in electronvolts.
Take $1\,\text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the energy in joules by the number of joules in one electronvolt.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{3.3 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{3.3 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 2.1\,\text{eV}$$

Work functions are quoted in electronvolts because the numbers are then small. A
photon of 2.1 eV is in the blue part of the visible spectrum.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A metal has a work function of $3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Calculate its
threshold wavelength. Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and
$c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $\Phi = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda_0}$ and rearrange for $\lambda_0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\lambda_0 = \dfrac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{3.3 \times
10^{-19}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda_0 = \frac{hc}{\Phi} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times
10^{8}}{3.3 \times 10^{-19}} = 6.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$$

That is 600 nm, in the orange part of the spectrum. Light of longer wavelength than
600 nm cannot release electrons from this metal.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Red light of wavelength 700 nm falls on a metal of work function
$3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Calculate the energy of one photon and say whether
electrons are released.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the photon energy, then compare it with the work function.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = \dfrac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{7.0 \times 10^{-7}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{7.0
\times 10^{-7}} = 2.8 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$

This is less than the work function of $3.3 \times 10^{-19}$ J, so no electron can
escape. No emission occurs, and making the light brighter does not change that.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Describe the four observations of the photoelectric effect, and explain why
the wave model of light cannot account for them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the threshold, the energy of the electrons, the intensity and the delay.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The photon model says one photon gives all its energy to one electron.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

There is a threshold frequency: below it, no electrons are released, however
intense the light. Above it, the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons rises with
the frequency. Increasing the intensity increases the number of electrons each
second but leaves their maximum kinetic energy unchanged. Emission also starts at
once, even in dim light.

The wave model spreads energy over the wavefront, so it predicts that brighter light
of any colour would release electrons, and that dim light would need time to build up
enough energy. It has no threshold set by colour. The photon model explains all four
observations, because one photon gives all its energy to one electron.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 17

**7.** An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 200 V.
Calculate the kinetic energy it gains, in joules and in electronvolts. Take
$e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The work done on the electron is $eV$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 200$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = eV = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 200 = 3.2 \times 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$$

In electronvolts the answer is 200 eV, because one electronvolt is the energy gained
by an electron accelerated through one volt.

Compare this with the photoelectric effect. Here the field gives energy to a free
electron. In the photoelectric effect a photon gives its energy to a bound electron
in a metal, and the work function must be paid before the electron can leave.

</details>
</details>
</details>
