# 8.1 Temperature, heat and thermal equilibrium — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A 3.0 kg mass is lifted 2.0 m. Calculate the work done against gravity. Take
   $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.
2. State the unit of energy and the unit of power.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $W = mgh = 3.0 \times 9.8 \times 2.0 = 59\,\text{J}$.
2. The unit of energy is the joule (J), and the unit of power is the watt (W).

Both ideas return here. Energy is measured in joules, and the joule is also the
unit of heat. Power is the rate at which energy is transferred.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A room is at 30°C. Write this temperature in kelvin. Take 0°C = 273 K.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $T = \theta + 273$. Add 273 to the Celsius value.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$T = 30 + 273$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$T = \theta + 273 = 30 + 273 = 303\,\text{K}$$

A change of 1°C is the same size as a change of 1 K, so only the starting point
differs between the two scales.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A gas is at 400 K. Write this temperature in degrees Celsius. Take
0°C = 273 K.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $T = \theta + 273$ to make $\theta$ the subject.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\theta = 400 - 273$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\theta = T - 273 = 400 - 273 = 127$$

so the temperature is 127°C.

The gas is well above the boiling point of water, which is 100°C.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Explain the difference between temperature and heat.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about what each quantity measures, and about the unit of each.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Temperature describes the particles of one body on average. Heat is energy that
moves between bodies.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Temperature is a measure of the average random kinetic energy of the particles
of a body. It is measured in °C or K.

Heat is energy in transit from a hotter body to a colder body because of the
temperature difference. It is measured in joules.

A body has a temperature, but heat is something that happens between bodies.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A cup of coffee at 70°C is left in a room at 20°C. State the direction of
the energy transfer and the temperature the coffee finally reaches.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Energy flows from the hotter body to the colder body.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The coffee is hotter than the room, so the coffee loses energy. It stops losing
energy when the two reach the same temperature.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Energy transfers from the coffee at 70°C to the room at 20°C, because energy
flows from a hotter body to a colder body.

The coffee cools until it reaches the temperature of the room, which is 20°C. At
that point the coffee and the room are in thermal equilibrium, so no net energy
flows between them.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A block at 80°C is placed in contact with a block at 30°C inside an
insulated container. Describe the energy transfer and the final state.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which block is hotter? Which way does the energy flow?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The hot block cools and the cold block warms. They stop changing when they reach
the same temperature.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Energy flows from the hot block at 80°C to the cold block at 30°C. The hot block
cools and the cold block warms.

The two blocks reach the same temperature, somewhere between 30°C and 80°C. They
are then in thermal equilibrium, so no net energy flows between them. The
container is insulated, so almost no energy leaves the two blocks.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** The temperature outside is −50°C. Write this temperature in kelvin. Take
0°C = 273 K.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add 273 to the Celsius value, even when the value is negative.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$T = -50 + 273$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$T = \theta + 273 = -50 + 273 = 223\,\text{K}$$

The value is positive, because −50°C is still above absolute zero at −273°C.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** A thermometer is placed in a beaker of warm water. Explain why the reading
stops changing after a short time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the thermometer and the water reaching the same temperature.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Energy flows from the water to the thermometer until the two are at the same
temperature.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Energy transfers from the warm water to the cooler thermometer, so the liquid
inside the thermometer expands and the reading rises.

The reading stops changing when the thermometer reaches the temperature of the
water. At that point the thermometer and the water are in thermal equilibrium,
so no net energy flows between them.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 4

**8.** A 500 W heater is switched on for 2.0 minutes. Calculate the energy
supplied.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Power is the rate of energy transfer, so use $E = Pt$. Convert the time to
seconds first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$t = 2.0 \times 60 = 120\,\text{s}$, then $E = 500 \times 120$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = Pt = 500 \times 120 = 6.0 \times 10^{4}\,\text{J}$$

The heater supplies $6.0 \times 10^{4}$ J. In this chapter that energy would warm
a known mass of water through a known temperature change.

</details>
</details>
</details>
