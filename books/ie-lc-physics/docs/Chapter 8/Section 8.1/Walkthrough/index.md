# 8.1 Temperature, heat and thermal equilibrium — Learn

**Prerequisites:** energy and the joule from Chapter 4; the idea that matter is
made of particles.

**Objectives:** define temperature and heat; describe thermal equilibrium;
convert between the Celsius and kelvin scales; state the direction of energy
transfer between two bodies.

By the end of this section, you can describe how hot a body is, and explain how
and why energy moves between bodies.

## Temperature and its scales

Temperature tells you how hot or cold a body is. You measure it with a
thermometer, and you report it on a scale. Two scales are in common use.

- The **Celsius** scale uses degrees Celsius (°C). Water freezes at 0°C and boils
  at 100°C at normal air pressure.
- The **kelvin** scale uses kelvin (K). It is the SI scale. It starts at
  **absolute zero**, the lowest temperature possible.

The two scales have the same size of degree, so a change of 1°C is a change of
1 K. They differ only in where they start. The conversion is

$$T = \theta + 273$$

- $T$ is the temperature in kelvin (K);
- $\theta$ is the temperature in degrees Celsius (°C).

Absolute zero is 0 K, which is −273°C. Nothing can be colder than absolute zero.

**Example 1.** A room is at 22°C. Write this temperature in kelvin.

$$
T = 22 + 273 = 295\,\text{K}
$$

**Example 2.** Liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K. Write this temperature in degrees
Celsius.

$$
\theta = 77 - 273 = -196
$$

so the temperature is −196°C.

## Temperature and heat are different

The particles of every body are moving. Temperature is a measure of the
**average** random kinetic energy of those particles. It says how fast the
particles move on average, not how much energy the body stores in total.

**Heat** is energy in transit. It is energy that moves from a hotter body to a
colder body because of the temperature difference. Heat is measured in joules
(J), the same unit as every other form of energy.

The total kinetic and potential energy of all the particles of a body is its
**internal energy**. A large body at a low temperature can store more internal
energy than a small body at a high temperature, because it has many more
particles.

> **Watch out.** Temperature and heat are not the same quantity. Temperature is
> measured in °C or K, and it describes one particle on average. Heat is measured
> in J, and it is energy on the move. A bath of warm water at 40°C has more
> internal energy than a spark at 1000°C, even though the spark is much hotter.
> **Re-check:** ask whether the question is about how hot something is, or about
> how much energy is transferred.

## Thermal equilibrium

When a hot body touches a cold body, energy flows from the hot body to the cold
body. The hot body cools and the cold body warms. After some time the two bodies
reach the same temperature. Energy still moves between them, but equal amounts
move each way, so there is no net transfer. The bodies are then in **thermal
equilibrium**.

Energy always flows from the hotter body to the colder body. It never flows the
other way on its own.

**Example 3.** A thermometer at 20°C is placed in a beaker of water at 60°C.
Describe what happens.

The water is hotter than the thermometer, so energy flows from the water to the
thermometer. The reading rises. It stops rising when the thermometer reaches
60°C. At that point the thermometer and the water are in thermal equilibrium.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The flask holds 1.0 kg of water at 20°C, and the
ice is at 0°C.

1. State the direction in which energy flows between the water and the ice.
2. State what thermal equilibrium will mean for the water and the melted ice.
3. Predict whether all the ice will melt. Give one reason.

Keep your answers. Section 8.2 works out the energy the warm water can give up,

## You should now be able to

- define temperature and state the two scales used to measure it;
- convert a temperature between the Celsius and kelvin scales;
- explain that temperature measures the average random kinetic energy of the
  particles;
- explain that heat is energy in transit from a hotter body to a colder body;
- describe thermal equilibrium and state the direction of energy flow;
- explain why a large cool body can store more internal energy than a small hot
  body.
