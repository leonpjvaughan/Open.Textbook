# 8.2 Specific heat capacity — Learn

**Prerequisites:** temperature, heat and thermal equilibrium from Section 8.1;
energy, work and power from Chapter 4.

**Objectives:** define specific heat capacity; use $E = mc\Delta\theta$; find a
specific heat capacity from a heating experiment; compare materials by their
specific heat capacity.

By the end of this section, you can work out the energy needed to change the
temperature of a body.

## The energy that changes a temperature

When you heat a body, its temperature rises. The energy needed depends on three
things:

- the mass of the body;
- the material the body is made of;
- the size of the temperature change.

The **specific heat capacity** of a substance is the energy needed to raise the
temperature of 1 kg of it by 1 K. It is measured in joules per kilogram per
kelvin, J/(kg K).

$$E = mc\Delta\theta$$

- $E$ is the energy transferred, in joules (J);
- $m$ is the mass, in kilograms (kg);
- $c$ is the specific heat capacity, in J/(kg K);
- $\Delta\theta$ is the temperature change, in K or °C.

The temperature change is the difference between the final and the initial
temperature. A change of 1 K is the same size as a change of 1°C, so you can use
either scale for $\Delta\theta$.

Water has a large specific heat capacity, 4200 J/(kg K). That value means 4200 J
raises 1 kg of water by 1 K. Metals have much smaller values, so they warm faster.

**Example 1.** Calculate the energy needed to heat 2.0 kg of water from 15°C to
65°C.

$$
E = mc\Delta\theta
$$

$$
E = 2.0 \times 4200 \times (65 - 15)
$$

$$
E = 2.0 \times 4200 \times 50 = 4.2 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}
$$

**Example 2.** A 3.0 kg block gains $7.8 \times 10^{4}$ J of energy, and its
temperature rises by 40 K. Calculate its specific heat capacity.

$$
c = \frac{E}{m\Delta\theta}
$$

$$
c = \frac{7.8 \times 10^{4}}{3.0 \times 40} = 650\,\text{J/(kg K)}
$$

**Example 3.** A 700 W heater warms 2.0 kg of water for 300 s. Calculate the
temperature rise of the water.

The energy supplied by the heater is
$E = Pt = 700 \times 300 = 2.1 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}$. Then

$$
\Delta\theta = \frac{E}{mc} = \frac{2.1 \times 10^{5}}{2.0 \times 4200} = 25\,\text{K}
$$

**Example 4.** A 0.50 kg aluminium block is heated from 20°C to 120°C. The
specific heat capacity of aluminium is 900 J/(kg K). Calculate the energy needed.

$$
E = mc\Delta\theta = 0.50 \times 900 \times 100 = 4.5 \times 10^{4}\,\text{J}
$$

> **Watch out.** Two traps appear in almost every question. First,
> $\Delta\theta$ is the **change** in temperature, not the final temperature.
> Heating water from 20°C to 80°C gives $\Delta\theta = 60$ K, not 80 K. Second,
> the mass must be in kilograms, so a mass given in grams must be divided by
> 1000 first. Using 250 instead of 0.25 for a 250 g block makes the energy 1000
> times too large.
> **Re-check:** subtract the initial temperature from the final temperature, and
> write every mass in kilograms, before you substitute.

## Measuring a specific heat capacity

A simple experiment uses an electrical heater and a metal block. A heater of
power $P$ works for a time $t$, and the temperature rise is recorded.

- The energy supplied is $E = Pt$.
- The temperature rise is $\Delta\theta$.
- The mass of the block is $m$.

Then

$$c = \frac{E}{m\Delta\theta}$$

Some energy warms the room, and the block loses energy to its surroundings. The
measured value is therefore a little too large. Insulating the block reduces this
loss.

**Example 5.** A 12 V, 2.0 A heater warms a 0.50 kg block for 240 s. The
temperature rises by 30 K. Calculate the specific heat capacity of the block.

The energy supplied is $E = IVt = 2.0 \times 12 \times 240 = 5760\,\text{J}$.
Then

$$
c = \frac{E}{m\Delta\theta} = \frac{5760}{0.50 \times 30} = 384\,\text{J/(kg K)}
$$

The value is close to the accepted value for copper, 390 J/(kg K).
