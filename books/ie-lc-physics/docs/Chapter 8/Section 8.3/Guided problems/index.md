# 8.3 Latent heat and changes of state — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Calculate the energy needed to heat 0.50 kg of water from 20°C to 60°C. The
   specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg K).
2. State what is meant by a change of state.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $E = mc\Delta\theta = 0.50 \times 4200 \times 40 = 8.4 \times 10^{4}\,\text{J}$.
2. A change of state happens when a substance turns from one state to another,
   for example from a solid to a liquid.

Both ideas return here. The first is the energy that changes a temperature. The
second is the process that this section describes with $E = mL$.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Calculate the energy needed to melt 0.50 kg of ice at 0°C. The specific
latent heat of fusion of ice is $3.34 \times 10^{5}$ J/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E = mL$. The ice is already at its melting point, so the temperature does
not change.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 0.50 \times 3.34 \times 10^{5}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = mL = 0.50 \times 3.34 \times 10^{5} = 1.67 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}$$

To two significant figures that is $1.7 \times 10^{5}$ J. The ice stays at 0°C
while it melts.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Calculate the energy needed to turn 0.20 kg of water at 100°C into steam
at 100°C. The specific latent heat of vaporisation of water is
$2.26 \times 10^{6}$ J/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E = mL$ with the latent heat of vaporisation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 0.20 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = mL = 0.20 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6} = 4.52 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}$$

To two significant figures that is $4.5 \times 10^{5}$ J. The water stays at
100°C while it boils.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A 0.30 kg mass of a solid needs $9.0 \times 10^{4}$ J to melt it at its
melting point. Calculate the specific latent heat of fusion of the solid.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $E = mL$ to make $L$ the subject.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$L = \dfrac{E}{m} = \dfrac{9.0 \times 10^{4}}{0.30}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$L = \frac{E}{m} = \frac{9.0 \times 10^{4}}{0.30} = 3.0 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J/kg}$$

The value is close to the latent heat of fusion of ice, which is
$3.34 \times 10^{5}$ J/kg.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A 0.20 kg mass of water is heated from 20°C to 100°C, and then all of it
is boiled away. Calculate the total energy supplied. Use
$c = 4200\,\text{J/(kg K)}$ and $L = 2.26 \times 10^{6}\,\text{J/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out the two stages separately, then add the amounts.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E_1 = 0.20 \times 4200 \times 80$, then $E_2 = 0.20 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Heating the water:

$$E_1 = mc\Delta\theta = 0.20 \times 4200 \times 80 = 6.72 \times 10^{4}\,\text{J}$$

Boiling the water:

$$E_2 = mL = 0.20 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6} = 4.52 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}$$

Total:

$$E = E_1 + E_2 = 6.72 \times 10^{4} + 4.52 \times 10^{5} = 5.2 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}$$

Boiling the water needs much more energy than heating it from 20°C to 100°C.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Energy is supplied to ice at 0°C, and the ice melts. Explain why the
temperature stays at 0°C while the ice melts.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What is the energy doing to the particles if it is not speeding them up?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The energy breaks the bonds that hold the particles in the solid.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The energy supplied changes the state of the ice. It breaks the bonds that hold
the particles in the solid, so the potential energy of the particles rises.

The average kinetic energy of the particles does not change, so the temperature
stays at 0°C. The internal energy of the ice rises even though its temperature
does not.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain the difference between the specific latent heat of fusion and the
specific latent heat of vaporisation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which change of state does each value describe?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Fusion is for melting and freezing. Vaporisation is for boiling and condensing.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The specific latent heat of fusion is the energy needed to change 1 kg of a
substance between its solid and liquid states, at its melting point. It is used
for melting and freezing.

The specific latent heat of vaporisation is the energy needed to change 1 kg of a
substance between its liquid and gas states, at its boiling point. It is used for
boiling and condensing.

For water the value for vaporisation is larger, because boiling needs more energy
than melting.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** A heating curve for water has a flat part at 0°C and a longer flat part at
100°C. Explain why the second flat part is longer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The length of a plateau shows the latent heat. Compare the two latent heats of
water.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The latent heat of vaporisation is $2.26 \times 10^{6}$ J/kg, and the latent heat
of fusion is $3.34 \times 10^{5}$ J/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The heater supplies energy at a steady rate, so the time for a stage is
proportional to the energy needed. A longer plateau means more energy.

The latent heat of vaporisation of water is about seven times the latent heat of
fusion. Boiling the same mass therefore needs about seven times as much energy as
melting it, so the boiling plateau is much longer than the melting plateau.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 8.2

**8.** Calculate the energy needed to heat 0.20 kg of water from 0°C to 20°C. The
specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg K).

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

This is a temperature change with no change of state, so use
$E = mc\Delta\theta$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Delta\theta = 20 - 0 = 20\,\text{K}$, then $E = 0.20 \times 4200 \times 20$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = mc\Delta\theta = 0.20 \times 4200 \times 20 = 1.68 \times 10^{4}\,\text{J}$$

To two significant figures that is $1.7 \times 10^{4}$ J. The same mass would
need $6.7 \times 10^{4}$ J to melt, which is about four times as much energy.

</details>
</details>
</details>
