# 8.3 Latent heat and changes of state — Learn

**Prerequisites:** specific heat capacity from Section 8.2; the idea that matter
is made of particles.

**Objectives:** describe the changes of state; explain why the temperature stays
constant during a change of state; define specific latent heat; use $E = mL$;
read a heating curve.

By the end of this section, you can work out the energy needed to melt or boil a
substance, and explain why its temperature does not change.

## Changes of state

Matter can exist as a solid, a liquid or a gas. A **change of state** happens
when a substance turns from one state to another.

- melting: solid to liquid;
- freezing: liquid to solid;
- boiling or evaporating: liquid to gas;
- condensing: gas to liquid.

During a change of state the temperature does not change. The energy supplied is
used to break the bonds between the particles, so the potential energy of the
particles rises. Their average kinetic energy stays the same, so the temperature
stays the same.

## Specific latent heat

The **specific latent heat** of a substance is the energy needed to change the
state of 1 kg of it, with no change in temperature. It is measured in joules per
kilogram, J/kg.

$$E = mL$$

- $E$ is the energy transferred, in joules (J);
- $m$ is the mass, in kilograms (kg);
- $L$ is the specific latent heat, in J/kg.

There are two values to know for each substance:

- the **specific latent heat of fusion**, for melting and freezing;
- the **specific latent heat of vaporisation**, for boiling and condensing.

For water, the latent heat of fusion is $3.34 \times 10^{5}$ J/kg and the latent
heat of vaporisation is $2.26 \times 10^{6}$ J/kg. Boiling needs about seven
times as much energy as melting for the same mass.

**Example 1.** Calculate the energy needed to melt 0.20 kg of ice at 0°C.

$$
E = mL = 0.20 \times 3.34 \times 10^{5} = 6.7 \times 10^{4}\,\text{J}
$$

**Example 2.** Calculate the energy needed to turn 0.050 kg of water at 100°C
into steam at 100°C.

$$
E = mL = 0.050 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6} = 1.1 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}
$$

## Warming and changing state together

Many processes warm a substance and then change its state. Work out each stage
separately, then add the amounts.

**Example 3.** A 0.50 kg mass of water is heated from 20°C to 100°C, and then
0.10 kg of it is boiled away. Calculate the total energy supplied.

Heating the water:

$$
E_1 = mc\Delta\theta = 0.50 \times 4200 \times 80 = 1.68 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}
$$

where $c$ is the specific heat capacity of water and $\Delta\theta$ the temperature
change.

Boiling 0.10 kg:

$$
E_2 = mL = 0.10 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6} = 2.26 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}
$$

Total:

$$
E = E_1 + E_2 = 1.68 \times 10^{5} + 2.26 \times 10^{5} = 3.9 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}
$$

## The heating curve

A graph of temperature against time for a substance heated at a steady rate is a
**heating curve**. It has sloping parts where the substance warms up, and flat
parts where it changes state.

The flat parts are called plateaus. During a plateau the heater still supplies
energy at a steady rate, but the temperature does not change. The energy is
changing the arrangement of the particles, not their average speed.

The length of a plateau shows the latent heat. For water, the boiling plateau is
much longer than the melting plateau, because the latent heat of vaporisation is
larger.

> **Watch out.** A flat part does not mean that the energy has stopped arriving.
> The heater still supplies energy, and the internal energy of the substance
> still rises. The temperature is constant because the energy breaks bonds
> between the particles. If you treat a plateau as no energy, every later
> calculation is too small. If you use $E = mc\Delta\theta$ during a plateau, you
> also get the wrong answer, because the temperature is not changing.
> **Re-check:** ask whether the substance is changing state at that moment. If it
> is, use $E = mL$.
