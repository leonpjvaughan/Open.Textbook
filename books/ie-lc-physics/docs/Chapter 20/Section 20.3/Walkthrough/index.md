# 20.3 Nuclear equations — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the nuclear model and nuclide notation from Chapter 19; alpha,
beta and gamma radiation from Section 20.1.

**Objectives:** write a nucleus in nuclide notation; state the two quantities
conserved in a nuclear decay; write and balance the nuclear equation for alpha, beta
and gamma decay; find a missing particle in a decay equation.

By the end of this section you can take any parent nucleus and any emission, and
write the equation for the decay.

## Nuclide notation

A nucleus is written with its mass number as a superscript and its atomic number as
a subscript.

$$^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$$

- $A$ is the **mass number**: the number of protons plus the number of neutrons;
- $Z$ is the **atomic number**: the number of protons, which fixes the element;
- $\text{X}$ is the chemical symbol of the element.

For carbon-14, $A = 14$ and $Z = 6$, so it is $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$. The number of
neutrons is $A - Z = 8$.

## What is conserved

A nuclear decay rearranges nucleons; it does not destroy them. Two totals are the
same before and after.

> **Conserved in every nuclear decay.** The total mass number and the total atomic
> number are each the same on both sides of the equation.

Those two rules are enough to balance any decay equation.

- The mass number tells you where the nucleons went. An alpha particle carries four
  of them away; a beta particle carries none.
- The atomic number tells you how the charge changed. An alpha particle removes two
  units of positive charge; a beta particle adds one unit, because a neutron has
  become a proton.

Notice what is *not* conserved: the number of neutrons, the number of protons, and
the element. Those all change in a decay, and that is the point of it.

## Alpha decay

An alpha particle is a helium nucleus, $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$. Emitting one lowers the
mass number by 4 and the atomic number by 2.

$$^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \rightarrow {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}\text{Y} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

The daughter is a different element, two places to the left in the periodic table.

**Example 1.** Radon-222 decays by alpha emission. Write the nuclear equation and
name the daughter.

Radon-222 is $^{222}_{86}\text{Rn}$. Subtract 4 from the mass number and 2 from the
atomic number:

$$^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} \rightarrow {}^{218}_{84}\text{Po} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

The daughter is polonium-218. Check the totals: $222 = 218 + 4$ and $86 = 84 + 2$.

## Beta decay

In beta decay a neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton and an electron. The
electron leaves the nucleus at high speed. The mass number does not change, because
the total number of nucleons is the same; the atomic number rises by one, because
there is now one more proton.

$$^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \rightarrow {}^{A}_{Z+1}\text{Y} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$$

The daughter is the next element to the right in the periodic table. An almost
massless particle called an **antineutrino** is released as well, and it carries away
some of the energy. It has no charge and no mass number, so it does not appear in the
two totals you balance.

**Example 2.** Phosphorus-32 decays by beta emission. Write the nuclear equation and
name the daughter.

Phosphorus-32 is $^{32}_{15}\text{P}$. The mass number stays at 32 and the atomic
number rises to 16:

$$^{32}_{15}\text{P} \rightarrow {}^{32}_{16}\text{S} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$$

The daughter is sulfur-32. Check the totals: $32 = 32 + 0$ and $15 = 16 + (-1)$.

**Example 3.** Carbon-14 decays by beta emission. Write the nuclear equation.

Carbon-14 is $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$, so the daughter has mass number 14 and atomic
number 7:

$$^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$$

The daughter is nitrogen-14. This is the decay that dates the bog oak in the chapter
problem: every carbon-14 nucleus that disappears becomes a nitrogen-14 nucleus.

## Gamma emission

A gamma ray is electromagnetic radiation with no charge and no mass. Emitting one
changes neither $A$ nor $Z$.

$$^{A}_{Z}\text{X}^{*} \rightarrow {}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} + \gamma$$

The asterisk marks an excited nucleus. Gamma emission usually follows an alpha or a
beta decay, when the daughter is left with extra energy. The nucleus settles down
without changing its identity.

## Finding a missing particle

If the parent and the daughter are both known, the conservation rules give the
missing particle.

**Example 4.** In the decay $^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + X$,
identify the particle $X$.

Balance the mass number:

$$226 - 222 = 4$$

Balance the atomic number:

$$88 - 86 = 2$$

So $X$ has mass number 4 and atomic number 2. That is an alpha particle,
$^{4}_{2}\text{He}$.

**Example 5.** A phosphorus-32 nucleus decays to sulfur-32. Identify the particle
emitted.

The mass number is unchanged, because $32 - 32 = 0$. The atomic number rises from
15 to 16, so the change is $16 - 15 = +1$. A particle with mass number 0 and charge
$+1$ relative to the daughter is a beta particle, $^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$. The equation
is the one in Example 2.

> **Watch out.** In beta decay the atomic number *rises*, so the emitted electron
> carries a charge of $-1$. A common error is to subtract one instead of adding it,
> which produces the wrong element. Balance the totals on both sides and the sign
> looks after itself: $15 = 16 + (-1)$.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The oak from the trackway is dated with carbon-14,
which decays by beta emission.

Write the full nuclear equation for the decay, including the antineutrino. Then
state the atomic number of the daughter and name the element. Keep the equation:
the Review uses it to explain what has happened to the carbon in the wood over the
last few thousand years.

## You should now be able to

- write a nucleus in nuclide notation and say what the mass number and the atomic
  number mean;
- state that the total mass number and the total atomic number are conserved in a
  nuclear decay;
- write the nuclear equation for an alpha decay, a beta decay and a gamma emission;
- name the daughter nucleus of a decay from its atomic number;
- find a missing particle in a decay equation by balancing the two totals.
