# 20.3 Nuclear equations — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What does the atomic number of a nucleus tell you?
2. How many neutrons are in a nucleus of $^{32}_{15}\text{P}$?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The number of protons in the nucleus. It fixes the element, so every nucleus with
   atomic number 15 is phosphorus.
2. $32 - 15 = 17$ neutrons. The mass number is the protons and neutrons together,
   so subtract the protons to find the neutrons.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** Uranium-238 decays by alpha emission. Write the nuclear equation, and name
the daughter nucleus.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

An alpha particle is $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$. Subtract 4 from the mass number and 2 from
the atomic number.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Uranium-238 is $^{238}_{92}\text{U}$. The daughter has mass number $238 - 4 = 234$
and atomic number $92 - 2 = 90$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$^{238}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

The daughter is thorium-234. Check the totals: $238 = 234 + 4$ and $92 = 90 + 2$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Carbon-14 decays by beta emission. Write the nuclear equation, and name the
daughter nucleus.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

In beta decay a neutron becomes a proton. What happens to the mass number, and what
happens to the atomic number?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The mass number stays at 14. The atomic number rises from 6 to 7.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$$

The daughter is nitrogen-14. Check the totals: $14 = 14 + 0$ and $6 = 7 + (-1)$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A nucleus of technetium-99m releases a gamma ray and becomes technetium-99.
State what happens to its mass number and its atomic number, and explain why.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What charge and what mass does a gamma ray carry?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A gamma ray has no charge and no mass, so neither total changes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Neither the mass number nor the atomic number changes.

A gamma ray is electromagnetic radiation with no charge and no mass. The nucleus
loses energy only, so it keeps the same number of protons and the same number of
nucleons. The equation is

$$^{99\text{m}}_{43}\text{Tc} \rightarrow {}^{99}_{43}\text{Tc} + \gamma$$

The symbol m marks the excited state; the nucleus is the same element on both sides.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** In the decay $^{210}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow {}^{206}_{82}\text{Pb} + X$,
identify the particle $X$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Balance the mass number first, then the atomic number.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Mass number: $210 - 206 = 4$. Atomic number: $84 - 82 = 2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$X$ has mass number 4 and atomic number 2, so it is an alpha particle,
$^{4}_{2}\text{He}$.

The full equation is

$$^{210}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow {}^{206}_{82}\text{Pb} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** State the two quantities that are conserved in every nuclear decay, and
explain how they are used to balance an equation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One of them counts nucleons and the other counts protons.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The total mass number and the total atomic number.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The total mass number is conserved, and the total atomic number is conserved.

To balance an equation, write the parent nucleus and the particle it emits, then
subtract to find the daughter. Add up each side afterwards and check that both
totals match. The mass number total fixes where the nucleons went, and the atomic
number total fixes how the charge changed.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Define isotopes, and explain why carbon-14 is an isotope of carbon.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What stays the same between isotopes, and what is different?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Same number of protons, different number of neutrons.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Isotopes are nuclei of the same element, so they have the same number of protons but
different numbers of neutrons.

Every carbon nucleus has six protons, so every isotope of carbon has atomic number

6. Carbon-14 has mass number 14, so it has $14 - 6 = 8$ neutrons, while carbon-12
has six. The extra neutrons make carbon-14 unstable, which is why it decays.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 19

**7.** In Rutherford's experiment, most alpha particles passed straight through a
thin gold foil. Explain what this showed about the structure of the atom.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

An alpha particle is positive. What would it do if it met a large region of
positive charge?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Most particles passed through undeflected, so most of the atom is empty space.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil with little or no deflection.
An alpha particle is positive, so it is repelled by positive charge. Passing through
undeflected shows that most of the atom is empty space, with the positive charge and
almost all the mass concentrated in a very small central nucleus.

A few particles were deflected through large angles, which is what the concentrated
positive charge of the nucleus would do.

</details>
</details>
</details>
