# 20.1 Radioactive decay and the three emissions — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the nuclear model of the atom and isotopes from Chapter 19; the
electric field between charged plates from Chapter 13; the force on a moving charge
from Chapter 15.

**Objectives:** describe radioactive decay as a spontaneous and random change in an
unstable nucleus; explain why background radiation must be subtracted from a
reading; compare alpha, beta and gamma radiation for charge, mass, ionising effect
and penetrating power; describe how each one moves in an electric field and in a
magnetic field; state the role of the strong and weak nuclear forces.

By the end of this section you can look at a reading from a detector, subtract the
background, and say which emission produced it.

## What makes a nucleus unstable

A nucleus is held together by the strong nuclear force. The protons in it also push
each other apart, because they all carry positive charge. In a stable nucleus the
strong force wins, and the balance of protons and neutrons keeps it that way.

Two things can upset that balance.

- **The wrong number of neutrons.** Isotopes of an element have the same number of
  protons but different numbers of neutrons. Some of those nuclei are stable and
  some are not. Carbon-12 is stable, and carbon-14 is not.
- **Too many nucleons altogether.** In a very large nucleus the strong force cannot
  reach every nucleon, because it acts only over a very short distance. Every
  nucleus heavier than lead is unstable.

An unstable nucleus changes into a different nucleus. The change is called
**radioactive decay**, and the radiation it gives out is called **ionising
radiation**, because it knocks electrons off the atoms it passes.

## Spontaneous and random

Radioactive decay has two properties that no other process in this book shares.

> **Spontaneous.** The decay comes from the unstable nucleus itself. Nothing
> outside the nucleus causes it.

> **Random.** You cannot predict which nucleus will decay next, or when it will
> decay.

The randomness has a surprising consequence. You cannot say when one particular
nucleus will decay, but in a sample of many millions the same small fraction decays
in every equal stretch of time. That is why a source has a steady, measurable
activity even though every individual event is unpredictable. Section 20.2 turns
that fraction into the half-life.

Because the change happens inside the nucleus, the conditions outside it make no
difference. Heating a source to white heat, cooling it in liquid nitrogen,
dissolving it in acid or squeezing it under a press all leave the activity
unchanged. The outer electrons are disturbed by all of those things; the nucleus is
not.

**Example 1.** A sample of a radioactive isotope is heated strongly in a furnace.
State and explain what happens to its activity.

The activity does not change. Heating adds energy to the atoms and disturbs the
outer electrons, but the unstable nucleus is unaffected. The decay is spontaneous,
so it carries on at the same rate.

## Background radiation

A detector in a laboratory records counts even when no source is near it. That
reading is **background radiation**: the ionising radiation that is present all the
time, from sources other than the one you are investigating.

Most of it is natural.

- **Radon gas** seeps out of rocks and soil that contain uranium. In Ireland this is
  the largest single source of background radiation.
- **Cosmic rays** arrive from space. The count rate is higher at the top of a
  mountain than at sea level, because less air has absorbed them.
- **Food, drink and building materials** contain small amounts of radioactive
  isotopes, including carbon-14 and potassium-40.
- **Medical procedures**, such as X-rays, add a small amount.

Background radiation is always present, so every measurement of a source includes
it. To find the count rate that comes from the source alone, measure the background
with the source removed and subtract it. The result is the **corrected count rate**.

**Example 2.** A detector records 750 counts per minute near a source. With the
source removed, the same detector records 130 counts per minute. Calculate the
corrected count rate of the source.

$$
\text{corrected count rate} = 750 - 130 = 620\ \text{counts per minute}
$$

That is $6.2 \times 10^2$ counts per minute to two significant figures.

> **Watch out.** Do not subtract the background from the *activity* of a source. The
> background is a count rate, so it is subtracted from a count rate. Activity and
> count rate are not the same thing: a detector catches only a fraction of the
> decays, so the count rate is usually smaller than the activity.

## The three emissions

An unstable nucleus can settle down in three different ways.

| Emission | What it is | Charge | Mass | Ionising effect | Penetrating power |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha, $\alpha$ | a helium nucleus, $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ | $+2e$ | 4 u | very strong | stopped by paper, or a few cm of air |
| Beta, $\beta$ | a fast electron, $^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ | $-e$ | about $1/1800$ u | moderate | stopped by a few mm of aluminium |
| Gamma, $\gamma$ | electromagnetic radiation | 0 | 0 | weak | reduced by several cm of lead |

Here $e$ is the size of the charge on one electron, $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C, and u
is the atomic mass unit.

The ionising effect and the penetrating power always go in opposite directions.
Alpha particles are large, doubly charged and slow, so they collide with many atoms
and lose all their energy in a short distance. Gamma rays have no charge at all, so
they pass through most matter and give up their energy only occasionally. Beta
particles sit between the two.

That trade-off decides which emission suits which job. A smoke detector wants an
alpha source, because the particles stop in a few centimetres of air and never
reach the people in the room. A medical tracer wants a gamma source, because the
rays must leave the body to be detected.

## Deflection in electric and magnetic fields

A charged particle moving through an electric field feels a force. The field
between two charged plates runs from the positive plate to the negative plate, so

- a **positive** particle is pushed along the field, towards the negative plate;
- a **negative** particle is pushed against the field, towards the positive plate;
- a **neutral** particle feels no force at all.

Alpha particles are positive, so an alpha beam bends towards the negative plate.
Beta particles are negative, so a beta beam bends the other way. A gamma beam is
not deflected, which is the direct evidence that a gamma ray carries no charge.

The size of the bend depends on the mass as well as the charge. The beta particle
is about 7000 times lighter than the alpha particle, so for the same force it
accelerates far more and its beam bends much further. A small bend towards the
negative plate means alpha; a large bend towards the positive plate means beta.

In a magnetic field the force on a moving charge is given by $F = Bqv$, with the
direction from the force rule you met in Chapter 15. A positive charge is deflected
one way and a negative charge the other way, so a magnetic field separates the
three emissions just as an electric field does.

**Example 3.** A beam from an unknown source passes between two charged plates. The
beam bends towards the positive plate. Which emission is it, and what does the
direction show?

The beam bends towards the positive plate, so it is attracted to positive charge
and must be negative. The emission is beta radiation. A gamma ray would not bend at
all, and an alpha particle would bend towards the negative plate instead.

## The nuclear forces

Two of the four fundamental forces act inside the nucleus.

The **strong nuclear force** holds protons and neutrons together. It is the
strongest force in nature, but it acts only over a distance about the size of a
nucleus. Inside that range it overcomes the electrostatic repulsion between
the protons. In a nucleus as large as uranium, however, the protons at one side are
too far from the nucleons at the other side for the strong force to reach them all,
and the nucleus becomes unstable.

The **weak nuclear force** acts on a single nucleon. It can change a neutron into a
proton, and when it does, an electron is thrown out of the nucleus. That process is
**beta decay**, so beta decay is caused by the weak force. Alpha decay is a
consequence of the strong force losing its grip on a very large nucleus.
