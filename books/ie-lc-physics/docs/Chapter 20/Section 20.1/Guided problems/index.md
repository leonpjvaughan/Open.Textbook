# 20.1 Radioactive decay and the three emissions — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A neutral atom of carbon-14 has 6 protons. How many neutrons and how many
   electrons does it have?
2. What is the charge on a single electron?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $14 - 6 = 8$ neutrons, and 6 electrons. A neutral atom has as many electrons as
   protons. The trap is to read the mass number as the number of neutrons: the mass
   number is the protons and neutrons together.
2. $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C. The size of the charge is $e$; the minus sign is what
   makes the electron negative.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A detector records 540 counts per minute near a source, and 60 counts per
minute with the source removed. Calculate the corrected count rate of the source.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which of the two readings is the background? That is the one to subtract.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$540 - 60$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$540 - 60 = 480\ \text{counts per minute}$$

The background is the reading with the source removed, so it is the part of the
first reading that did not come from the source. Subtracting it leaves the counts
that did.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A source gives out radiation that passes through a sheet of paper but is
stopped by 3 mm of aluminium. Identify the emission and explain your reasoning.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the three penetrating powers. Which emission is stopped by paper, and which
one passes through aluminium as well?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Alpha particles are stopped by paper. Gamma rays pass through both paper and
aluminium. The emission here is neither of those.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The emission is beta radiation.

Alpha particles would have been stopped by the paper, so it is not alpha. Gamma
rays would have passed through the aluminium as well, so it is not gamma. Beta
particles pass through paper and are stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium,
which matches the observation.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A narrow beam from an unknown source passes between two charged plates and
bends towards the negative plate. Identify the emission.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The field between the plates runs from the positive plate to the negative plate.
Which way is a positive charge pushed?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A positive charge is pushed along the field, so it moves towards the negative
plate.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The beam bends towards the negative plate, so it is attracted to negative charge
and carries a positive charge. The emission is alpha radiation.

A beta beam would bend towards the positive plate instead, and a gamma beam would
not bend at all.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A source is cooled in liquid nitrogen. Explain why its activity does not
change.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Where in the atom does the decay happen?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The decay happens in the nucleus. Cooling affects the outer electrons and the
motion of the atoms.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Radioactive decay is spontaneous: it comes from the unstable nucleus itself, and
nothing outside the nucleus causes it. Cooling changes the outer electrons and
slows the motion of the atoms, but it does not reach inside the nucleus. The
activity stays the same.

The same reasoning explains why heating, squeezing or dissolving a source makes no
difference either.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A student says that gamma rays are the most dangerous emission because they
penetrate furthest. Give one reason why this is not the whole story.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the ionising effect, and about what happens when an alpha source gets
inside the body.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Gamma rays ionise weakly. Alpha particles ionise very strongly, but the skin stops
them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Penetrating power is only one half of the picture. A gamma ray passes through the
body and deposits only a small part of its energy, because it ionises weakly. An
alpha particle that is breathed in or swallowed has nothing between it and living
tissue, and it ionises very strongly, so all its energy is deposited in a small mass
of tissue.

That is why an alpha source is safe to hold but must never be taken into the body.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A detector near a source records 800 counts per minute. When the source is
removed, the same detector records 100 counts per minute. Explain what the 100
counts per minute is, and give two sources of it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The reading with the source removed is the one that comes from everywhere else.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

It is the background radiation. Name two of its natural sources.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The 100 counts per minute is the background radiation: the ionising radiation that
is present all the time, from sources other than the one being investigated.

Two of its sources are radon gas released by rocks and soil, and cosmic rays from
space. Food, drink and building materials also contribute.

The corrected count rate of the source is $800 - 100 = 700$ counts per minute.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 15

**7.** An alpha particle moves at $2.0 \times 10^6$ m/s at right angles to a
uniform magnetic field of flux density $3.0 \times 10^{-2}$ T. Calculate the
magnetic force on it. Take the charge on an alpha particle as $3.2 \times 10^{-19}$
C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The force on a moving charge in a magnetic field is $F = Bqv$, where $v$ is
perpendicular to $B$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = Bqv = 3.0 \times 10^{-2} \times 3.2 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^6$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = Bqv = 3.0 \times 10^{-2} \times 3.2 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^6 = 1.9 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{N}$$

This is the force that bends the beam. It is perpendicular to the velocity, so it
changes the direction of the alpha particle without changing its speed. That is how
a magnetic field sorts alpha, beta and gamma radiation into separate beams.

</details>
</details>
</details>
