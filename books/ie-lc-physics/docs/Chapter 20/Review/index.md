# Chapter 20: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The bog oak trackway.** A sample of oak from the trackway gives a count rate of
4 counts per minute. Living wood of the same mass gives 16 counts per minute. The
half-life of carbon-14 is 5730 years.

**Step 1: how many half-lives.** The activity halves with every half-life, so

$$16 \to 8 \to 4$$

The count rate has halved twice. **Two half-lives** have passed.

**Step 2: the age.**

$$2 \times 5730 = 11\,460 \text{ years}$$

The trackway is about **11 500 years old**.

**Step 3: what carbon-14 emits.** Carbon-14 decays by beta emission, because it has too
many neutrons for its protons. A neutron turns into a proton and an electron, and the
electron leaves the nucleus:

$${}^{14}_{6}\text{C} \to {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}e$$

The atomic number rises by one, from 6 to 7, and the mass number stays at 14. The
nitrogen stays in the wood; the electron is the beta particle that a detector counts.

**Step 4: why it stops working.** Each half-life divides the activity by two, so after
$n$ half-lives the activity is $\dfrac{16}{2^n}$ counts per minute. After 10 half-lives
it is $0.0156$ counts per minute, which is far below the background count of a real
detector. The carbon-14 is still there — it has not run out — but the count can no longer
be told apart from background radiation. That is why carbon dating is reliable to about
50 000 years and not beyond.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **what is being asked** before you
calculate: a number of half-lives, an activity, an age, or a nuclear equation.

1. A source has a half-life of 8 days and an initial activity of 640 Bq. Find its
   activity after 32 days.
2. A sample's activity falls from 200 Bq to 25 Bq in 45 minutes. Find the half-life.
3. A wooden artefact gives 12 counts per minute; living wood gives 48. The half-life of
   carbon-14 is 5730 years. Find the age.
4. Write the nuclear equation for the alpha decay of radon-222.
5. A nucleus emits a beta particle. State what happens to its atomic number and its mass
   number, and why.
6. Explain why heating a radioactive source to 500 °C does not change its activity.
7. A radioactive source has a half-life of 5.3 years. What fraction of it remains after
   21.2 years?
8. The background count in a laboratory is 20 counts per minute. A source gives a total
   count of 180 counts per minute. Find the corrected count rate of the source.
9. A student says a source has "run out" after 10 half-lives. Explain why that is not
   what happens.
10. A smoke detector contains americium-241, which emits alpha particles. State one
    reason an alpha emitter suits this use and one hazard it creates.

## What you can now do

- describe radioactive decay as spontaneous and random, and say what those two words
  mean;
- name the three emissions and compare their charge, mass, ionising effect and
  penetrating power;
- use half-life to find an activity, a fraction remaining or a time elapsed;
- correct a count rate for background radiation;
- write a nuclear equation for alpha and beta decay, balancing mass number and atomic
  number;
- give one use and one hazard of each kind of radiation, and say why the hazard depends
  on the penetrating power.

## Thinking back

Look at the quantity you chose at the start of the chapter — a smoke detector, a
radiocarbon date, a medical tracer, a nuclear power station.

You can now say something quantitative about it. Write down its half-life, and work out
what fraction of it remains after ten half-lives. Then answer the question you started
with: does the source last forever, or does it become too weak to detect — and which of
those two is the real limit on how it is used?

If your answer depends on a number you are not sure of, write down your estimate and say
how rough it is. Judging whether a source is usable is part of the physics, not a
separate question.
