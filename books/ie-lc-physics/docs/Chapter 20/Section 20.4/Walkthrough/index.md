# 20.4 Uses and hazards of radiation — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the three emissions and their penetrating powers from Section
20.1; half-life and activity from Section 20.2.

**Objectives:** explain how the emission and the half-life are chosen for a use;
describe medical, industrial and dating uses of radiation; explain how ionising
radiation damages cells; describe how a worker reduces the dose received; explain
why a long half-life makes waste a long-term problem.

By the end of this section you can look at a job, say which emission and which
half-life suit it, and say how the people involved are protected.

## Choosing the emission and the half-life

Every use of radiation is a choice of two things.

- **The emission** decides what the radiation can pass through. An alpha particle
  stops in a few centimetres of air, a beta particle in a few millimetres of
  aluminium, and a gamma ray passes through the body and through several centimetres
  of lead.
- **The half-life** decides how long the source stays useful, and how long it stays
  a problem afterwards. A source for a scan must stay active long enough to make the
  measurement and then fade quickly. A source in a smoke detector must last for
  years.

Getting the pair right is most of the design.

## Medical uses

**Tracers.** A small amount of a gamma-emitting isotope is injected into the
patient, or swallowed, and a detector outside the body follows it. The tracer shows
where a fluid goes, how an organ is working, or where a blockage is.

A gamma emitter is chosen because the rays must leave the body to be detected. An
alpha or beta emitter would be stopped inside the patient, and its energy would be
deposited in healthy tissue for no benefit. Technetium-99m is the workhorse: it has
a half-life of 6.0 hours, long enough for a scan and short enough to fade quickly.

**Radiotherapy.** A beam of gamma rays from outside the body is aimed at a tumour.
The gamma rays are intense enough to damage the cells in their path, and the beam is
shaped and rotated so that the tumour receives a much larger dose than the healthy
tissue around it.

## Industrial uses

**Smoke detectors.** A small americium-241 source sits in a chamber open to the air.
Its alpha particles ionise the air, so a small current flows between two plates.
Smoke particles absorb the ions, the current falls, and the circuit sounds the
alarm.

Alpha radiation is safe here because it travels only a few centimetres in air and
cannot pass through the plastic case. If the case is opened, the source must not be
touched or taken apart.

**Sterilising.** Medical instruments and some foods are sterilised by gamma
radiation. The gamma rays pass through the packaging and kill the microorganisms
inside, so the product can be sealed before treatment. The method leaves no
radioactive residue, because the objects themselves do not become radioactive.

**Testing welds.** A gamma source is placed on one side of a weld and a film on the
other. Cracks and voids show up as darker patches on the film, because less
radiation reached those places.

## Dating with carbon-14

Living things take in carbon from the air, and a tiny fraction of it is carbon-14.
While the plant or animal is alive, the carbon-14 that decays is replaced, so the
activity stays steady. When it dies, the replacement stops and the activity falls.

The half-life of carbon-14 is 5730 years, which is the right size for dating things
that are a few thousand years old. An archaeologist measures the activity of a
sample, compares it with the activity of living material, and counts the half-lives.

**Example 1.** A sample of wood has one quarter of the carbon-14 activity of living
wood. Calculate its age.

One quarter is $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2$, so two half-lives have passed:

$$t = 2 \times 5730 = 11460\ \text{years}$$

That is $1.1 \times 10^4$ years to two significant figures.

**Why the method has a limit.** After about ten half-lives the activity left is
under a thousandth of the starting value, and it is too small to measure above the
background. For carbon-14 that is about 57000 years, so older material must be dated
with a longer-lived isotope.

## Hazards

Ionising radiation knocks electrons off atoms. Inside a living cell it can break
chemical bonds, including the bonds in DNA. A cell may repair the damage, may die, or
may be damaged in a way it passes on when it divides.

- **A large dose in a short time** kills many cells. The immediate effects are burns
  and radiation sickness, and a very large dose is fatal.
- **A small dose** leaves a raised chance of cancer many years later. The risk grows
  with the dose, so the aim is to keep every dose as low as reasonably possible.

The damage depends on where the radiation gives up its energy. Alpha particles
deposit all of it in a small mass of tissue, beta particles spread it a little
further, and gamma rays spread it through the whole body.

> **Watch out.** A source that is safe to hold can be dangerous to breathe in. Alpha
> particles are stopped by the dead outer layer of the skin, so an alpha source
> outside the body is harmless. The same particles inside the body have nothing
> between them and living cells, and because they ionise strongly the dose to that
> small mass of tissue is large. Never eat, drink or smoke near an unsealed source.

## Reducing the dose

Three things reduce the dose, and all three are used together.

1. **Time.** Spend as little time near the source as possible.
2. **Distance.** Keep as far away as possible. The radiation spreads out as it
   travels, so the count rate falls quickly with distance.
3. **Shielding.** Put an absorber between the source and the person. Choose the
   material for the emission: paper for alpha, a few millimetres of aluminium for
   beta, and several centimetres of lead for gamma.

The people who work with radiation wear a film badge, which records the dose they
receive over a period. The reading is checked regularly, so a dose that is building
up is noticed.

**Example 2.** A gamma source gives 800 counts per minute at a detector. Each 10 mm
of lead halves the count rate. Calculate the count rate behind 50 mm of lead.

Fifty millimetres is five lots of 10 mm, so the count rate is halved five times:

$$800 \div 2^5 = 800 \div 32 = 25\ \text{counts per minute}$$

## Radioactive waste

A source that has finished its useful life is still radioactive, and some waste
stays dangerous for thousands of years. A long half-life means the activity falls
slowly, so the waste must be kept away from people for a very long time and still
needs shielding.

The waste is sealed in solid containers and buried deep underground in a stable rock
formation. The plan is to keep it isolated until its activity has fallen to the level
of the rock around it.
