# 20.4 Uses and hazards of radiation — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Which emission is stopped by a sheet of paper?
2. A source has a half-life of 6.0 hours. How many half-lives fit into 24 hours?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. Alpha radiation. A sheet of paper stops alpha particles, but beta particles and
   gamma rays pass through it.
2. Four, because $24 \div 6.0 = 4$. Divide the time by the half-life to count the
   half-lives.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A hospital needs a tracer that can be followed through a patient by a
detector outside the body. State which emission the tracer should give out, and
explain why the other two are unsuitable.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The radiation has to leave the patient and reach the detector.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A gamma emitter is needed, because gamma rays pass through the body.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The tracer should be a gamma emitter.

Gamma rays pass through the body and reach the detector outside it. Alpha particles
are stopped by a few centimetres of air or by the skin, so they would never leave
the patient. Beta particles travel further, but a few millimetres of tissue stop
them. Either way, the radiation would be absorbed inside the patient, so the
detector would record nothing useful and healthy tissue would receive the dose.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A tracer is prepared with an activity of 800 MBq and a half-life of 6.0
hours. Calculate its activity 24 hours later.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

First count the half-lives in 24 hours.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$24 \div 6.0 = 4$, so divide by $2^4 = 16$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{number of half-lives} = \frac{24}{6.0} = 4$$

$$A = \frac{800}{2^4} = \frac{800}{16} = 50\ \text{MBq}$$

That is $5.0 \times 10^1$ MBq. A short half-life like this is chosen so the
patient's dose falls quickly after the scan.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A smoke detector contains an alpha source. Explain how the source and the
detector work together to sound the alarm, and why the source is safe in a home.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The alpha particles ionise the air. What happens to that ionised air when smoke
enters the chamber?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The ions carry a small current. Smoke absorbs the ions, so the current falls.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The alpha particles ionise the air in the chamber, so the air conducts and a small
current flows between two plates. When smoke enters, the smoke particles absorb the
ions. Fewer ions are left to carry the current, so the current falls, and the circuit
sounds the alarm.

The source is safe in a home because alpha particles travel only a few centimetres
in air and cannot pass through the plastic case or the dead outer layer of the skin.
The source is sealed inside the case, and it must not be opened or taken apart.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A worker handles radioactive sources every day. Describe three ways to reduce
the dose received, and explain why each one works.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the time spent near the source, the distance from it, and what is
between the worker and it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Time, distance and shielding.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Reduce the time spent near the source. The chance of radiation reaching the body
grows with the time, so less time means less dose.

Increase the distance from the source. The radiation spreads out as it travels, so
the count rate falls quickly with distance.

Use shielding, and choose the material for the emission: paper for alpha particles,
a few millimetres of aluminium for beta particles, and several centimetres of lead
for gamma rays. The absorber stops the radiation or reduces it before it reaches the
worker.

A film badge records the dose received over a period, so a dose that is building up
is noticed.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A sample of wood from an old building has one quarter of the carbon-14
activity of living wood. The half-life of carbon-14 is 5730 years. Calculate the age
of the wood, to two significant figures.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One quarter is one half of one half. How many half-lives is that?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Two half-lives. Multiply the half-life by two.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}$$

so two half-lives have passed.

$$t = 2 \times 5730 = 11460\ \text{years}$$

Here $t$ is the age of the wood.

That is $1.1 \times 10^4$ years to two significant figures.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** An alpha source is safe to carry in a pocket but dangerous if the material is
breathed in. Explain this difference.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What is between the alpha particles and living cells in each case?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Outside the body the skin stops them. Inside, nothing does.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Outside the body, the dead outer layer of the skin stops the alpha particles, so
they never reach living cells.

Inside the body, there is nothing between the alpha particles and living tissue. An
alpha particle ionises strongly, so all its energy is deposited in a small mass of
tissue. That concentrated dose damages cells, which is why an alpha source must
never be eaten, drunk or breathed in.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 8

**7.** A 0.20 kg mass of tissue absorbs 0.60 J of energy from radiation. The
specific heat capacity of the tissue is 3400 J/(kg·K). Calculate the temperature
rise of the tissue.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E = mc\Delta\theta$, rearranged for the temperature change.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Delta\theta = \dfrac{E}{mc} = \dfrac{0.60}{0.20 \times 3400}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\Delta\theta = \frac{E}{mc} = \frac{0.60}{0.20 \times 3400} = 8.8 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{K}$$

The temperature rise is less than a thousandth of a kelvin, so you would never feel
it. That is the point: radiation damages cells by breaking chemical bonds, not by
heating the tissue. A dose that causes no noticeable warming can still do harm,
which is why doses are limited by law and monitored with a film badge.

</details>
</details>
</details>
