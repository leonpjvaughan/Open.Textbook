# Chapter 20: Radioactivity

Some nuclei are unstable. Sooner or later one of them changes into a different
nucleus, and throws out a particle or a burst of energy as it does. Nothing you do
from outside can hurry it up or slow it down.

That one fact is the whole of this chapter. It is why a radioactive source keeps
working in the dark, in the cold and under water, and it is why the activity of a
source falls by the same fraction in every equal stretch of time.

## The chapter problem

**The bog oak trackway.** Workers cutting turf in a bog uncover a trackway of oak
planks. The wood is far older than any written record, so the age must come from
the wood itself.

Every living thing takes in carbon, and a tiny fraction of that carbon is
carbon-14. Carbon-14 is unstable, so it decays. While the tree is alive it keeps
replacing what decays, and its carbon-14 activity stays steady. When the tree dies,
the replacement stops and the activity begins to fall.

A sample of the oak gives a count rate of 4 counts per minute. A sample of living
wood of the same mass gives 16 counts per minute. The half-life of carbon-14 is
5730 years.

By the end of the chapter you can say what carbon-14 emits, work out how many
half-lives have passed, write the nuclear equation for the decay, and explain why
the method stops working for a sample that is very old.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- describe radioactive decay as a spontaneous and random change in an unstable
  nucleus, and explain why heating or squeezing a source does not change it;
- name the three emissions and compare their charge, mass, ionising effect and
  penetrating power;
- describe how each emission moves in an electric field and in a magnetic field;
- use the half-life of a source to find its activity after a whole number of
  half-lives, and to find the half-life from a decay curve;
- write a balanced nuclear equation for alpha, beta and gamma decay;
- describe uses of radiation in medicine, industry and dating, and explain how a
  worker reduces the dose received.

## Where it is used

Smoke detectors, medical tracers and cancer treatment, the sterilising of
instruments, the dating of bones and timber, and the testing of welds in pipelines.

## Your turn to connect it

Radioactivity is used where nothing else reaches. A tracer follows a fluid through
an engine without taking it apart. A source checks a weld from the outside. A
dating method puts a number on an object no one wrote about.

Choose one use you have met, or one you would like to understand. Write down what
the radiation has to pass through to do its job. You will be able to say which
emission suits that job by the end of the chapter.
