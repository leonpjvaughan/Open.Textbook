# 3.1 Forces and free-body diagrams — Learn

**Prerequisites:** motion in a straight line and acceleration from Chapter 2;
measurement and units from Chapter 1.

**Objectives:** identify the forces acting on an object; calculate a weight; draw a
free-body diagram; find the resultant force from the diagram.

By the end of this section, you can list the forces on an object and work out the
resultant force from a diagram.

## What a force is

A **force** is a push or a pull. It is measured in **newtons** (N). One newton is the
force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of $1\,\text{m/s}^2$.

A force has a size and a direction, so it is a **vector**. That is why we draw forces
as arrows. The length of the arrow shows the size, and the way it points shows the
direction.

Forces come in two kinds.

- **Contact forces** act where two objects touch. A push, a pull on a rope, friction
  and the contact force from a floor are all contact forces.
- **Non-contact forces** act at a distance, through a field. Weight, the magnetic
  force and the electric force are non-contact forces.

## Weight

The **weight** of an object is the force of gravity on it. It acts downwards, towards
the centre of the Earth.

> **Weight.** $W = mg$

- $W$ is the weight, in newtons (N);
- $m$ is the mass, in kilograms (kg);
- $g$ is the **gravitational field strength**, which is $9.8$ N/kg near the surface of
  the Earth.

Mass and weight are not the same thing. Mass is the amount of matter, in kilograms,
and it does not change if you travel to the Moon. Weight is a force, and it changes
with $g$.

**Example 1.** A passenger has a mass of 60 kg. Calculate her weight.

$$
W = mg
$$

$$
W = 60 \times 9.8
$$

$$
W = 588\,\text{N}
$$

The weight acts downwards. Her mass is still 60 kg.

## The free-body diagram

A **free-body diagram** shows one object on its own, with an arrow for every force
that acts on it. Nothing else appears on the diagram.

Four rules keep the diagram honest.

1. Draw **one** object, and draw every force acting **on** it.
2. Draw an arrow for each force, starting from the object and pointing the way the
   force acts.
3. Make the length of each arrow match the size of the force.
4. Label each arrow with the force it shows.

The forces that the object exerts on other things do not go on its own diagram.

**Example 2.** A box of mass 5.0 kg rests on a table. Draw its free-body diagram.

The weight is $W = mg = 5.0 \times 9.8 = 49\,\text{N}$ downwards. The box is at rest,
so the forces on it balance. The table pushes up with a contact force of $49\,\text{N}$.

Two arrows, each 49 N: one down, labelled weight, and one up, labelled contact force.

**Example 3.** A box is pushed to the right with a force of 20 N. Friction acts on it
to the left with a force of 8.0 N. Find the resultant force.

Four arrows act on the box. The weight and the contact force are equal and opposite,
so they add to zero. The horizontal forces are 20 N to the right and 8.0 N to the
left, so the **resultant force** is

$$
20 - 8.0 = 12\,\text{N}
$$

to the right.

> **Watch out.** There is no such force as "the force of motion". A moving object does
> not need a forward force to keep moving. If you see an arrow labelled "motion" or
> "forward force" on a free-body diagram, ask which object is pushing or pulling. If
> nothing is, the arrow does not belong on the diagram. A moving object with no
> forward force carries on at a steady speed.

## The lift from the chapter problem

The lift has a mass of 800 kg, so its weight is

$$
W = mg = 800 \times 9.8 = 7840\,\text{N}
$$

Three forces act on the lift while it moves upwards.

- The **tension** in the cable acts upwards, because the cable pulls the lift up.
- The **weight** acts downwards, because the Earth pulls the lift down.
- **Friction** in the guide rails acts downwards, because the lift is moving upwards
  and friction opposes the motion. It is 160 N.

The diagram shows the three arrows. The tension is not equal to the weight, because
the lift is speeding up.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The lift is moving upwards and speeding up at
$1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$.

Write down the three forces acting on the lift, with their directions. Then say which
of them is largest, and why. Keep your answer. Section 3.2 uses the same three forces
to find the tension in the cable.

## You should now be able to

- say what a force is, give its unit, and explain why it is a vector;
- tell a contact force from a non-contact force, with an example of each;
- calculate a weight from a mass using $W = mg$;
- explain the difference between mass and weight;
- draw a free-body diagram of an object, with the arrows and labels;
- find the resultant force from the forces on an object, and give its direction.
