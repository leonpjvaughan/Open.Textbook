# 3.1 Forces and free-body diagrams — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the unit of force, and what does a force of 1 N do?
2. A car speeds up from 4.0 m/s to 16 m/s in 6.0 s. Calculate its acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The newton, N. A force of 1 N gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of
   $1\,\text{m/s}^2$.
2. $a = \dfrac{v - u}{t} = \dfrac{16 - 4.0}{6.0} = 2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. If you wrote
   12, you found the change in speed but did not divide by the time. An acceleration
   is a change in velocity **each second**.

Both return here: a force changes the motion of an object, and the change is an
acceleration.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A bag has a mass of 4.0 kg. Calculate its weight. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Weight is a force, so use $W = mg$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 4.0 \times 9.8$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mg = 4.0 \times 9.8 = 39.2\,\text{N}$$

The data give two significant figures, so write $39\,\text{N}$. The weight acts
downwards. The mass is still 4.0 kg.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A crate has a weight of 490 N. Calculate its mass. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $W = mg$ for the mass.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$m = \dfrac{W}{g} = \dfrac{490}{9.8}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$m = \frac{W}{g} = \frac{490}{9.8} = 50\,\text{kg}$$

Check the sense of it: a mass of 50 kg has a weight of about 500 N, and 490 N is
close to that.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A box of weight 80 N rests on a floor. State the size and direction of the
contact force from the floor, and explain your answer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The box is at rest. What does that tell you about the forces on it?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

At rest, the forces balance, so the contact force must match the weight.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The contact force is $80\,\text{N}$ upwards.

The box is at rest, so its velocity is not changing. The resultant force on it is
therefore zero. The only two forces are the weight, 80 N downwards, and the contact
force. They must be equal in size and opposite in direction.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A ball is falling through the air. Its weight is 6.0 N and the air resistance
is 2.0 N. Calculate the resultant force on the ball, and state its direction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The two forces act along the same line, in opposite directions.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Subtract the smaller force from the larger one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$6.0 - 2.0 = 4.0\,\text{N}$$

The weight is the larger force, so the resultant is $4.0\,\text{N}$ downwards. The
resultant points the same way as the acceleration, so the ball speeds up as it falls.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A trolley is pulled to the right with a force of 15 N. Friction acts on it to
the left with a force of 6.0 N. Draw the free-body diagram and find the resultant
force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The diagram needs four arrows: two vertical and two horizontal.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The vertical pair cancels. Work out the horizontal pair.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The diagram has the weight downwards, the contact force upwards, the pull of 15 N to
the right, and friction of 6.0 N to the left.

The weight and the contact force are equal and opposite, so they add to zero. The
horizontal forces are

$$15 - 6.0 = 9.0\,\text{N}$$

to the right. The resultant is $9.0\,\text{N}$ to the right, so the trolley speeds up
to the right.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A parachutist has a weight of 700 N. The air resistance on the parachute is
500 N. Calculate the resultant force, and say what happens to her speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The weight acts downwards and the air resistance acts upwards.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$700 - 500$, downwards.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$700 - 500 = 200\,\text{N}$$

The resultant is $200\,\text{N}$ downwards. There is still a resultant force in the
direction she is falling, so she is still speeding up. When the air resistance grows
to 700 N, the resultant becomes zero and her speed stops changing.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 2

**7.** A stone is dropped from rest and falls for 2.5 s. Calculate its speed. Take
$g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The stone starts from rest and gains speed at $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = gt = 9.8 \times 2.5$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = u + gt = 0 + 9.8 \times 2.5 = 24.5\,\text{m/s}$$

The data give two significant figures, so write $25\,\text{m/s}$.

This matters here: the only force on a falling stone is its weight. Section 3.4 shows
why the speed stops growing once air resistance becomes large enough.

</details>
</details>
</details>
