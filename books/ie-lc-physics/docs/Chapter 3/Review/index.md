# Chapter 3: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The lift that feels heavier.** A lift and its passengers have a total mass of
800 kg. A single cable pulls the lift up. Friction in the guide rails is 160 N. The
lift starts from rest and speeds up at $1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ for 2.0 s. It then travels
at a constant speed, and finally slows down at $1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ and stops. A
passenger of mass 60 kg stands on a scale inside the lift. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.

In these formulas $W$ is the weight, $m$ the mass, $g$ the gravitational field
strength, $T$ the tension and $f$ the friction. Also $a$ is the acceleration, $N$ the
contact force, $t$ the time, $p$ the momentum and $\Delta p$ the change in momentum.

**The forces on the lift.** Three forces act on the lift: the tension $T$ upwards, the
weight downwards, and friction downwards. The weight is

$$W = mg = 800 \times 9.8 = 7840\,\text{N}$$

**Speeding up.** Take upwards as positive. The resultant force is $T - mg - f$, and it
equals $ma$:

$$T - mg - f = ma$$

$$T = m(g + a) + f = 800 \times (9.8 + 1.2) + 160 = 8960\,\text{N}$$

The scale reading for the passenger comes from the same equation, with $f = 0$ because
no friction acts on her:

$$N = m(g + a) = 60 \times (9.8 + 1.2) = 660\,\text{N}$$

Her weight is $60 \times 9.8 = 588\,\text{N}$, so the scale reads 92 N more than her
weight. She feels heavier, and her mass has not changed.

**The change in momentum.** The lift reaches $v = at = 1.2 \times 2.0 = 2.4\,\text{m/s}$
at the end of this stage. The passenger's change in momentum is

$$\Delta p = mv = 60 \times 2.4 = 144\,\text{kg m/s}$$

The resultant force on her is the rate of change of momentum:

$$F = \frac{\Delta p}{t} = \frac{144}{2.0} = 72\,\text{N}$$

Check this against the forces: the contact force is 660 N and her weight is 588 N, so
the resultant is $660 - 588 = 72\,\text{N}$ upwards. The two methods agree.

**Moving at a constant speed.** Now $a = 0$, so the tension balances the weight and
the friction:

$$T = mg + f = 7840 + 160 = 8000\,\text{N}$$

The scale reads her weight, 588 N. She feels normal again.

**Slowing down.** The lift is still moving upwards, but the acceleration points
downwards, so $a = -1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$:

$$T = m(g + a) + f = 800 \times (9.8 - 1.2) + 160 = 7040\,\text{N}$$

$$N = m(g + a) = 60 \times (9.8 - 1.2) = 516\,\text{N}$$

The scale reads 72 N less than her weight, so she feels lighter.

**The third-law pair at the scale.** The scale pushes up on the passenger with 660 N,
and the passenger pushes down on the scale with 660 N. Those two forces are equal and
opposite, and they act on different objects. They are a third-law pair, so neither one
cancels the other.

<!-- tier:h -->

**The counterweight.** With the motor off, the 800 kg lift is balanced by a 600 kg
counterweight. The lift is the heavier side, so it descends:

$$a = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{(800 - 600) \times 9.8}{800 + 600} =
1.4\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The tension is less than the weight of the lift, which is why the lift accelerates
downwards:

$$T = m_2(g + a) = 600 \times (9.8 + 1.4) = 6720\,\text{N}$$

<!-- /tier:h -->

**The answer in one line.** The scale reads more than her weight while the lift speeds
up, exactly her weight at a constant speed, and less than her weight while it slows
down, because the scale supplies whatever extra force the acceleration needs.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies**
and say why. Three of them ask you to choose between $F = ma$ and the impulse.

1. A resultant force of 48 N acts on a mass of 12 kg. Calculate the acceleration.
2. A box of mass 8.0 kg is pushed with a force of 30 N. Friction is 14 N. Calculate
   the acceleration.
3. A passenger of mass 55 kg stands in a lift that accelerates downwards at
   $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$. Calculate the reading on the scale. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.
4. A car of mass 1000 kg travels at 25 m/s. Calculate its momentum.
5. A ball of mass 0.15 kg hits a wall at 10 m/s and rebounds at 8.0 m/s. Taking the
   direction towards the wall as positive, calculate the change in momentum.
6. A skydiver of mass 75 kg falls at terminal velocity. Calculate the drag force on
   her.
7. A box slides with a contact force of 400 N and a coefficient of friction of 0.25.
   Calculate the friction force.
8. State Newton's third law, and name the partner of the weight of a book resting on
   a table.
9. A stone is dropped from rest and falls for 1.2 s. Calculate its speed and the
   distance it falls. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$.
10. A car brakes from 24 m/s to rest in 3.0 s. Calculate its deceleration and the
    distance it travels while braking.
11. A passenger is held by a seat belt in a crash. To find the force on her, would
    you use $F = ma$ or the impulse $Ft = mv - mu$? Say why, and name what each method
    needs.
12. A crate is pushed with 50 N and does not move. A student says the friction force
    is $\mu N$. Explain why the student could be wrong.

## What you can now do

- identify the forces on an object and draw its free-body diagram, with labels;
- calculate a weight, and tell mass from weight;
- state Newton's first law and use a steady speed to say that the resultant force is
  zero;
- use $F = ma$ with the **resultant** force to find a force, a mass or an
  acceleration;
- state Newton's third law, identify a pair, and explain why the pair does not cancel;
- calculate a momentum and a change in momentum, and use the impulse;
- describe static and kinetic friction, and calculate a friction force from $\mu$;
- explain terminal velocity in terms of the drag and the weight;
- find the acceleration of two connected objects and the tension between them.

## Thinking back

Return to the moment you chose at the start of the chapter — a lift starting, a bus
over a hump, a plane taking off, a rollercoaster, a trampoline.

You can now say why you feel heavier and lighter without your mass changing. Write
down the direction of the acceleration at each moment, then use $N = m(g + a)$ with
your own mass to estimate the two scale readings. A rough estimate is a real answer,
and saying how rough it is belongs in the physics.
