# 3.2 Newton's first and second laws — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A resultant force of zero acts on a moving object. What happens to its velocity?
2. Calculate the weight of a crate of mass 25 kg. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The velocity does not change. The object keeps moving at the same speed in the
   same direction. That is Newton's first law.
2. $W = mg = 25 \times 9.8 = 245\,\text{N}$ downwards. If you wrote 25, that is the
   mass in kilograms, not the weight in newtons.

Both return here: the second law connects the resultant force to the acceleration,
and a weight is one of the forces you have to add up.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A resultant force of 18 N acts on a mass of 3.0 kg. Calculate the acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $F = ma$ for the acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{18}{3.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{18}{3.0} = 6.0\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The unit works out too: $\text{N/kg} = \text{m/s}^2$, because a newton is a kilogram
metre per second squared.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A mass of 500 kg accelerates at $0.40\,\text{m/s}^2$. Calculate the resultant
force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

This time you want the force, so multiply.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = ma = 500 \times 0.40$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = ma = 500 \times 0.40 = 200\,\text{N}$$

A resultant force of 200 N is small for a 500 kg mass, so an acceleration of
$0.40\,\text{m/s}^2$ is gentle. Check that the size makes sense.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A resultant force of 45 N gives an object an acceleration of
$3.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. Calculate its mass.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $F = ma$ for the mass.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$m = \dfrac{F}{a} = \dfrac{45}{3.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$m = \frac{F}{a} = \frac{45}{3.0} = 15\,\text{kg}$$

Check the sense of it: a 15 kg mass with a 45 N resultant force has an acceleration
of $3.0\,\text{m/s}^2$, because $15 \times 3.0 = 45$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A box of mass 7.0 kg is pushed to the right with a force of 40 N. Friction
acts to the left with a force of 12 N. Calculate the acceleration of the box.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the resultant force first, then use $F = ma$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The resultant is $40 - 12 = 28\,\text{N}$ to the right. Then $a = \dfrac{28}{7.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The two horizontal forces act in opposite directions, so the resultant is

$$40 - 12 = 28\,\text{N}$$

to the right. Then

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{28}{7.0} = 4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The acceleration points the same way as the resultant force. Using the 40 N push on
its own would give $5.7\,\text{m/s}^2$, which is too large.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A passenger of mass 60 kg stands on a scale in a lift. Calculate the reading
when the lift accelerates upwards at $0.80\,\text{m/s}^2$, and again when it
accelerates downwards at $0.80\,\text{m/s}^2$. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The scale reads the contact force, $N = m(g + a)$. Watch the sign of $a$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Upwards: $N = 60 \times (9.8 + 0.80)$. Downwards: $N = 60 \times (9.8 - 0.80)$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Accelerating upwards:

$$N = m(g + a) = 60 \times (9.8 + 0.80) = 636\,\text{N}$$

Accelerating downwards:

$$N = m(g - a) = 60 \times (9.8 - 0.80) = 540\,\text{N}$$

Her weight is 588 N. The scale reads more than her weight when the acceleration is
upwards, and less when it is downwards.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A lift of mass 500 kg has a friction force of 100 N in its guide rails. It
accelerates upwards at $0.60\,\text{m/s}^2$. Calculate the tension in the cable. Take
$g = 9.8$ N/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The tension must lift the weight, overcome friction and provide the accelerating
force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$T = m(g + a) + f = 500 \times (9.8 + 0.60) + 100$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$T = m(g + a) + f = 500 \times 10.4 + 100 = 5300\,\text{N}$$

Check the parts: the weight is 4900 N, the accelerating force is 300 N, and the
friction is 100 N. Together they make 5300 N, which matches.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 2

**7.** A car starts from rest and accelerates at $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ for 5.0 s.
Calculate its final speed and the distance it travels.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the equations of motion for constant acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = u + at = 0 + 2.0 \times 5.0$, then $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = u + at = 0 + 2.0 \times 5.0 = 10\,\text{m/s}$$

$$s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 = 0 + \frac{1}{2} \times 2.0 \times 5.0^2 = 25\,\text{m}$$

This matters here: the acceleration in these equations is the same $a$ that appears
in $F = ma$. If you know the resultant force and the mass, you can find the
acceleration and feed it into the equations of motion.

</details>
</details>
</details>
