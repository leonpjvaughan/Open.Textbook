# 3.2 Newton's first and second laws — Learn

**Prerequisites:** free-body diagrams and weight from Section 3.1; acceleration from
Chapter 2.

**Objectives:** state Newton's first law; use $F = ma$ in any direction; find the
reading on a scale in a moving lift.

By the end of this section, you can use the resultant force on an object to find its
acceleration, and the other way round.

## Newton's first law

> **Newton's first law.** An object stays at rest, or keeps moving at a constant
> velocity, unless a resultant force acts on it.

A constant velocity means the same speed **and** the same direction. So the first law
says two things.

- With no resultant force, the velocity does not change.
- With a resultant force, the velocity does change, so the object accelerates.

This is why a moving object does not need a forward force to keep going. A book
sliding across a table slows down only because friction is a resultant force on it.

## Newton's second law

When a resultant force does act, it changes the velocity. Newton's second law says
how much.

> **Newton's second law.** The resultant force on an object is equal to its mass
> multiplied by its acceleration.

$$F = ma$$

- $F$ is the **resultant force**, in newtons (N);
- $m$ is the mass, in kilograms (kg);
- $a$ is the acceleration, in metres per second squared (m/s$^2$).

The force and the acceleration point the same way. The unit of force is built from
this equation: $1\,\text{N} = 1\,\text{kg}\,\text{m/s}^2$.

**Example 1.** A resultant force of 30 N acts on a trolley of mass 6.0 kg. Calculate
the acceleration.

$$
a = \frac{F}{m}
$$

$$
a = \frac{30}{6.0}
$$

$$
a = 5.0\,\text{m/s}^2
$$

**Example 2.** A car of mass 1200 kg accelerates at $1.5\,\text{m/s}^2$. Calculate the
resultant force.

$$
F = ma
$$

$$
F = 1200 \times 1.5
$$

$$
F = 1800\,\text{N}
$$

This is the resultant force, not the driving force. The engine pushes harder than
1800 N, and friction and air resistance take the rest.

> **Watch out.** $F$ in $F = ma$ is the **resultant** force, not any single force. If
> two forces act along the same line, add them with their signs first, and put the
> answer into $F = ma$. A common mistake is to use the driving force on its own, which
> gives an acceleration that is too large. Check the direction too: a negative
> resultant force means the acceleration points the other way.

## The scale in the lift

The passenger has a mass of 60 kg, so her weight is

$$
W = mg = 60 \times 9.8 = 588\,\text{N}
$$

A scale measures the **contact force** it pushes up with. That force is not always
equal to her weight, because the lift can accelerate.

Take upwards as positive. Two forces act on her: the contact force $N$ upwards and
her weight $mg$ downwards. The resultant is $N - mg$, and it equals $ma$.

$$N - mg = ma$$

$$N = m(g + a)$$

**Example 3.** The lift accelerates upwards at $1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$. Calculate the
reading on the scale.

$$
N = m(g + a)
$$

$$
N = 60 \times (9.8 + 1.2)
$$

$$
N = 660\,\text{N}
$$

The scale reads 660 N, which is more than her weight of 588 N. She feels heavier,
even though her mass has not changed.

**Example 4.** The lift moves at a constant speed. Calculate the reading.

At a constant speed the acceleration is zero, so $a = 0$ and

$$
N = 60 \times 9.8 = 588\,\text{N}
$$

The scale now reads her weight. When the lift slows down at the top, the acceleration
points downwards, so $a$ is negative and the reading drops below 588 N.

## The tension in the cable

The same method gives the tension in the cable. Three forces act on the lift: the
tension $T$ upwards, the weight $mg$ downwards, and friction $f$ downwards. Take
upwards as positive, so the resultant is $T - mg - f$, and

$$T - mg - f = ma$$

**Example 5.** The lift accelerates upwards at $1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$, with
$m = 800\,\text{kg}$ and $f = 160\,\text{N}$. Calculate the tension.

$$
T = m(g + a) + f
$$

$$
T = 800 \times (9.8 + 1.2) + 160
$$

$$
T = 8960\,\text{N}
$$

The tension must lift the weight, overcome the friction, and still provide the force
that accelerates the lift.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The lift starts from rest and speeds up at
$1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$.

Use $N = m(g + a)$ to find the scale reading for the 60 kg passenger. Then use
$T = m(g + a) + f$ to find the tension in the cable for the 800 kg lift, with
$f = 160\,\text{N}$. Keep both answers. Section 3.3 uses the same journey to find the
change in the passenger's momentum.

## You should now be able to

- state Newton's first law and explain what it says about a constant velocity;
- say why a moving object does not need a forward force to keep moving;
- use $F = ma$ to find a resultant force, a mass or an acceleration;
- explain why $F$ must be the resultant force, not a single force;
- find the reading on a scale in a lift that is speeding up, moving steadily or
  slowing down;
- find the tension in the cable of a moving lift.
