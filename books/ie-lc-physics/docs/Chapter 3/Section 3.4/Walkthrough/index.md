# 3.4 Friction and resistive forces — Learn

**Prerequisites:** free-body diagrams from Section 3.1; $F = ma$ from Section 3.2.

**Objectives:** describe friction and the factors that set its size; calculate a
friction force from the coefficient of friction; explain terminal velocity.

By the end of this section, you can find the resistive force on a moving object and
explain why a falling object stops speeding up.

## Friction

Push a heavy box along a floor and you feel a force pushing back. That force is
**friction**, and it acts where the two surfaces meet. Friction always opposes the
motion, or the tendency to move.

Friction is a contact force, so it only acts while the surfaces touch. Its size
depends on two things.

- The **surfaces**. Rough surfaces give more friction than smooth ones.
- The **contact force** between the surfaces. A heavier box is pressed harder onto
  the floor, so it has more friction.

Friction does not depend on the area of contact, and for the sliding case it does not
depend much on the speed.

## Static and kinetic friction

There are two cases, and they behave differently.

- **Static friction** acts while the surfaces are not sliding past one another. It
  grows to match the push, up to a maximum. The box does not move until the push
  reaches that maximum.
- **Kinetic friction** acts once the surfaces are sliding. Its size is roughly
  constant, and it is usually smaller than the maximum static friction.

That is why a heavy box is harder to start moving than to keep moving. It is also why
the box can sit still on a slope, but slide once it is nudged hard enough.

## The coefficient of friction

For two sliding surfaces, the friction force is proportional to the contact force.

> **The coefficient of friction.** $F_f = \mu N$

- $F_f$ is the friction force, in newtons (N);
- $\mu$ is the **coefficient of friction**, which has no unit;
- $N$ is the contact force between the surfaces, in newtons (N).

**Example 1.** A box slides on a floor. The contact force is 150 N and the
coefficient of friction is 0.40. Calculate the friction force.

$$
F_f = \mu N
$$

$$
F_f = 0.40 \times 150
$$

$$
F_f = 60\,\text{N}
$$

**Example 2.** A car of mass 1000 kg accelerates at $0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$. The driving
force is 800 N. Calculate the total resistive force on the car.

The resultant force is

$$
F = ma = 1000 \times 0.50 = 500\,\text{N}
$$

The driving force is 800 N, so the resistive force is

$$
800 - 500 = 300\,\text{N}
$$

Air resistance and friction between the tyres and the road make up this force.

> **Watch out.** Friction is not always $\mu N$. That formula gives the friction for
> surfaces that are **sliding**. If the box is at rest, static friction is whatever is
> needed to balance the push, and it can be smaller than $\mu N$. A box pushed with
> 20 N on a floor with a limiting friction of 60 N feels a friction force of 20 N, not
> 60 N, and it does not move.

## Air resistance and terminal velocity

**Air resistance**, also called **drag**, is the resistive force on an object moving
through a fluid. It increases as the object moves faster.

A falling object is the clearest case. Two forces act on it: its weight downwards, and
the drag upwards.

- At the start the weight is much larger than the drag, so the object accelerates.
- As it speeds up, the drag grows.
- When the drag equals the weight, the resultant force is zero.

The object then falls at a constant speed. That speed is the **terminal velocity**.

**Example 3.** A skydiver of mass 80 kg falls at terminal velocity. Calculate the drag
force on her.

At terminal velocity the resultant force is zero, so the drag equals her weight.

$$
W = mg = 80 \times 9.8 = 784\,\text{N}
$$

where $W$ is the weight, $m$ the mass and $g$ the gravitational field strength.

The drag force is 784 N upwards.

## You should now be able to

- describe friction as a contact force that opposes motion;
- name the two things that set the size of a friction force;
- tell static friction from kinetic friction, and explain why starting a box is harder
  than keeping it moving;
- calculate a friction force from $\mu$ and the contact force;
- explain what air resistance is and how it changes with speed;
- explain terminal velocity in terms of the drag and the weight.
