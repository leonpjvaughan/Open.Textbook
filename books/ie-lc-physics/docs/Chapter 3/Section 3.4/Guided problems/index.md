# 3.4 Friction and resistive forces — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. State Newton's second law, and write the equation.
2. A resultant force of 24 N acts on a box of mass 6.0 kg. Calculate the
   acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The resultant force on an object equals its mass multiplied by its acceleration:
   $F = ma$.
2. $a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{24}{6.0} = 4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. If you wrote 144, you
   multiplied instead of dividing.

Both return here: friction is one of the forces you add up to find the resultant, and
the resultant gives the acceleration.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A box slides on a floor. The contact force is 250 N and the coefficient of
friction is 0.30. Calculate the friction force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $F_f = \mu N$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F_f = 0.30 \times 250$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F_f = \mu N = 0.30 \times 250 = 75\,\text{N}$$

The coefficient has no unit, so the answer is a force in newtons. The friction acts
along the floor, opposing the sliding.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A force of 90 N keeps a crate sliding at a constant speed. The contact force
between the crate and the floor is 300 N. Calculate the coefficient of friction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At a constant speed the push equals the friction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\mu = \dfrac{F_f}{N} = \dfrac{90}{300}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

At a constant speed the resultant force is zero, so the friction equals the push,
which is 90 N. Then

$$\mu = \frac{F_f}{N} = \frac{90}{300} = 0.30$$

Dividing a force by a force leaves no unit, which is why $\mu$ is a plain number.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A car of mass 1200 kg accelerates at $0.40\,\text{m/s}^2$. The driving force is
900 N. Calculate the total resistive force on the car.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the resultant force first, then compare it with the driving force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = ma = 1200 \times 0.40 = 480\,\text{N}$. The resistive force is what is left of
the 900 N.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = ma = 1200 \times 0.40 = 480\,\text{N}$$

The driving force is 900 N and the resultant is 480 N, so the resistive force is

$$900 - 480 = 420\,\text{N}$$

The resistive force points backwards, opposing the motion. Air resistance and
friction between the tyres and the road make it up.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A skydiver of mass 65 kg falls at terminal velocity. Calculate the drag force
on her. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At terminal velocity the resultant force is zero.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The drag equals her weight, $W = mg = 65 \times 9.8$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

At terminal velocity the speed is constant, so the resultant force is zero. The drag
force equals her weight:

$$W = mg = 65 \times 9.8 = 637\,\text{N}$$

The drag force is 637 N upwards, which is $6.4 \times 10^{2}\,\text{N}$ to two
significant figures.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A block is pushed with a force of 35 N and does not move. State the friction
force on the block, and explain your answer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The block is at rest. What does that say about the resultant force?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Static friction grows to match the push, as long as the push is below the limit.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The friction force is $35\,\text{N}$, opposing the push.

The block is at rest, so the resultant force on it is zero. The push and the friction
must be equal and opposite. This is static friction, and it matches the push exactly
because the push is below the limiting value.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A lift of mass 600 kg moves upwards at a constant speed. The tension in the
cable is 6200 N. Calculate the friction force in the guide rails. Take
$g = 9.8$ N/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At a constant speed the tension balances the weight and the friction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = mg = 600 \times 9.8 = 5880\,\text{N}$. Then subtract.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mg = 600 \times 9.8 = 5880\,\text{N}$$

At a constant speed the resultant force is zero, so the tension balances the weight
and the friction:

$$f = 6200 - 5880 = 320\,\text{N}$$

The friction acts downwards, because the lift is moving upwards.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 2

**7.** A car travelling at 20 m/s brakes to rest in 4.0 s. Calculate its deceleration
and the distance it travels while braking.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the equations of motion. Take the direction of travel as positive.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{v - u}{t} = \dfrac{0 - 20}{4.0}$, then $s = \dfrac{u + v}{2}t$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{0 - 20}{4.0} = -5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The deceleration is $5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$, and the minus sign says it opposes the
motion.

$$s = \frac{u + v}{2}t = \frac{20 + 0}{2} \times 4.0 = 40\,\text{m}$$

This matters here: the force that slows the car is friction between the brake pads and
the discs. A larger friction force would give a larger deceleration and a shorter
stopping distance.

</details>
</details>
</details>
