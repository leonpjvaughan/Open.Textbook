# 3.5 Connected objects and systems — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Calculate the weight of a mass of 6.0 kg. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.
2. A resultant force of 36 N acts on an object of mass 9.0 kg. Calculate the
   acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $W = mg = 6.0 \times 9.8 = 58.8\,\text{N}$ downwards. If you wrote 6.0, that is the
   mass, not the weight.
2. $a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{36}{9.0} = 4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$.

Both return here: a connected system is solved by finding the weights, then using
$F = ma$ on the whole system and on one object.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

In these problems $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the two masses, $g$ the acceleration of free
fall, $a$ the acceleration, $T$ the tension and $F$ a force.

**1.** Masses of 6.0 kg and 2.0 kg hang from a light string over a frictionless
pulley. Calculate the acceleration. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Treat the two masses as one system. The net force is the difference in the weights,
and the mass is the total.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{(6.0 - 2.0) \times 9.8}{6.0 + 2.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)g}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{(6.0 - 2.0) \times 9.8}{6.0 + 2.0} =
\frac{4.0 \times 9.8}{8.0} = 4.9\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The 6.0 kg mass falls and the 2.0 kg mass rises, both at $4.9\,\text{m/s}^2$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** For the same pulley system, calculate the tension in the string.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Now treat one mass on its own. Use the 2.0 kg mass, which is rising.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$T = m_2(g + a) = 2.0 \times (9.8 + 4.9)$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The 2.0 kg mass is rising, so the tension is larger than its weight:

$$T = m_2(g + a) = 2.0 \times (9.8 + 4.9) = 29.4\,\text{N}$$

The data give two significant figures, so write $29\,\text{N}$.

Check it on the 6.0 kg mass, which is falling:
$T = m_1(g - a) = 6.0 \times (9.8 - 4.9) = 29.4\,\text{N}$. Both give the same
tension, which is the check that the working is sound.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Masses of 5.0 kg and 2.0 kg hang from a light string over a frictionless
pulley. Calculate the acceleration and the tension in the string.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Two steps: the system first, then one mass on its own.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{(5.0 - 2.0) \times 9.8}{5.0 + 2.0} = \dfrac{3.0 \times 9.8}{7.0}$.
Then $T = m_2(g + a)$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{(5.0 - 2.0) \times 9.8}{5.0 + 2.0} = \frac{3.0 \times 9.8}{7.0} =
4.2\,\text{m/s}^2$$

$$T = m_2(g + a) = 2.0 \times (9.8 + 4.2) = 28\,\text{N}$$

The tension is 28 N, which is larger than the weight of the 2.0 kg mass, 19.6 N. That
is why the lighter mass rises.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A locomotive of mass 25000 kg pulls two carriages, each of mass 10000 kg. The
driving force is 22500 N. Ignore resistive forces. Calculate the acceleration of the
train.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The driving force accelerates the whole train, so add all three masses.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Total mass $= 25000 + 10000 + 10000 = 45000\,\text{kg}$. Then $a = \dfrac{22500}{45000}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{22500}{25000 + 10000 + 10000} = \frac{22500}{45000} =
0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$$

Every part of the train has this acceleration, because the couplings hold the parts
together.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** For the same train, calculate the force in the coupling between the locomotive
and the first carriage.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The coupling pulls both carriages, so the mass it accelerates is their total mass.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = ma = (10000 + 10000) \times 0.50$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The coupling behind the locomotive pulls both carriages, so the mass it accelerates
is $10000 + 10000 = 20000\,\text{kg}$:

$$F = ma = 20000 \times 0.50 = 10000\,\text{N}$$

That is $1.0 \times 10^{4}\,\text{N}$. The coupling between the two carriages pulls
only the rear carriage, so it carries half of this force, which is 5000 N.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain why the tension in the string is not equal to the weight of either
hanging mass.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

If the tension equalled the weight, what would the resultant force on that mass be?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A zero resultant force means zero acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

If the tension equalled the weight of a hanging mass, the two forces on that mass
would be equal and opposite. The resultant force would be zero, so the mass would not
accelerate.

Both masses are accelerating, so on each of them the tension and the weight are not
equal. On the falling mass the weight is larger, and on the rising mass the tension is
larger. The difference between the two forces is what produces the acceleration.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 3.4

**7.** A skydiver of mass 80 kg falls at terminal velocity. Calculate the drag force
on her. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At terminal velocity the resultant force is zero.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The drag equals her weight, $W = mg = 80 \times 9.8$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mg = 80 \times 9.8 = 784\,\text{N}$$

At terminal velocity the drag force is 784 N upwards, equal to her weight. This is the
same balance you use in a connected system: when the two forces are equal, the
resultant is zero and there is no acceleration.

</details>
</details>
</details>
