# 3.3 Newton's third law and momentum — Learn

**Prerequisites:** free-body diagrams from Section 3.1; $F = ma$ from Section 3.2;
velocity from Chapter 2.

**Objectives:** state Newton's third law and identify a pair of forces; calculate a
momentum and a change in momentum; use the impulse.

By the end of this section, you can find the force on an object from the change in its
momentum. You can also say why a pair of third-law forces never cancels.

## Newton's third law

Push a wall and the wall pushes back. The harder you push, the harder it pushes. That
is Newton's third law.

> **Newton's third law.** If object A exerts a force on object B, then object B
> exerts a force on object A. The two forces are equal in size and opposite in
> direction.

The important part is **which object each force acts on**. The two forces act on
different objects, so they never cancel. Forces cancel only when they act on the same
object.

The lift and its cable are a pair. The cable pulls the lift up, and the lift pulls the
cable down. The two forces are equal in size. One acts on the lift and the other acts
on the cable, so neither one cancels the other.

> **Watch out.** A book resting on a table has two forces on it: its weight downwards
> and the contact force upwards. They are equal and opposite, so they balance. They
> are **not** a third-law pair, because both act on the book. The third-law partner of
> the book's weight is the pull of the book on the Earth, which acts on the Earth.

## Momentum

A moving object has **momentum**. A heavy object has more momentum than a light one
at the same speed, and a fast object has more than a slow one.

> **Momentum.** $p = mv$

- $p$ is the momentum, in kilogram metres per second (kg m/s);
- $m$ is the mass, in kilograms (kg);
- $v$ is the velocity, in metres per second (m/s).

Momentum is a **vector**, so it points the way the object is moving. If the object
turns round, the sign of its momentum changes.

**Example 1.** A car of mass 1500 kg travels at 20 m/s. Calculate its momentum.

$$
p = mv
$$

$$
p = 1500 \times 20
$$

$$
p = 30000\,\text{kg m/s}
$$

That is $3.0 \times 10^{4}\,\text{kg m/s}$ to two significant figures.

## Change in momentum and impulse

A force changes the momentum of an object. The change is called the **impulse**, and
it equals the force multiplied by the time the force acts.

$$Ft = mv - mu$$

- $F$ is the force, in newtons (N);
- $t$ is the time the force acts, in seconds (s);
- $mu$ is the momentum before, and $mv$ is the momentum after.

**Example 2.** A ball of mass 0.40 kg hits a wall at 12 m/s and rebounds at 9.0 m/s.
Calculate the change in momentum.

Take the direction towards the wall as positive. Then $u = +12\,\text{m/s}$ and
$v = -9.0\,\text{m/s}$.

$$
\Delta p = m(v - u)
$$

$$
\Delta p = 0.40 \times (-9.0 - 12)
$$

$$
\Delta p = -8.4\,\text{kg m/s}
$$

The minus sign says the change points away from the wall. The wall pushed the ball
away, so the force on the ball points away from the wall too.

**Example 3.** The passenger of mass 60 kg in the lift speeds up from rest to
2.4 m/s in 2.0 s. Calculate her change in momentum and the resultant force on her.

$$
\Delta p = mv = 60 \times 2.4 = 144\,\text{kg m/s}
$$

$$
F = \frac{\Delta p}{t}
$$

$$
F = \frac{144}{2.0} = 72\,\text{N}
$$

The resultant force on her is 72 N upwards. Her weight is 588 N, so the scale pushes
up with $588 + 72 = 660\,\text{N}$, which agrees with Section 3.2.

**Why a longer contact time is safer.** In a crash the change in momentum is fixed by
the speeds and the masses. Because $Ft = \Delta p$, a longer contact time gives a
smaller force. A crumple zone, a seat belt and an air bag all stretch the time, so the
force on the passengers is smaller.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The lift speeds up from rest to $2.4\,\text{m/s}$ in
2.0 s, and the passenger has a mass of 60 kg.

Calculate the change in the passenger's momentum, and then the resultant force on
her. Check your resultant force against the scale reading you found in Section 3.2:
the contact force minus the weight should give the same answer. Keep this working.
Section 3.5 uses the same method on two objects joined together.

## You should now be able to

- state Newton's third law and say which object each force in a pair acts on;
- explain why a third-law pair does not cancel, and why a balanced pair is not a
  third-law pair;
- calculate the momentum of a moving object, with its unit and its direction;
- find a change in momentum, including when an object rebounds;
- use $Ft = mv - mu$ to find a force, a time or a change in momentum;
- explain why a longer contact time gives a smaller force for the same change in
  momentum.
