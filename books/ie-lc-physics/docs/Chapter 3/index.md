# Chapter 3: Forces and Newton's laws

Nothing moves on its own. A bus pulls away because the engine pushes it, a ball
falls because the Earth pulls it, and a cup stays on a table because the table
pushes up on it. A **force** is a push or a pull, and this chapter is about finding
the forces on an object and saying what they do to its motion.

## The chapter problem

**The lift that feels heavier.** A lift and its passengers have a total mass of
800 kg. A single cable pulls the lift up. Friction in the guide rails is 160 N. The
lift starts from rest and speeds up at $1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ for 2.0 s. It then travels
at a constant speed, and finally slows down at $1.2\,\text{m/s}^2$ and stops. A
passenger of mass 60 kg stands on a scale inside the lift. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.

By the end of the chapter you can draw the free-body diagram of the lift and of the
passenger, work out what the scale reads at each stage of the journey, find the
tension in the cable, and explain how the passenger's momentum changes.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- identify the forces acting on an object, and draw a free-body diagram of it;
- calculate a weight from a mass, and find the resultant force from the forces on an
  object;
- state Newton's first law, and say what a steady speed tells you about the forces;
- use $F = ma$ to find a force, a mass or an acceleration;
- state Newton's third law, and explain why the two forces act on different objects;
- calculate a momentum and a change in momentum, and use the impulse;
- describe friction and the resistive forces on a moving object, and explain terminal
  velocity;
- find the acceleration of connected objects and the tension in the string or cable
  between them.

## Where it is used

Lifts and cranes, seat belts and crumple zones, the brakes on a bike, a parachute, a
rocket, and the tug that moves an aircraft on the apron. Every one of them is a
question about the forces on an object and what those forces do to its motion.
