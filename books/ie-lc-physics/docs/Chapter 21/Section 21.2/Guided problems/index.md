# 21.2 Binding energy and the mass defect — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write down the equation that links mass and energy, and state what each symbol
   means.
2. How many nucleons are in a helium-4 nucleus, and what are they?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $E = mc^2$. Here $E$ is the energy in joules, $m$ is the mass in kilograms and
   $c$ is the speed of light, $3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s. The trap is to forget that the
   mass must be in kilograms before you square the speed of light.
2. Four nucleons: two protons and two neutrons. The mass number, 4, is the number
   of nucleons, and the atomic number, 2, is the number of protons.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A helium-4 nucleus contains 2 protons and 2 neutrons. A proton has a mass of
1.0073 u, a neutron 1.0087 u, and the nucleus 4.0015 u. Calculate the mass defect.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the masses of the separate nucleons, then subtract the mass of the nucleus.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$2 \times 1.0073 + 2 \times 1.0087 - 4.0015$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The separate nucleons have a mass of

$$2 \times 1.0073 + 2 \times 1.0087 = 4.0320\ \text{u}$$

The mass defect is

$$4.0320 - 4.0015 = 0.0305\ \text{u}$$

Check the direction of the subtraction: the separate nucleons must weigh more than
the nucleus, so the answer is positive. A negative mass defect means the two sides
have been swapped.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** The mass defect of a helium-4 nucleus is 0.0305 u. Calculate its binding
energy in joules. Take $1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg and
$c = 3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Change the mass defect to kilograms, then use $E = mc^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Delta m = 0.0305 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, then $E_b = \Delta m\,c^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\Delta m = 0.0305 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 5.06 \times 10^{-29}\ \text{kg}$$

$$E_b = \Delta m\,c^2 = 5.06 \times 10^{-29} \times 9.0 \times 10^{16} = 4.6 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{J}$$

That is $4.6 \times 10^{-12}$ J to two significant figures. It is the energy needed
to pull the nucleus into four separate nucleons.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** The binding energy of a helium-4 nucleus is $4.56 \times 10^{-12}$ J.
Calculate its binding energy per nucleon in MeV. Take
$1\ \text{MeV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

First change the binding energy to MeV, then divide by the number of nucleons.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{4.56 \times 10^{-12}}{1.6 \times 10^{-13}}$ gives the binding energy in MeV,
then divide by 4.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_b = \frac{4.56 \times 10^{-12}}{1.6 \times 10^{-13}} = 28.5\ \text{MeV}$$

A helium-4 nucleus has four nucleons, so

$$\frac{28.5}{4} = 7.1\ \text{MeV per nucleon}$$

That is about 7 MeV per nucleon, which is lower than the value for carbon-12. Each
nucleon in helium-4 is bound less tightly than each nucleon in carbon-12.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A carbon-12 nucleus has a mass defect of 0.0993 u. Calculate its binding
energy per nucleon in MeV. Take $1\ \text{u} = 9.3 \times 10^2$ MeV.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

There are two steps: change the mass defect to energy, then divide by 12.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E_b = 0.0993 \times 9.3 \times 10^2$ MeV, then divide by 12.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_b = 0.0993 \times 9.3 \times 10^2 = 92.3\ \text{MeV}$$

A carbon-12 nucleus has twelve nucleons, so

$$\frac{92.3}{12} = 7.7\ \text{MeV per nucleon}$$

The result is larger than the 7.1 MeV per nucleon of helium-4, so carbon-12 sits
higher on the curve of binding energy per nucleon.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain the difference between the mass defect of a nucleus and its binding
energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One is a mass and one is an energy. Say what each one is, and how they are linked.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The mass defect is the missing mass; the binding energy is its energy equivalent,
and it is the energy needed to separate the nucleus.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The mass defect is the difference between the mass of the separate nucleons and the
mass of the nucleus. It is a mass, measured in atomic mass units or kilograms.

The binding energy is the energy equivalent of that mass defect, found from
$E_b = \Delta m\,c^2$. It is the energy needed to pull the nucleus apart into its
separate nucleons, and the energy released when the nucleons join to form the
nucleus.

So the two describe the same thing, one as a mass and one as an energy.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain why the binding energy per nucleon is used to compare one nucleus
with another, rather than the binding energy itself.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about a small nucleus and a large one with the same binding energy per
nucleon.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A large nucleus has more nucleons, so its total binding energy is larger even when
each nucleon is held equally tightly.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A large nucleus has more nucleons than a small one, so its total binding energy is
larger even if each nucleon is bound equally tightly. Comparing the total binding
energies would say only that one nucleus is bigger.

Dividing by the number of nucleons gives the average energy holding each nucleon.
That value is a fair comparison between nuclei of different sizes, and plotting it
against mass number gives the curve with its peak at iron-56.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 19

**7.** In a hydrogen atom the $n = 3$ level has an energy of $-1.51$ eV and the
$n = 1$ level has an energy of $-13.6$ eV. Calculate the energy of the photon
emitted when the electron moves from $n = 3$ to $n = 1$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The photon energy is the difference between the two levels, $E_3 - E_1$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$-1.51 - (-13.6)$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = E_3 - E_1 = -1.51 - (-13.6) = 12.09\ \text{eV}$$

So the photon carries 12.1 eV. This is an ultraviolet photon, because the energy is
larger than the red line at 1.89 eV. Energy is released when the electron drops, so
the atom gives out a photon.

</details>
</details>
</details>
