# 21.2 Binding energy and the mass defect — Learn

**Prerequisites:** $E = mc^2$ and the atomic mass unit from Section 21.1; protons,
neutrons and isotopes from Chapter 19; reading a graph from Chapter 1.

**Objectives:** define the mass defect and the binding energy; calculate a mass
defect from the masses of the nucleons and the nucleus; convert a mass defect into
a binding energy and a binding energy per nucleon; describe the curve of binding
energy per nucleon against mass number and explain what its peak means.

By the end of this section you can take a list of nuclear masses, find the mass
defect, and say whether a reaction will release energy.

## A nucleus weighs less than its parts

Take a helium-4 nucleus apart. It contains two protons and two neutrons. Weigh the
separate nucleons, then weigh the nucleus they form, and the nucleus is lighter.
The parts weigh more than the whole.

Here are the masses.

| Particle | Mass / u |
|---|---|
| Proton | 1.0073 |
| Neutron | 1.0087 |
| Helium-4 nucleus | 4.0015 |

The two protons and two neutrons together have a mass of

$$2 \times 1.0073 + 2 \times 1.0087 = 4.0320\ \text{u}$$

The helium-4 nucleus has a mass of 4.0015 u. The difference is

$$4.0320 - 4.0015 = 0.0305\ \text{u}$$

This missing mass is the **mass defect** of the nucleus. It is not a mistake in the
measurement, and it is not lost. The nucleons are bound together, and a bound
system has less energy than the same particles separated. Less energy means less
mass, by $E = mc^2$.

## Binding energy

The mass defect has an energy equivalent. That energy is the **binding energy** of
the nucleus: the energy needed to pull the nucleus apart into its separate
nucleons, and the energy released when the nucleons come together to form it.

$$E_b = \Delta m\,c^2$$

**Example 1.** Calculate the binding energy of a helium-4 nucleus, whose mass
defect is 0.0305 u.

First change the mass defect to kilograms:

$$\Delta m = 0.0305 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 5.06 \times 10^{-29}\ \text{kg}$$

Then

$$E_b = \Delta m\,c^2 = 5.06 \times 10^{-29} \times 9.0 \times 10^{16} = 4.56 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{J}$$

That is $4.6 \times 10^{-12}$ J to two significant figures. In megaelectronvolts,

$$E_b = \frac{4.56 \times 10^{-12}}{1.6 \times 10^{-13}} = 28.5\ \text{MeV}$$

So the helium-4 nucleus is bound by about 29 MeV. Breaking it into two protons and
two neutrons would cost that much energy.

## Binding energy per nucleon

A big nucleus has a big binding energy because it has more nucleons. To
compare one nucleus with another, divide by the number of nucleons, $A$. The result
is the **binding energy per nucleon**.

$$\text{binding energy per nucleon} = \frac{E_b}{A}$$

For helium-4, $A = 4$, so

$$\frac{28.5}{4} = 7.1\ \text{MeV per nucleon}$$

This number is a measure of how tightly each nucleon is held. The larger it is, the
harder it is to pull the nucleus apart.

## The curve of binding energy per nucleon

Plot the binding energy per nucleon against the mass number for every stable
nucleus, and one shape appears.

| Nucleus | Mass number, $A$ | Binding energy per nucleon / MeV |
|---|---|---|
| Helium-4 | 4 | 7.1 |
| Carbon-12 | 12 | 7.7 |
| Oxygen-16 | 16 | 8.0 |
| Iron-56 | 56 | 8.8 |
| Uranium-235 | 235 | 7.6 |

The curve rises steeply for the lightest nuclei, reaches a maximum near iron-56 at
about 8.8 MeV per nucleon, and then falls slowly for the heaviest nuclei.

The peak is the most important feature. Iron-56 is the most tightly bound nucleus,
so a nucleus can release energy by moving towards the peak on the curve.

- A **light** nucleus is to the left of the peak, so joining two light nuclei into
  a heavier one moves them right, towards the peak. This is **fusion**, and it
  releases energy.
- A **heavy** nucleus is to the right of the peak, so splitting it into two lighter
  nuclei moves them left, towards the peak. This is **fission**, and it also
  releases energy.

Both processes release energy, and for the same reason: the products are more
tightly bound than the starting nucleus, so the products have less mass, and the
difference appears as energy. This is the idea behind the whole of Section 21.3.

**Example 2.** A carbon-12 nucleus has a mass defect of 0.0993 u. Calculate its
binding energy per nucleon in MeV. Take $1\ \text{u} = 9.3 \times 10^2$ MeV.

Convert the mass defect to energy:

$$E_b = 0.0993 \times 9.3 \times 10^2 = 92.3\ \text{MeV}$$

Divide by the 12 nucleons:

$$\frac{92.3}{12} = 7.7\ \text{MeV per nucleon}$$

So each nucleon in a carbon-12 nucleus is bound by about 7.7 MeV. The value is
higher than the 7.1 MeV of helium-4, which is why carbon-12 sits further up the
curve.

> **Watch out.** The mass defect is the mass of the separate nucleons minus the
> mass of the nucleus, so it is always positive. If you subtract the other way you
> get a negative mass, which cannot be right. Check that the separate nucleons
> weigh more than the nucleus they form, and remember to divide by the number of
> nucleons when the question asks for the value per nucleon.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. In one fission of a uranium-235 nucleus the mass
defect is about 0.21 u.

1. Convert 0.21 u to kilograms. Take $1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.
2. Use $E = mc^2$ to find the energy released, in joules.
3. Compare your answer with the 200 MeV quoted for one fission.

The two numbers should be close. The small difference comes from rounding the mass
defect, and from quoting 200 MeV rather than the exact value.

## You should now be able to

- define the mass defect and the binding energy of a nucleus;
- find a mass defect from the masses of the nucleons and the mass of the nucleus;
- convert a mass defect into a binding energy in joules and in megaelectronvolts;
- find a binding energy per nucleon, and explain why it is used to compare nuclei;
- describe the shape of the curve of binding energy per nucleon against mass
  number;
- explain why fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy nuclei both release
  energy.
