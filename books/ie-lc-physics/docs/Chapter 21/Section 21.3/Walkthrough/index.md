# 21.3 Fission and fusion — Learn

**Prerequisites:** $E = mc^2$ from Section 21.1; the mass defect, binding energy and
the curve of binding energy per nucleon from Section 21.2; nuclear equations from
Section 20.3.

**Objectives:** describe nuclear fission and write an equation for it; explain where
the energy comes from; describe a chain reaction and the difference between a
controlled and an uncontrolled one; describe nuclear fusion and the conditions it
needs; describe positron–electron annihilation.

By the end of this section you can say which way a nucleus must move on the
binding-energy curve to release energy, and describe how that is made to happen in
a reactor and in a star.

## Splitting a heavy nucleus

A heavy nucleus sits to the right of the peak on the curve of binding energy per
nucleon. If it splits into two lighter nuclei, the products sit closer to the peak,
so they are more tightly bound and have less mass. The missing mass appears as
energy.

This is **nuclear fission**: the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two lighter
nuclei, with the release of energy.

Fission is started by a neutron. A slow neutron is absorbed by a uranium-235
nucleus, which becomes uranium-236. Uranium-236 is unstable, and it splits into two
smaller nuclei and two or three more neutrons. One possible reaction is

$${}^{235}_{92}\text{U} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \to {}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$$

The mass numbers balance: $235 + 1 = 236$ on the left, and
$141 + 92 + 3 = 236$ on the right. The atomic numbers balance too:
$92 + 0 = 92$ on the left, and $56 + 36 + 0 = 92$ on the right.

Each fission of a uranium-235 nucleus releases about 200 MeV, which is
$3.2 \times 10^{-11}$ J. That is millions of times more than a chemical reaction
releases for each atom, because the mass defect is a measurable fraction of the
nucleus rather than a fraction of the electrons.

## The chain reaction

Every fission releases two or three neutrons, and each of those neutrons can be
absorbed by another uranium-235 nucleus and cause another fission. The process can
therefore feed itself. This is a **chain reaction**.

The chain reaction can do one of three things.

- **If one neutron from each fission causes exactly one more fission**, the number
  of fissions stays constant. The reaction is **controlled**, and it can run
  steadily for months. A power reactor is held in this state.
- **If more than one neutron from each fission causes another fission**, the number
  of fissions grows with every generation. The reaction is **uncontrolled**, and it
  releases energy faster and faster. This is what happens in a weapon.
- **If fewer than one neutron from each fission causes another fission**, the
  reaction dies out.

A chain reaction is controlled with two parts.

- A **moderator**, such as water or graphite, slows the neutrons. Slow neutrons are
  much more likely than fast ones to be absorbed by uranium-235, so the moderator
  keeps the reaction going at a lower fuel concentration.
- **Control rods**, made of boron or cadmium, absorb neutrons. Lowering the rods
  into the core absorbs more neutrons and slows the reaction; raising them lets the
  reaction speed up.

## Joining light nuclei

A light nucleus sits to the left of the peak on the curve. If two light nuclei join
to form a heavier one, the product sits closer to the peak, so it is more tightly
bound and has less mass. Again the missing mass appears as energy.

This is **nuclear fusion**: the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier
nucleus, with the release of energy.

Fusion powers the Sun. In the core of the Sun, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium:

$$4\,{}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \to {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + 2\,{}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} + 2\nu + \text{energy}$$

The reaction releases about 26.7 MeV. The positrons are emitted as antiparticles of
electrons, and $\nu$ stands for a neutrino, a very light particle with no charge.

Fusion needs two conditions.

- **A very high temperature**, of millions of kelvin. Both nuclei carry positive
  charge, so they repel each other. Only at a very high temperature do they move
  fast enough to get close enough for the strong nuclear force to bind them.
- **A very high pressure**, so that many nuclei are close together and collisions
  are frequent.

In a star, gravity provides the pressure. On Earth, the hot gas is held away from
the walls by strong magnetic fields in a machine called a tokamak. Fusion has been
made to happen in the laboratory, but no fusion power station exists yet, because
more energy is used to run the machine than the fusion releases.

**Example 1.** A sample contains $2.0 \times 10^{20}$ uranium-235 nuclei, and each
fission releases $3.2 \times 10^{-11}$ J. Calculate the total energy released.

$$E = 2.0 \times 10^{20} \times 3.2 \times 10^{-11} = 6.4 \times 10^{9}\ \text{J}$$

That is $6.4 \times 10^{9}$ J, about the energy a large power station delivers in
two minutes. The number of nuclei in the sample is the key quantity: each one
contributes a very small amount of energy, but there are enormous numbers of them.

## Particle and antiparticle interactions

When a particle meets its antiparticle, the two annihilate. Their mass becomes
energy in the form of gamma-ray photons. For an electron and a positron, the total
energy released is 1.02 MeV, carried away by two photons of 0.51 MeV each.

This is the third way that mass becomes energy in this chapter, after fission and
fusion. It is also the only one in which the whole mass of both particles becomes
energy, with nothing left behind.

> **Watch out.** Fission and fusion are opposite processes, and the words look
> alike. Fission splits one heavy nucleus into two lighter ones. Fusion joins two
> light nuclei into one heavier one. Check which way the nucleus is moving before
> you answer. Both release energy, but for the same reason rather than opposite
> reasons: the products end up more tightly bound, so they have less mass.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. One kilogram of uranium-235 contains about
$2.55 \times 10^{24}$ nuclei, and each fission releases $3.2 \times 10^{-11}$ J.

1. Calculate the total energy released by the fission of all the nuclei in one
   kilogram.
2. Explain in one or two sentences why the reaction in a reactor does not run away.

Keep both answers. Section 21.4 uses the first number to compare nuclear fuel with
coal, and uses the second to describe how a reactor is built.

## You should now be able to

- describe nuclear fission and write an equation for it, balancing mass number and
  atomic number;
- explain why fission releases energy, using the curve of binding energy per
  nucleon;
- describe a chain reaction, and the difference between a controlled and an
  uncontrolled one;
- explain the job of the moderator and of the control rods in a reactor;
- describe nuclear fusion and write an equation for the fusion of hydrogen in the
  Sun;
- explain why fusion needs a very high temperature and a very high pressure;
- describe positron–electron annihilation and the energy it releases.
