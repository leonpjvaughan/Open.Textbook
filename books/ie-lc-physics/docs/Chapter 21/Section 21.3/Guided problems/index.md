# 21.3 Fission and fusion — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the binding energy per nucleon of a nucleus, and what does a large value
   mean?
2. A uranium-238 nucleus decays by alpha emission. Write the nuclear equation, and
   state the mass number and the atomic number of the daughter nucleus.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. It is the binding energy of the nucleus divided by the number of nucleons. A
   large value means each nucleon is held tightly, so the nucleus is hard to pull
   apart. The trap is to give the total binding energy instead: a large nucleus has
   a large total even when each nucleon is loosely held.
2. ${}^{238}_{92}\text{U} \to {}^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$. An
   alpha particle is a helium nucleus, so the mass number falls by 4 and the atomic
   number by 2. The totals balance on both sides: $238 = 234 + 4$ and
   $92 = 90 + 2$.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into barium-141 and
krypton-92. Calculate the number of neutrons released.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The total mass number must be the same before and after. The neutron that starts
the reaction adds 1 to the left-hand side.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$235 + 1 - 141 - 92$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$235 + 1 - 141 - 92 = 3$$

So three neutrons are released. The mass numbers balance:
$235 + 1 = 236$ on the left, and $141 + 92 + 3 = 236$ on the right. Those three
neutrons are what can carry the chain reaction on.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** One fission of a uranium-235 nucleus releases 200 MeV. Calculate the energy
released in joules. Take $1\ \text{MeV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply the energy in MeV by the number of joules in one MeV.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$200 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-13}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = 200 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-13} = 3.2 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{J}$$

So each fission releases $3.2 \times 10^{-11}$ J. This is the number used in the
chapter problem to find the energy stored in a kilogram of fuel.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A sample contains $2.0 \times 10^{20}$ uranium-235 nuclei, and each fission
releases $3.2 \times 10^{-11}$ J. Calculate the total energy released.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply the number of nuclei by the energy released by each one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$2.0 \times 10^{20} \times 3.2 \times 10^{-11}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = 2.0 \times 10^{20} \times 3.2 \times 10^{-11} = 6.4 \times 10^{9}\ \text{J}$$

That is $6.4 \times 10^{9}$ J to two significant figures. Each fission releases a
tiny energy, but a sample holds an enormous number of nuclei, so the total is large.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Describe a chain reaction, and explain the difference between a controlled
and an uncontrolled one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start with the neutrons released by one fission. Then compare what happens when
exactly one of them causes a further fission with what happens when more than one
does.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A chain reaction feeds itself through the neutrons released at each fission. The
number of fissions stays constant when exactly one neutron per fission causes
another, and grows when more than one does.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A chain reaction is a self-sustaining series of fissions. Each fission of a
uranium-235 nucleus releases two or three neutrons, and each of those neutrons can
be absorbed by another uranium-235 nucleus and cause another fission.

The reaction is controlled when exactly one neutron from each fission causes
exactly one more fission. The number of fissions stays constant, so the energy is
released at a steady rate, and the reaction can run for months. A power reactor is
held in this state by control rods, which absorb the surplus neutrons.

The reaction is uncontrolled when more than one neutron from each fission causes
another fission. The number of fissions then grows with every generation, so the
energy is released faster and faster. This is what happens in a weapon.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain why nuclear fusion needs a very high temperature.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What charge do the two nuclei carry? What force acts between them when they are
close?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Both nuclei are positive, so they repel each other. They need enough kinetic energy
to get close enough for the strong nuclear force to take over.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Both nuclei carry positive charge, so they repel each other by the electrostatic
force. To fuse, they must get close enough for the strong nuclear force, which acts
only over a very short range, to bind them together.

The electrostatic repulsion becomes stronger as the nuclei approach, so the nuclei
need a large kinetic energy to overcome it. Temperature is a measure of the average
kinetic energy of the particles, so a very high temperature, of millions of kelvin,
is needed. At that temperature the nuclei move fast enough to fuse.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain, using the curve of binding energy per nucleon, why the fusion of
hydrogen into helium in the Sun releases energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Where do hydrogen and helium sit on the curve, relative to the peak?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Hydrogen is far to the left of the peak, and helium is closer to it. Moving towards
the peak means the product is more tightly bound.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Hydrogen is the lightest nucleus, so it sits far to the left of the peak on the
curve of binding energy per nucleon. Helium-4 sits much closer to the peak, with a
binding energy per nucleon of about 7.1 MeV compared with almost zero for hydrogen.

When four hydrogen nuclei fuse into one helium nucleus, the product is more tightly
bound than the starting nuclei. A more tightly bound nucleus has less mass, so the
mass defect appears as energy. The reaction releases about 26.7 MeV, which is why
the Sun can shine for billions of years.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 20

**7.** A radioactive source has a half-life of 6.0 hours. Calculate the fraction of
the source that remains after 24 hours.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

First work out how many half-lives fit into 24 hours.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$24 \div 6.0 = 4$ half-lives, so the fraction is $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^4$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{24}{6.0} = 4\ \text{half-lives}$$

$$\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \frac{1}{16}$$

So one sixteenth of the source remains. Each half-life halves the amount left, and
the halving happens four times. After four half-lives the activity is
$1/16$ of its starting value.

</details>
</details>
</details>
