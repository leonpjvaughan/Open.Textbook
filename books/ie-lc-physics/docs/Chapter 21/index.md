# Chapter 21: Mass and energy

A kilogram of coal and a kilogram of uranium do not look very different on a
balance. Burn the coal and you can boil a few kettles. Split the uranium and you
release the energy of about three million kilograms of coal.

The difference is not in the fuel. It is in the process. Burning coal rearranges
the electrons in an atom and releases a few electronvolts. Splitting a nucleus
rearranges its nucleons and releases about two hundred million electronvolts. In
both cases a tiny amount of mass goes missing, and the equation $E = mc^2$ says
how much energy that mass becomes.

## The chapter problem

**One kilogram of fuel.** A reactor is fuelled with uranium-235. When a
uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron it splits into two smaller nuclei and
releases about 200 MeV of energy.

One kilogram of uranium-235 contains about $2.55 \times 10^{24}$ nuclei. By the end
of the chapter you can work out the total energy in that kilogram, explain where
the energy came from, explain why the reaction can run away or be held steady, and
weigh the evidence for using it to make electricity in Ireland.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- state that mass and energy are equivalent, and use $E = mc^2$;
- convert between atomic mass units, joules and megaelectronvolts;
- describe the Cockcroft and Walton experiment and say why it mattered;
- explain what a mass defect is, and find a binding energy and a binding energy
  per nucleon;
- describe fission and fusion, and explain why both release energy;
- describe a chain reaction and explain how a reactor holds it steady;
- evaluate evidence about nuclear fission and fusion for making electricity, and
  compare them with other sources.

## Where it is used

Nuclear power stations in several countries make electricity from fission. The Sun
and every other star are powered by fusion. Hospitals use positron emission
tomography, which relies on the annihilation of electrons and positrons. Ireland
has no nuclear power station, so the debate about building one is a debate about
evidence and trade-offs.
