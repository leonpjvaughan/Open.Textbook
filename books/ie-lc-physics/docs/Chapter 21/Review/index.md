# Chapter 21: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**One kilogram of fuel.** A reactor is fuelled with uranium-235. One kilogram of it
contains about $2.55 \times 10^{24}$ nuclei, and each fission releases about
200 MeV.

**Step 1: the energy from one fission, in joules.**

$$E = 200 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-13} = 3.2 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{J}$$

**Step 2: the number of nuclei in one kilogram.** One mole of uranium-235 has a
mass of 235 g and contains $6.0 \times 10^{23}$ nuclei, so

$$N = \frac{1000}{235} \times 6.0 \times 10^{23} = 2.55 \times 10^{24}$$

**Step 3: the total energy.**

$$E = 2.55 \times 10^{24} \times 3.2 \times 10^{-11} = 8.2 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}$$

So one kilogram of uranium-235 releases about $8.2 \times 10^{13}$ J. That is the
answer to the chapter problem.

**Step 4: where the energy came from.** The two fission products are more tightly
bound than the uranium nucleus, so they have slightly less mass. The mass defect is
about 0.21 u, and $E = mc^2$ turns it into energy:

$$\Delta m = 0.21 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 3.5 \times 10^{-28}\ \text{kg}$$

$$E = 3.5 \times 10^{-28} \times 9.0 \times 10^{16} = 3.1 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{J}$$

That is close to the 200 MeV of Step 1. The small difference comes from rounding the
mass defect.

**Step 5: why the reaction does not run away.** Each fission releases two or three
neutrons, and each of those can cause another fission. In a reactor the control rods
absorb the surplus neutrons, so on average exactly one neutron from each fission
causes one more fission. The number of fissions stays constant and the reactor
produces a steady output. Without the control rods, the number of fissions would
grow with every generation.

**Step 6: the comparison with coal.** Burning one kilogram of coal releases about
$3.0 \times 10^7$ J, so the mass of coal with the same energy as one kilogram of
uranium-235 is

$$\frac{8.2 \times 10^{13}}{3.0 \times 10^7} = 2.7 \times 10^6\ \text{kg}$$

That is nearly three thousand tonnes of coal for each kilogram of fuel.

**Step 7: the Irish question.** Ireland has no nuclear power station, and Irish law
has prohibited the generation of electricity from nuclear fission since 1999. The
evidence cuts both ways. Nuclear power gives a large, steady output with almost no
carbon dioxide while it runs, but it leaves long-lived waste and carries an accident
risk. Wind and solar produce no waste but vary with the weather, so they need
storage or a backup source. A fair comparison measures the same things on both
sides, and states the assumptions behind each figure.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **what is being asked**
before you calculate: an energy from a mass, a binding energy, a number of
half-lives, or an argument to be judged.

1. Calculate the energy equivalent of 1.0 g of matter. Take
   $c = 3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s.
2. A reaction has a mass defect of 0.0305 u. Find the energy released in joules.
   Take $1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.
3. Explain the difference between nuclear fission and nuclear fusion, and say why
   both release energy.
4. A sample contains $5.0 \times 10^{20}$ nuclei, and each fission releases
   $3.2 \times 10^{-11}$ J. Find the total energy released.
5. Explain why the moderator is needed in a reactor, and why the control rods are
   needed as well.
6. A nucleus has a binding energy of $1.2 \times 10^{-11}$ J and contains 16
   nucleons. Find its binding energy per nucleon in MeV.
7. In the Sun, four hydrogen nuclei fuse to form one helium nucleus and release
   26.7 MeV. Find the energy released in joules.
8. A radioactive source has a half-life of 6.0 hours. What fraction of the source
   remains after 24 hours?
9. An electron is accelerated through a potential difference of 3000 V. Find its
   kinetic energy in electronvolts and in joules.
10. In a hydrogen atom the electron drops from $n = 3$ to $n = 1$, from $-1.51$ eV
    to $-13.6$ eV. Find the energy of the emitted photon.
11. Two reports give different figures for the cost of electricity from nuclear
    power. Describe what you would check before deciding which one to trust.
12. Explain why fusion needs a very high temperature, and state why no fusion power
    station exists yet.

## What you can now do

- convert between atomic mass units, joules and megaelectronvolts;
- describe the Cockcroft and Walton experiment and say why it mattered;
- find a mass defect, a binding energy and a binding energy per nucleon;
- use the curve of binding energy per nucleon to explain why fission and fusion
  release energy;

- compare nuclear power fairly with the alternatives, and judge the evidence behind
  a claim.

## Thinking back

At the start of the chapter you chose what a country should value most when it
chooses how to make electricity, and you were allowed to pick only two of four:
price, steady supply, low carbon dioxide, and no long-lived waste.

You can now say something quantitative about the trade-off. One kilogram of
uranium-235 releases $8.2 \times 10^{13}$ J, the same as about three thousand tonnes
of coal, so nuclear fuel buys both a low price per unit and a steady supply. What it
does not buy is an end to the waste, because some fission products stay radioactive
for thousands of years.

Write down your two choices again, and then answer this question: which of the four
does your pair leave out, and what would have to be true for that omission to be
acceptable? If your answer depends on a number you are not sure of, write down your
estimate and say how rough it is. Judging an energy choice is part of the physics,
not a separate question.
