# 21.1 Mass-energy equivalence — Learn

**Prerequisites:** work, energy and power from Chapter 4; the electron and its
kinetic energy from Chapter 17; the nucleus, protons and neutrons from Chapter 19;
photon energy and $E = hf$ from Chapter 19.

**Objectives:** state that mass and energy are equivalent; use $E = mc^2$ to find
an energy from a mass or a mass from an energy; convert between atomic mass units,
joules and megaelectronvolts; describe the Cockcroft and Walton experiment and its
significance; describe matter in terms of fundamental particles and their
antiparticles.

By the end of this section you can take a small mass and say how much energy it
represents, and explain why the number is so large.

## Energy has mass

In 1905 Albert Einstein showed that mass and energy are two forms of the same
thing. They are linked by one equation:

$$E = mc^2$$

- $E$ is the energy, in joules (J);
- $m$ is the mass, in kilograms (kg);
- $c$ is the speed of light in a vacuum, $3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s.

The speed of light is squared in the equation, so $c^2 = 9.0 \times 10^{16}$ m$^2$/s$^2$.
That number is enormous. A small mass is therefore equivalent to a very large
energy, and a very large energy is equivalent to a small mass.

**Example 1.** Calculate the energy equivalent of 1.0 g of matter.

Convert the mass to kilograms: $1.0\ \text{g} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ kg. Then

$$E = mc^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 9.0 \times 10^{16} = 9.0 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}$$

That is $9.0 \times 10^{13}$ J, which is more than a large power station delivers
in a day. The mass that produced it is smaller than a sugar cube.

## The atomic mass unit

The mass of a nucleus is far too small for the kilogram to be convenient. Nuclear
physicists use the **atomic mass unit**, u. One atomic mass unit is defined as one
twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom:

$$1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$$

The energy equivalent of one atomic mass unit follows from $E = mc^2$:

$$E = 1.66 \times 10^{-27} \times 9.0 \times 10^{16} = 1.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{J}$$

Expressed in megaelectronvolts, with $1\ \text{MeV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}$ J,

$$E = \frac{1.5 \times 10^{-10}}{1.6 \times 10^{-13}} = 9.3 \times 10^2\ \text{MeV}$$

So one atomic mass unit is equivalent to about $9.3 \times 10^2$ MeV. These
conversions are used so often that they are worth holding in memory.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Speed of light, $c$ | $3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s |
| Atomic mass unit, 1 u | $1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg |
| Electronvolt, 1 eV | $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J |
| Megaelectronvolt, 1 MeV | $1.6 \times 10^{-13}$ J |
| Energy equivalent of 1 u | $9.3 \times 10^2$ MeV |

> **Watch out.** Do not say that the mass disappears. Mass and energy are
> equivalent, so mass that leaves a nucleus is carried away as kinetic energy and
> as radiation. The total of mass and energy is conserved. When you add up the
> masses of the products and find them smaller, the difference is the mass
> equivalent of the energy released.

## Splitting the nucleus

A heavy nucleus can be made to break into two smaller nuclei. This is **nuclear
fission**, and Section 21.3 takes it further. The two products are more tightly
bound than the original nucleus, so they have slightly less mass. The missing mass
appears as energy.

## Accelerating particles: Cockcroft and Walton

The first nuclear reaction driven by a machine was carried out in 1932 by John
Cockcroft and Ernest Walton in Cambridge. Walton was born in Dungarvan, County
Waterford, and the two men shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1951.

Cockcroft and Walton built a machine that accelerated protons through a high
potential difference and fired them at a lithium target. A proton that reached the
lithium nucleus could be absorbed by it, and the lithium-7 nucleus then split into
two alpha particles:

$${}^{1}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{7}_{3}\text{Li} \to {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

The mass numbers balance: $1 + 7 = 8$ on the left, and $4 + 4 = 8$ on the right.
The atomic numbers balance too: $1 + 3 = 4$ on the left, and $2 + 2 = 4$ on the
right.

The two alpha particles flew apart with a total kinetic energy of about 17 MeV.
That is far more than the energy the accelerator gave the proton, which was about
0.7 MeV. The extra energy came from mass: the mass of the two alpha particles is
less than the mass of the proton and the lithium nucleus by an amount whose energy
equivalent is 17 MeV.

The experiment mattered for two reasons. It was the first nuclear transformation
produced by artificially accelerated particles, so it showed that a nucleus can be
changed on demand. It was also the first direct confirmation that $E = mc^2$
describes what happens inside a nucleus, not only in a theory.

## Particles and antiparticles

Every kind of particle has a matching **antiparticle**, with the same mass and the
opposite charge. The antiparticle of the electron is the **positron**, written
$e^+$; it has the same mass as an electron and a charge of $+e$. The antiparticle
of the proton is the antiproton.

Matter is built from **fermions**. Two families of fermions matter here.

- **Quarks** combine in threes to make protons and neutrons.
- **Leptons** include the electron and the neutrino, and they are not made of
  quarks.

Forces are carried by a second family of particles, the **bosons**. A boson is
exchanged between two particles, and that exchange is the force. The photon
carries the electromagnetic force, and the gluon carries the strong nuclear force.
This is the modern picture of matter, and it is still being tested.

When a particle meets its antiparticle, the two can **annihilate**. Both particles
disappear, and their mass becomes energy in the form of gamma-ray photons. The
whole mass is converted, so annihilation is the cleanest example of $E = mc^2$.

**Example 2.** An electron and a positron annihilate at rest. Calculate the total
energy of the photons produced. Take the mass of an electron as
$9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.

The two particles have the same mass, so the mass that becomes energy is
$2 \times 9.1 \times 10^{-31} = 1.82 \times 10^{-30}$ kg. Then

$$E = mc^2 = 1.82 \times 10^{-30} \times 9.0 \times 10^{16} = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$$

That is 1.02 MeV in total, so each of the two photons carries 0.51 MeV. Two
photons are produced, not one, because the two photons must carry away the total
momentum in opposite directions. Positron emission tomography, used in hospitals,
detects these pairs of photons.
