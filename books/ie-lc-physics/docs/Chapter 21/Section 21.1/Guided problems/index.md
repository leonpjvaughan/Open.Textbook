# 21.1 Mass-energy equivalence — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in a neutral atom of carbon-14?
2. A photon has an energy of $3.0 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Calculate its frequency. Take
   $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. 6 protons, 8 neutrons and 6 electrons. The mass number is the protons and
   neutrons together, so $14 - 6 = 8$ neutrons. A neutral atom has as many
   electrons as protons. The trap is to read the mass number as the number of
   neutrons.
2. $f = \dfrac{E}{h} = \dfrac{3.0 \times 10^{-19}}{6.6 \times 10^{-34}} = 4.5 \times 10^{14}$ Hz.
   The energy of a photon and its frequency are linked by $E = hf$, so divide the
   energy by the Planck constant.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** Calculate the energy equivalent of 1.0 kg of matter. Take
$c = 3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E = mc^2$. The mass is already in kilograms, so no conversion is needed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 1.0 \times (3.0 \times 10^8)^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = mc^2 = 1.0 \times (3.0 \times 10^8)^2 = 1.0 \times 9.0 \times 10^{16} = 9.0 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J}$$

That is $9.0 \times 10^{16}$ J to two significant figures. For comparison, a large
power station delivers about $4 \times 10^{13}$ J of electrical energy in a day, so
this mass is worth more than two thousand station-days. This is why $E = mc^2$ is
the key to nuclear energy.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** The atomic mass unit is $1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg. Calculate
the energy equivalent of 1 u in joules.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

This is the same calculation as question 1, with a much smaller mass.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 1.66 \times 10^{-27} \times 9.0 \times 10^{16}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = mc^2 = 1.66 \times 10^{-27} \times 9.0 \times 10^{16} = 1.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{J}$$

That is $1.5 \times 10^{-10}$ J to two significant figures. Although the mass is
tiny, the energy is measurable, because $c^2$ is so large.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Express $1.5 \times 10^{-10}$ J in MeV. Take
$1\ \text{MeV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the energy in joules by the number of joules in one MeV.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{1.5 \times 10^{-10}}{1.6 \times 10^{-13}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{1.5 \times 10^{-10}}{1.6 \times 10^{-13}} = 9.4 \times 10^2\ \text{MeV}$$

So one atomic mass unit is equivalent to about $9.4 \times 10^2$ MeV. The
conversion is easier if you remember that $10^{-10} \div 10^{-13} = 10^3$, so the
answer is close to $1.5 \div 1.6$ thousands.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** In one nuclear reaction the mass defect is 0.21 u. Calculate the energy
released in joules. Take $1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg and
$c = 3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

First change the mass defect from atomic mass units to kilograms, then use
$E = mc^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$m = 0.21 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, then $E = m c^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$m = 0.21 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 3.5 \times 10^{-28}\ \text{kg}$$

$$E = mc^2 = 3.5 \times 10^{-28} \times 9.0 \times 10^{16} = 3.1 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{J}$$

That is $3.1 \times 10^{-11}$ J. It is close to the 200 MeV quoted for one fission
of uranium-235, which is the number the chapter problem uses.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Describe what Cockcroft and Walton did in their experiment, and explain why
the result mattered.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Name what was accelerated, what it hit, and what came out. Then say what the
energy of the products showed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Protons were accelerated and fired at lithium. The lithium nucleus split into two
alpha particles, whose kinetic energy was greater than the energy put in.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Cockcroft and Walton accelerated protons through a high potential difference and
fired them at a lithium target. A proton was absorbed by a lithium-7 nucleus, and
the nucleus split into two alpha particles.

The alpha particles carried away about 17 MeV of kinetic energy, while the
accelerator gave each proton only about 0.7 MeV. The extra energy came from mass,
so the experiment was the first direct evidence that $E = mc^2$ applies inside a
nucleus. It was also the first nuclear transformation caused by artificially
accelerated particles, so it showed that a nucleus can be changed on demand.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** An electron and a positron annihilate at rest. Explain what is produced and
why two photons are produced rather than one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the mass of the pair and about momentum. Before the annihilation the
total momentum is zero.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The whole mass becomes energy, and the photons must carry away zero total momentum.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

When a particle meets its antiparticle, both disappear and their mass becomes
energy. An electron and a positron have the same mass, so the whole mass of the
pair becomes energy in the form of gamma-ray photons.

Two photons are produced, travelling in opposite directions. Before the
annihilation the total momentum of the pair is zero, so the photons must carry away
zero total momentum. One photon could not do that, because a single photon always
carries momentum. Two photons in opposite directions can, and their total energy is
1.02 MeV, so each carries 0.51 MeV.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 17

**7.** An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of
3000 V. Calculate its kinetic energy in electronvolts and in joules.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The energy gained by a charge $e$ moving through a potential difference $V$ is
$eV$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = eV = 3000$ eV. Then convert to joules with $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = eV = 3000\ \text{eV}$$

In joules,

$$E = 3000 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 4.8 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{J}$$

The electronvolt is the energy gained by one electron when it moves through a
potential difference of one volt, so a 3000 V supply gives each electron 3000 eV.
The same number appears in the mass-energy work, where the energies are millions of
times larger.

</details>
</details>
</details>
