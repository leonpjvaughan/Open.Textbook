# 21.4 Nuclear power and its alternatives — Learn

**Prerequisites:** fission, the chain reaction and the control of a reactor from
Section 21.3; the energy stored in a kilogram of uranium-235 from the chapter
problem; energy resources from Chapter 4.

**Objectives:** describe how a nuclear power station turns fission into electricity;
explain the job of each part of a reactor; explain why spent fuel is a long-term
problem; describe the alternatives to nuclear power and compare them fairly; judge
a secondary source of evidence about nuclear fission and fusion.

By the end of this section you can weigh the evidence for and against nuclear power
in Ireland, and say what a fair comparison with the alternatives requires.

## A power station that runs on fission

A nuclear power station is a steam engine. The heat comes from fission, and the
rest of the plant works exactly like a coal or gas station.

| Part | What it does |
|---|---|
| Fuel rods | Hold uranium-235, usually as uranium dioxide sealed in metal tubes |
| Moderator | Slows the neutrons, because slow neutrons are more likely to be absorbed by uranium-235 |
| Control rods | Absorb neutrons; lowering them slows the reaction and raising them speeds it up |
| Coolant | Carries heat from the core to the boiler |
| Shielding | Thick concrete and steel absorb the radiation |
| Turbine and generator | Turn the heat into electrical energy |

The order matters. Fission heats the coolant. The coolant boils water in a second
circuit, and the steam turns a turbine. The turbine turns a generator, and the
generator produces electricity. At every stage some energy is lost, so the
electrical output is smaller than the heat produced.

The fuel rods are the part that is different from every other kind of power station.
A single kilogram of uranium-235 releases about $8.2 \times 10^{13}$ J, so the fuel
lasts a long time and very little of it is needed. A reactor refuels every year or
two rather than every hour.

## Holding the reaction steady

A reactor is kept **critical**: on average, exactly one neutron from each fission
causes exactly one more fission. The number of fissions then stays constant, and
the reactor produces a steady output for months.

The control rods are the main way this is held. Boron and cadmium absorb neutrons
strongly. Pushing the rods deeper into the core absorbs more neutrons and lowers the
rate. Pulling them out raises it. The operator adjusts the rods as the fuel is used
up and as the demand for electricity changes.

If the reaction does start to run away, the control rods are dropped fully into the
core. They absorb so many neutrons that the chain reaction stops within a fraction
of a second. The core still produces heat from the decay of the fission products,
so the coolant must keep flowing even after the reaction has stopped.

## The fuel cycle and the waste

Uranium is mined, then enriched, so that the fraction of uranium-235 is high enough
to sustain a chain reaction. Natural uranium is mostly uranium-238, which does not
fission in this way.

After a year or two in the reactor, the fuel is spent. Two things have changed.

- The uranium-235 has been used up.
- **Fission products** have built up. These are the lighter nuclei left behind by
  fission, and they absorb neutrons, which slows the reaction.

Spent fuel is highly radioactive, and it stays dangerous for a very long time.
Some fission products have half-lives of seconds, and others have half-lives of
thousands of years. The waste is first cooled in a pool of water, because it
produces heat for years after it leaves the reactor. It is then sealed in solid
containers and, in the long term, buried deep underground in a stable rock
formation, where it must stay isolated from people for thousands of years.

That is the central difficulty. A reactor produces a small volume of waste compared
with a coal station, but that waste stays hazardous for far longer than any human
institution has lasted.

> **Watch out.** A power reactor cannot explode like a nuclear weapon. Reactor fuel
> is enriched far less than weapon material, and the chain reaction is held steady
> by the control rods. The serious accident risk is a loss of cooling, which can
> melt the fuel and release radioactive material into the surroundings. Keep the
> two ideas apart: a meltdown is not a nuclear explosion.

## The alternatives

Every way of making electricity has costs and benefits. The comparison is fair only
when the same things are measured on both sides.

| Source | Benefit | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Nuclear fission | Large, steady output; almost no carbon dioxide while running; very little fuel | Long-lived radioactive waste; high construction cost; accident risk |
| Wind | No fuel; no waste; no carbon dioxide while running | Output varies with the weather; needs storage or backup |
| Solar | No fuel; no waste; output matches daytime demand | Output varies with daylight and cloud; needs a large area |
| Hydro | Steady, and can be switched on quickly | Limited by geography; building a dam floods land |
| Gas | Can be switched on quickly; cheap to build | Produces carbon dioxide; fuel must be imported |

Two points make the comparison honest. First, a source that varies with the weather
needs something to fill the gaps, and that something has a cost of its own. Second,
the cost of a source depends on what is counted: whether waste disposal is included,
how long the plant lasts, and what interest rate is used. Two figures for the same
source can differ for those reasons alone.

## Judging the evidence

Most of what you can read about nuclear power is a **secondary source**: a report,
an article or a web page written by someone who was not there. Secondary sources are
useful, and they must be judged.

- **Who wrote it, and what do they gain?** A report from the operator of a power
  station and a report from a group that opposes nuclear power may both quote true
  numbers and still choose different ones.
- **How old is it?** The cost of wind and solar has fallen sharply in the last
  twenty years, so a figure from 2005 describes a different world.
- **Are the units and the assumptions stated?** A cost per unit of electricity is
  meaningless without knowing what is included and over what lifetime.
- **Does an independent source agree?** If three separate organisations reach the
  same number, it is more likely to be reliable. If only one does, say so.

Fusion is a separate question. Fusion produces far less long-lived waste than
fission, and its fuel is abundant. The difficulty is that no fusion device has yet
delivered more energy to the grid than it took from it, so a fusion power station
does not exist. Anyone who claims that fusion will solve an energy problem soon
must explain how that gap will be closed.

## The Irish case

Ireland has no nuclear power station. Irish law has prohibited the generation of
electricity from nuclear fission since the Electricity Regulation Act 1999, so the
debate is about whether that should change.

Most of Ireland's electricity comes from burning gas, which must be imported. Wind
now supplies a large and growing share, and the country is connected to Wales and
Northern Ireland by undersea interconnectors, which allow electricity to be bought
and sold.

The evidence on both sides is real.

- **For nuclear power**: it delivers a large, steady output with almost no carbon
  dioxide while it runs, and it needs very little land and fuel. A small country
  with a limited grid may value that steadiness.
- **Against nuclear power**: it leaves long-lived waste, it carries an accident
  risk, it costs a great deal to build, and Ireland has no nuclear industry or
  regulator to run it.

A reader who has followed this chapter can do something better than take a side.
They can ask what the comparison assumes, and check whether the same assumptions
were used for wind, gas and nuclear alike.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. One kilogram of uranium-235 releases about
$8.2 \times 10^{13}$ J, and burning one kilogram of coal releases about
$3.0 \times 10^7$ J.

1. Calculate the mass of coal that would release the same energy as one kilogram of
   uranium-235.
2. Name one advantage and one disadvantage of nuclear power, and name one
   alternative source that could replace it.
3. Write one sentence saying what evidence you would want before deciding whether
   Ireland should build a nuclear power station.

- describe how a nuclear power station turns fission into electrical energy;
- explain the job of the fuel, the moderator, the control rods, the coolant and the
  shielding;
- explain what it means for a reactor to be critical, and how the control rods hold
  it there;
- explain why spent fuel is a long-term problem;
- compare nuclear power fairly with wind, solar, hydro and gas;
- judge a secondary source of evidence by its author, its age, its assumptions and
  its agreement with other sources;
- describe the state of fusion power and why no fusion power station exists yet.
