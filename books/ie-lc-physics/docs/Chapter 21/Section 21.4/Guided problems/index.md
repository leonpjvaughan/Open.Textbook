# 21.4 Nuclear power and its alternatives — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the job of the moderator in a nuclear reactor?
2. A source falls to one eighth of its original activity in 30 years. State its
   half-life.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The moderator slows the neutrons. Slow neutrons are more likely than fast ones
   to be absorbed by uranium-235 and cause fission. The trap is to say that the
   moderator absorbs neutrons: that is the job of the control rods.
2. 10 years. One eighth is $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^3$, so three half-lives have
   passed, and $30 \div 3 = 10$ years. The trap is to answer with the number of
   half-lives instead of the length of one.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** Name four parts of a nuclear reactor and state the job of each one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the fuel, the neutrons, the heat and the radiation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The fuel rods hold the uranium, the moderator slows the neutrons, the control rods
absorb them, and the coolant carries the heat away.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The **fuel rods** hold the uranium-235, which is where fission happens. The
**moderator** slows the neutrons so that they are more likely to be absorbed and
cause fission. The **control rods** absorb neutrons, so lowering them slows the
reaction and raising them speeds it up. The **coolant** carries heat from the core
to the boiler, where it makes steam to turn a turbine and a generator.

Thick concrete and steel **shielding** around the core absorbs the radiation and
protects the workers.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A reactor is fuelled with uranium-235. Each fission releases
$3.2 \times 10^{-11}$ J. Calculate the energy released by all the nuclei in 1.0 kg
of uranium-235. Take the molar mass as 235 g/mol and Avogadro's number as
$6.0 \times 10^{23}$ per mole.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

First find the number of nuclei in 1.0 kg, then multiply by the energy per fission.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{1000}{235} \times 6.0 \times 10^{23}$ nuclei, then multiply by
$3.2 \times 10^{-11}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The number of nuclei is

$$N = \frac{1000}{235} \times 6.0 \times 10^{23} = 2.55 \times 10^{24}$$

The total energy is

$$E = 2.55 \times 10^{24} \times 3.2 \times 10^{-11} = 8.2 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}$$

That is $8.2 \times 10^{13}$ J. The mass of fuel is small, but the number of nuclei
in it is enormous, so the energy is very large.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Burning 1.0 kg of coal releases about $3.0 \times 10^7$ J. Calculate the mass
of coal that would release the same energy as 1.0 kg of uranium-235, which gives
$8.2 \times 10^{13}$ J.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the energy from the uranium by the energy from one kilogram of coal.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{8.2 \times 10^{13}}{3.0 \times 10^7}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{8.2 \times 10^{13}}{3.0 \times 10^7} = 2.7 \times 10^6\ \text{kg}$$

So 1.0 kg of uranium-235 releases the same energy as about $2.7 \times 10^6$ kg of
coal, which is nearly three thousand tonnes. This is why a reactor needs so little
fuel, and why the waste it produces is so concentrated.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Explain why spent nuclear fuel is a long-term problem.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the half-lives of the fission products, and about what the waste gives
out.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Some fission products have half-lives of thousands of years, so the activity falls
very slowly. The waste stays hot and radioactive, so it must be cooled, shielded and
isolated.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Spent fuel contains fission products, and some of them have half-lives of thousands
of years. Their activity therefore falls very slowly, so the waste stays radioactive
for far longer than a human lifetime.

The waste also produces heat for years after it leaves the reactor, so it must be
cooled in a pool of water and then shielded. In the long term it is sealed in solid
containers and buried deep underground in a stable rock formation, where it must
stay isolated from people for thousands of years. No institution has yet lasted
that long, which is what makes the problem hard.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Two secondary sources give different figures for the cost of electricity from
a nuclear power station. Describe what you would check before deciding which figure
to trust.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about who wrote each source, when it was written, what it counted, and whether
anyone else agrees.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Check the author and their interest, the date, the assumptions behind the figure,
and whether an independent source gives the same number.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

First, check who wrote each source and what they gain from the conclusion. A report
from the operator and a report from a group that opposes nuclear power may both
quote true numbers and choose different ones.

Second, check the date. A figure from twenty years ago describes a different world,
especially for wind and solar, whose costs have fallen.

Third, check the assumptions. A cost per unit of electricity depends on the lifetime
of the plant, the interest rate and whether the cost of waste disposal is included.
Two figures are comparable only when the same items are counted in both.

Finally, check whether an independent source agrees. If several separate
organisations reach the same number, it is more likely to be reliable. If only one
does, that must be said.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain why a nuclear power reactor cannot explode like a nuclear weapon.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the enrichment of the fuel, and think about how the chain reaction is held.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Reactor fuel is enriched far less than weapon material, and the control rods hold
the chain reaction steady.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A weapon needs fuel that is highly enriched in uranium-235, so that the chain
reaction grows extremely quickly. Reactor fuel is enriched far less, and much of it
is uranium-238, which does not fission in this way. The chain reaction is held
steady by the control rods and by the design of the core, so it cannot grow in the
way a weapon does.

The serious accident risk in a reactor is different: a loss of cooling can melt the
fuel and release radioactive material into the surroundings. That is a meltdown, not
a nuclear explosion, and the two must not be confused.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 4

**7.** A power station delivers an output power of 500 MW. Calculate the electrical
energy it delivers in one day. Take 1 day $= 8.64 \times 10^4$ s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Energy is power multiplied by time. Change 500 MW to watts first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = Pt = 500 \times 10^6 \times 8.64 \times 10^4$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = Pt = 500 \times 10^6 \times 8.64 \times 10^4 = 4.32 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}$$

So the station delivers about $4.3 \times 10^{13}$ J in a day. Compare that with the
$8.2 \times 10^{13}$ J in one kilogram of uranium-235: the fission of about two
kilograms of fuel could power the station for a whole day.

</details>
</details>
</details>
