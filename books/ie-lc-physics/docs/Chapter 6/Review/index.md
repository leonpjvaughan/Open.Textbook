# Chapter 6: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The satellite over Ireland.** A communications satellite of mass 1200 kg sits in a
geostationary orbit $4.22 \times 10^{7}\,\text{m}$ from the centre of the Earth. The
Earth has a mass of $5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and a radius of
$6.37 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$.

**Step 1: mass and weight.** The mass is 1200 kg on the launch pad and 1200 kg in
orbit. Mass does not change. The weight does change, because the field strength
changes.

On the launch pad, with $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$:

$$W = mg = 1200 \times 9.8 = 1.2 \times 10^{4}\,\text{N}$$

**Step 2: the field strength at the orbit.** Use the field form of the law, with the
distance measured from the centre:

$$g = \frac{GM}{r^2} = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24}}{(4.22 \times 10^{7})^2} = 0.224\,\text{N/kg}$$

The weight in orbit follows from the same mass:

$$W = mg = 1200 \times 0.224 = 2.7 \times 10^{2}\,\text{N}$$

The weight has fallen by a factor of about 44. The ratio of the two weights is the
square of the ratio of the two distances from the centre,
$\left(\dfrac{4.22 \times 10^{7}}{6.37 \times 10^{6}}\right)^2 = 44$, which is what an
inverse-square law predicts.

**Step 3: the force between the Earth and the satellite.** The force in orbit is the
weight in orbit, because $W = mg$ and $g = F/m$ say the same thing:

$$F = \frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24} \times 1200}{(4.22 \times 10^{7})^2} = 2.7 \times 10^{2}\,\text{N}$$

**Step 4: the energy to lift it.** The potential energy is negative at both distances,
so use the difference:

$$\Delta E_p = GMm\left(\frac{1}{R} - \frac{1}{r}\right)$$

$$\Delta E_p = 3.98 \times 10^{14} \times 1200 \times \left(\frac{1}{6.37 \times 10^{6}} - \frac{1}{4.22 \times 10^{7}}\right) = 6.4 \times 10^{10}\,\text{J}$$

That is about 64 000 MJ. The energy goes in to raise the satellite, which is why the
answer is positive even though both potential energies are negative.

**Step 5: the speed and the period.** Gravity provides the centripetal force, so
$\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ and the mass cancels:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{4.22 \times 10^{7}}} = 3.1 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m/s}$$

$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi \times 4.22 \times 10^{7}}{3.1 \times 10^{3}} = 8.6 \times 10^{4}\,\text{s}$$

That is 24 hours, so the satellite turns once while the Earth turns once and stays
above the same point. It must also sit above the equator and travel west to east, or
it would drift out of position.

**The answer.** The satellite weighs $1.2 \times 10^{4}\,\text{N}$ on the pad and
$2.7 \times 10^{2}\,\text{N}$ in orbit. The force holding it there is
$2.7 \times 10^{2}\,\text{N}$, the energy to lift it is
$6.4 \times 10^{10}\,\text{J}$, and it travels at
$3.1 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m/s}$ with a period of 24 hours. Nothing about it is
weightless: it is falling around the Earth rather than into it.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies**
and say why. Two of them ask you to choose between the energy form for a short climb
and the energy form for a long one.

1. A 3.0 kg mass is raised 4.0 m near the Earth's surface. Calculate the gain in
   potential energy. Would $mgh$ still be accurate on the space station?
2. The Earth's mass is $5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$. Calculate the field strength
   $2.0 \times 10^{7}\,\text{m}$ from its centre.
3. Two masses attract each other with a force of 24 N. Calculate the force when the
   distance between their centres is halved.
4. A satellite orbits at $r = 1.0 \times 10^{7}\,\text{m}$. Calculate its speed and
   its period.
5. Explain why a satellite 400 km above the equator is not geostationary, even though
   it is high above the Earth.
6. A spring of constant 200 N/m is stretched by 0.15 m. Calculate the energy stored.
   Which earlier chapter does that energy belong to, and why is it not gravitational?
7. Calculate the escape velocity from a planet with the same radius as the Earth but
   twice its mass.
8. A ball is dropped from rest and falls 20 m. Calculate its speed when it lands,
   taking $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^{2}$. Which chapter does that method come from?
9. An astronaut of mass 70 kg stands on the Moon, where $g = 1.6\,\text{N/kg}$.
   Calculate her weight there, and compare it with her weight on the Earth.
10. You must find the mass of a planet. You can measure the radius of a moon's orbit
    and its period. Which relationship would you use, and what else do you need to
    know?

## What you can now do

- tell mass from weight, and use $W = mg$ with the mass in kilograms;
- define gravitational field strength and use $g = F/m$;
- use Newton's law of universal gravitation and explain the inverse-square law;
- find the field strength at a planet's surface and at a height above it, adding the
  radius to the height;
- find gravitational potential energy near a surface and in a field, keeping the minus
  sign;
- calculate the energy needed to lift a mass to a great height, and the escape velocity
  from a planet;
- find the orbital speed and period of a satellite with $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$ and
  $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v}$;
- use Kepler's third law, $T^2 \propto r^3$, to compare two orbits;
- explain why a geostationary satellite stays above one point, and why an astronaut in
  orbit feels weightless.

## Thinking back

Look at the thing in the sky you chose at the start of the chapter — a satellite dish,
a weather forecast, a satnav route, a phone call.

You can now say what keeps the satellite above one point. The period fixes the radius,
the equator fixes the latitude, and the field strength at that radius fixes the speed.

Write down one number for your own choice: the height of the orbit, the period, or the
speed. Then work out what would happen if it were different. If your number is a rough
estimate, write that down too. Saying how rough an estimate is belongs in the physics,
and it is exactly what the Physics In Practice Investigation asks you to do.
