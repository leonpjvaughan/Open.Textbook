# 6.2 Newton's law of universal gravitation — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A 3.0 kg mass has a weight of 29.4 N on a planet. Calculate the gravitational field
   strength at the surface.
2. Write down the unit of gravitational field strength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $g = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{29.4}{3.0} = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.
2. The newton per kilogram, N/kg.

Both return here. The field strength is what the law of gravitation explains, and its
unit is the one that falls out of $g = F/m$.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Two spheres, each of mass 1000 kg, have their centres 2.0 m apart. Calculate the
gravitational force between them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $F = \dfrac{Gm_1m_2}{d^2}$ with $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = \dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1000 \times 1000}{2.0^2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 10^{6}}{4.0} = 1.7 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{N}$$

where $F$ is the force between the masses, $m_1$ and $m_2$ their masses, $d$ the
distance between their centres and $G$ the gravitational constant.

That is a tiny force. $G$ is very small, so two everyday masses pull each other by an
amount you cannot feel.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** The Earth has a mass of $5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and the Moon has a mass
of $7.35 \times 10^{22}\,\text{kg}$. Their centres are $3.84 \times 10^{8}\,\text{m}$
apart. Calculate the force between them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Put all three values into the law. Take care with the powers of ten.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = \dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24} \times 7.35 \times 10^{22}}{(3.84 \times 10^{8})^2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = \frac{2.927 \times 10^{37}}{1.475 \times 10^{17}} = 1.98 \times 10^{20}\,\text{N}$$

This is the force that keeps the Moon in its orbit. The Moon pulls the Earth with the
same size of force, because the two forces are a Newton's third law pair.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Two masses attract each other with a force of 64 N. Calculate the force between
them when the distance between their centres is doubled.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The force depends on $1/d^2$. What does doubling $d$ do to the force?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The force is divided by $2^2 = 4$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F_{\text{new}} = \frac{64}{4} = 16\,\text{N}$$

Doubling the distance quarters the force. An answer of 32 N comes from treating the law
as an inverse relationship rather than an inverse-square one.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** The force between two masses is 90 N. Calculate the force when the distance is
tripled.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide by $3^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F_{\text{new}} = \dfrac{90}{9}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F_{\text{new}} = \frac{90}{9} = 10\,\text{N}$$

The distance is three times bigger, so the force is nine times smaller.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Calculate the gravitational field strength at the surface of the Earth. Take
$M = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and $R = 6.37 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $g = \dfrac{GM}{d^2}$ with $d = R$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$g = \dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24}}{(6.37 \times 10^{6})^2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$g = \frac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{4.06 \times 10^{13}} = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$$

That is the value of $g$ you have used since Chapter 2. The law of gravitation explains
where it comes from.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A satellite orbits 400 km above the Earth's surface. Calculate the distance from
the centre of the Earth, then the field strength there.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

First add the height to the radius. Keep both distances in metres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$d = 6.37 \times 10^{6} + 4.00 \times 10^{5}$, then use $g = \dfrac{GM}{d^2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$d = 6.37 \times 10^{6} + 0.40 \times 10^{6} = 6.77 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$$

$$g = \frac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{(6.77 \times 10^{6})^2} = \frac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{4.58 \times 10^{13}} = 8.7\,\text{N/kg}$$

The field strength at the orbit is $8.7\,\text{N/kg}$, lower than the surface value but
still large. A satellite there is not outside gravity.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** A student calculates the force on a satellite 400 km above the Earth by using
$d = 4.00 \times 10^{5}\,\text{m}$. Explain why the answer is far too large.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What does $d$ measure in the law?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$d$ is the distance between the centres, not the height above the surface.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The law needs the distance between the **centres** of the two masses. The student used
the height above the surface instead, which is about 16 times too small.

Because the force depends on $1/d^2$, a distance 16 times too small makes the force
about $16^2 = 256$ times too large. The correct distance is
$6.77 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 6.1

**8.** A 0.60 kg ball has a weight of 0.96 N on the Moon. Calculate the gravitational
field strength at the Moon's surface.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

This is $g = F/m$ from Section 6.1. Section 6.2 could also give it from the Moon's mass
and radius.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$g = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{0.96}{0.60} = 1.6\,\text{N/kg}$$

The Moon's mass is about 81 times smaller than the Earth's and its radius is about 3.7
times smaller. The smaller radius partly cancels the smaller mass, which is why the
Moon's field strength is 1.6 N/kg rather than zero.

</details>
</details>
</details>
