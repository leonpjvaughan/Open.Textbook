# 6.1 Weight, mass and gravitational field strength — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the unit of force, and what is the unit of mass?
2. State Newton's second law as an equation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. Force is measured in newtons (N). Mass is measured in kilograms (kg).
2. $F = ma$. A resultant force $F$ on a mass $m$ gives an acceleration $a$.

Both ideas return in this section. A weight is a force, so it is measured in newtons.
The field strength $g$ plays the part of the acceleration in $F = ma$.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A bag of flour has a mass of 1.5 kg. Calculate its weight on the Earth, taking
$g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $W = mg$. The mass is already in kilograms.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 1.5 \times 9.8$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mg = 1.5 \times 9.8 = 14.7\,\text{N}$$

To two significant figures, the weight is $15\,\text{N}$.

The unit check: $\text{kg} \times \text{N/kg} = \text{N}$. Correct.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A student has a mass of 55 kg. Calculate her weight on the Earth.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

$W = mg$ again, with $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 55 \times 9.8$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = 55 \times 9.8 = 539\,\text{N}$$

To two significant figures, that is $5.4 \times 10^{2}\,\text{N}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** The same student of mass 55 kg stands on the Moon, where
$g = 1.6\,\text{N/kg}$. Calculate her weight there, and say what has changed and what
has not.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The mass does not change. Put the new $g$ into $W = mg$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 55 \times 1.6$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = 55 \times 1.6 = 88\,\text{N}$$

Her mass is still 55 kg, because mass is the amount of matter and that does not
change. Her weight has fallen from 539 N to 88 N, because the Moon pulls on each
kilogram less strongly.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A 4.0 kg box has a weight of 6.4 N on a moon of Saturn. Calculate the
gravitational field strength at its surface.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You are given a force and a mass and asked for $g$. Rearrange $g = F/m$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$g = \dfrac{6.4}{4.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$g = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{6.4}{4.0} = 1.6\,\text{N/kg}$$

That is the same field strength as the Moon's surface. The two bodies pull on each
kilogram by the same amount.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A student says: "My mass is 55 kg, so my weight is 55 N." Explain what is wrong
with the statement.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What does each number measure, and what unit does each one carry?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Mass is in kilograms; weight is a force, so it is in newtons. They are not the same
number.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The number 55 is a mass in kilograms, not a weight. Weight is a force, so it is in
newtons, and it equals the mass multiplied by the field strength.

On the Earth, $W = 55 \times 9.8 = 539\,\text{N}$, not 55 N. The two quantities are
equal in size only where $g = 1\,\text{N/kg}$, which is nowhere in the Solar System.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A 0.50 kg stone is taken from the Earth to the Moon. State its mass and its
weight on the Moon, taking $g = 1.6\,\text{N/kg}$, and explain the difference in one
sentence.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the weight first. Then decide which of the two quantities cannot change.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 0.50 \times 1.6 = 0.80\,\text{N}$. The mass stays 0.50 kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The mass is 0.50 kg on the Moon, exactly as on the Earth. The weight is
$0.50 \times 1.6 = 0.80\,\text{N}$.

Mass is the amount of matter, so it does not depend on place. Weight is the
gravitational force, and the Moon pulls on each kilogram with a smaller force than the
Earth does.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** A rocket engine gives a 1200 kg satellite an upward acceleration of
$2.0\,\text{m/s}^{2}$ at launch. Calculate the resultant force on it, using
$F = ma$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

This is Newton's second law, not the weight formula. Use the acceleration, not $g$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = 1200 \times 2.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = ma = 1200 \times 2.0 = 2400\,\text{N}$$

This force is the **resultant**, the engine thrust minus the weight. The engine
therefore has to give more than 2400 N, because gravity pulls the satellite down as
well. That difference is the whole reason a launch is hard, and it returns in
Section 6.3.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 3

**8.** A resultant force of 18 N acts on a 6.0 kg trolley. Calculate the acceleration
of the trolley.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $F = ma$ and rearrange it for $a$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{18}{6.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{18}{6.0} = 3.0\,\text{m/s}^{2}$$

Notice how similar this is to $g = F/m$. The field strength is the acceleration a
mass would have if gravity were the only force on it, which is why the two share a
unit once you convert N/kg to m/s².

</details>
</details>
</details>
