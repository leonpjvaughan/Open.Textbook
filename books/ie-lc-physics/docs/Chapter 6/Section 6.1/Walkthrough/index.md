# 6.1 Weight, mass and gravitational field strength — Learn

**Prerequisites:** force and Newton's laws from Chapter 3; units and prefixes from
Chapter 1.

**Objectives:** tell mass from weight; define gravitational field strength; use
$W = mg$ and $g = F/m$; compare the weight of an object on the Earth and on another
body.

By the end of this section, you can say what a mass and a weight each measure, and
work out either one from the other.

## Mass and weight are different quantities

Hold a bag of sugar. It has a mass of 1 kg, and it feels heavy. The mass is the amount
of matter in it. The heaviness is the gravitational force pulling it down, and that
force is its weight.

- **Mass** is the amount of matter in an object. It is measured in kilograms (kg).
  Mass is the same everywhere: a 1 kg mass is 1 kg on the Earth, on the Moon and in
  deep space.
- **Weight** is the gravitational force on the object. It is measured in newtons (N).
  Weight depends on where the object is. One kilogram weighs 9.8 N on the Earth and
  about 1.6 N on the Moon.

Weight is a force, so it has a direction. It points towards the centre of the body
that pulls the object.

## Gravitational field strength

Why does 1 kg weigh 9.8 N here and 1.6 N on the Moon? The Moon pulls on each kilogram
less strongly. That pull per kilogram is the **gravitational field strength**.

> **Gravitational field strength.** The gravitational field strength at a point is the
> gravitational force per unit mass at that point.

$$g = \frac{F}{m}$$

- $g$ is the gravitational field strength, in newtons per kilogram (N/kg);
- $F$ is the gravitational force, in newtons (N);
- $m$ is the mass, in kilograms (kg).

At the Earth's surface, $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$. At the Moon's surface, $g$ is about
$1.6\,\text{N/kg}$.

Rearranging $g = F/m$ for the force gives the rule for weight:

$$W = mg$$

**Example 1.** A student has a mass of 60 kg. Calculate her weight on the Earth.

$$
W = mg
$$

$$
W = 60 \times 9.8
$$

$$
W = 588\,\text{N}
$$

To two significant figures, that is $5.9 \times 10^{2}\,\text{N}$.

**Example 2.** Calculate the weight of the same student on the Moon, where
$g = 1.6\,\text{N/kg}$.

$$
W = mg
$$

$$
W = 60 \times 1.6
$$

$$
W = 96\,\text{N}
$$

Her mass is still 60 kg. Only the weight changed.

**Example 3.** A 2.0 kg mass has a weight of 19.6 N on a planet. Calculate the
gravitational field strength at the surface of that planet.

$$
g = \frac{F}{m}
$$

$$
g = \frac{19.6}{2.0}
$$

$$
g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}
$$

The planet has the same field strength as the Earth at its surface.

> **Watch out.** The mass goes into the formula in **kilograms**, not grams. A 250 g
> bar has a mass of 0.25 kg, so its weight is $0.25 \times 9.8 = 2.5\,\text{N}$. The
> answer $250 \times 9.8 = 2450\,\text{N}$ comes from using the number of grams. If
> your weight for a small object comes out in thousands of newtons, check the units
> of the mass first.

## Field strength and acceleration

The two units for $g$ say the same thing. A newton per kilogram is a metre per second
squared:

$$\frac{\text{N}}{\text{kg}} = \frac{\text{kg m/s}^{2}}{\text{kg}} = \text{m/s}^{2}$$

So $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$ is also $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^{2}$. That is the acceleration
of an object in free fall, which you met in Chapter 2. Both descriptions are the same
physics: the field pulls each kilogram with a force of 9.8 N, so each kilogram
accelerates at $9.8\,\text{m/s}^{2}$.

## Comparing places

The mass of an object never changes. The weight changes whenever $g$ changes.

| Place | Field strength $g$ (N/kg) | Weight of a 60 kg student (N) |
|---|---|---|
| Earth | 9.8 | 588 |
| Moon | 1.6 | 96 |
| Mars | 3.7 | 222 |

The Moon is smaller than the Earth and has less mass, so its pull at the surface is
weaker. Mars sits between the two. Section 6.2 shows how to work out $g$ for a planet
from its mass and radius.

1. Calculate its weight on the launch pad, where $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.
2. Write one sentence explaining why its mass is still 1200 kg once it is in orbit.
3. Write a second sentence saying what the field strength at the orbit would tell you

## You should now be able to

- say what mass measures and what weight measures, with the unit of each;
- define gravitational field strength and state its unit;
- use $g = F/m$ and $W = mg$ in either direction;
- explain why a mass in kilograms must never be written in grams in these formulas;
- compare the weight of an object on the Earth with its weight on another body, and
  say which part of it changes.
