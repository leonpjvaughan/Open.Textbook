# 6.3 Gravitational potential energy in a field — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Calculate the gain in potential energy when a 3.0 kg mass is raised 2.0 m near the
   Earth's surface.
2. Write down the formula for kinetic energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $E_p = mgh = 3.0 \times 9.8 \times 2.0 = 58.8\,\text{J}$.
2. $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$.

Both return in this section. The near-surface energy uses a constant $g$; the field
energy does not. The kinetic energy formula is what turns an escape speed into an
energy.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A 5.0 kg mass is raised 12 m near the Earth's surface. Calculate the gain in
gravitational potential energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The height is small, so use $E_p = mgh$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E_p = 5.0 \times 9.8 \times 12$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_p = mgh = 5.0 \times 9.8 \times 12 = 588\,\text{J}$$

To two significant figures, that is $5.9 \times 10^{2}\,\text{J}$. Over 12 m the field
strength hardly changes, so the simple formula is safe here.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Calculate the gravitational potential energy of a 1.0 kg mass at the Earth's
surface. Take $M = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\text{kg}$ and $R = 6.37 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E_p = -\dfrac{GMm}{r}$ with $r = R$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E_p = -\dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24} \times 1.0}{6.37 \times 10^{6}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_p = -\frac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{6.37 \times 10^{6}} = -6.25 \times 10^{7}\,\text{J}$$

The energy is negative because the zero is at infinity. A 1 kg mass at the surface is
bound to the Earth, and you would have to add $6.25 \times 10^{7}\,\text{J}$ to remove
it completely.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Calculate the energy needed to lift the 1200 kg satellite from the Earth's
surface to an orbit $4.22 \times 10^{7}\,\text{m}$ from the centre.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the potential energy at both distances and subtract. The larger distance has the
less negative energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\Delta E_p = GMm\left(\dfrac{1}{R} - \dfrac{1}{r}\right)$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_{p,\text{surface}} = -\frac{3.98 \times 10^{14} \times 1200}{6.37 \times 10^{6}} = -7.50 \times 10^{10}\,\text{J}$$

$$E_{p,\text{orbit}} = -\frac{3.98 \times 10^{14} \times 1200}{4.22 \times 10^{7}} = -1.13 \times 10^{10}\,\text{J}$$

$$\Delta E_p = (-1.13 \times 10^{10}) - (-7.50 \times 10^{10}) = 6.4 \times 10^{10}\,\text{J}$$

The answer is positive, as it must be: energy goes in to lift the satellite. Notice
that the two potential energies are both negative, and the difference is positive.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Calculate the escape velocity from the Moon. Take
$M = 7.35 \times 10^{22}\,\text{kg}$ and $R = 1.74 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{R}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 7.35 \times 10^{22}}{1.74 \times 10^{6}}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{5.63 \times 10^{6}} = 2.4 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m/s}$$

That is 2.4 km/s, less than a quarter of the Earth's escape velocity. It is still fast
for a rocket, which is why the Apollo missions needed a large rocket to leave the Moon
as well as to reach it.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** The escape velocity from the Earth is $1.1 \times 10^{4}\,\text{m/s}$. Calculate
the escape velocity from a planet with twice the Earth's mass and the same radius.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Write down how $v_{\text{esc}}$ depends on $M$ and on $R$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v_{\text{esc}} \propto \sqrt{M}$, and $R$ has not changed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} \propto \sqrt{M}$$

Doubling $M$ multiplies the escape velocity by $\sqrt{2} = 1.41$:

$$v_{\text{esc}} = 1.41 \times 1.1 \times 10^{4} = 1.6 \times 10^{4}\,\text{m/s}$$

Note that the escape velocity depends on the mass of the planet, not on the mass of the
rocket. Doubling the rocket's mass changes nothing.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain why the gravitational potential energy of a mass in the Earth's field
is negative.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Where is the zero of energy, and which way does a mass move if you let it go?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The zero is at infinity. A mass left alone falls towards the Earth.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The potential energy is defined as zero at infinity, where the gravitational pull has
faded to nothing.

A mass released near the Earth falls towards it, so its energy must fall. If the value
at infinity is zero and the value at the Earth is lower, the value at the Earth must be
negative. A negative value means the mass is bound: you must supply energy to bring it
up to zero and let it escape.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** Explain why $E_p = mgh$ cannot be used to find the energy needed to lift a
satellite to a geostationary orbit.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which quantity in $E_p = mgh$ is assumed to be constant, and is it?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$g$ is treated as constant in $mgh$. At the orbit $g$ has fallen to 0.22 N/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The formula $E_p = mgh$ treats $g$ as constant. That is a good approximation over a few
metres, where the field strength changes by a tiny fraction.

Over 36 000 km the field strength falls from 9.8 N/kg to about 0.22 N/kg, so the weight
of the satellite changes by a factor of about 45 on the way up. There is no single
force to multiply by the height.

The field form, $\Delta E_p = GMm\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right)$, handles the
change. Using $mgh$ with the surface value of $g$ would give an answer far too large,
because it keeps the full surface weight for the whole climb.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 6.2

**8.** Calculate the gravitational field strength $400\,\text{km}$ above the Earth's
surface, using $g = \dfrac{GM}{d^2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the height to the radius first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$d = 6.77 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$, then
$g = \dfrac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{(6.77 \times 10^{6})^2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$g = \frac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{4.58 \times 10^{13}} = 8.7\,\text{N/kg}$$

This is why $mgh$ nearly works for a laboratory height and fails for an orbit: at
400 km the field strength has already fallen by about 11%, and it keeps falling.

</details>
</details>
</details>
