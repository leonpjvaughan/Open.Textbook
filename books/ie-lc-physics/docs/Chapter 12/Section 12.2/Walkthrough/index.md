# 12.2 The speed of light — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the wave equation $v = f\lambda$ from Section 9.2; the
refractive index and the speed of light in a medium from Section 10.2.

**Objectives:** state the speed of light in a vacuum; use $c = f\lambda$; explain
why every electromagnetic wave has the same speed in a vacuum; calculate the time
light takes to travel a distance; explain the light year.

By the end of this section, you can work out how long light takes to cross a
distance, and you can find the frequency or the wavelength of any
electromagnetic wave.

## One speed for the whole family

In a vacuum, every electromagnetic wave travels at the same speed, whatever its
frequency. That speed is called the **speed of light**:

$$c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$$

A radio wave, a microwave, visible light and a gamma ray all travel at exactly
this speed in a vacuum. In air the speed is only a little lower, so we use the
same value for air.

In a material such as glass or water, the light travels more slowly. The
**refractive index** compares the two speeds:

$$n = \frac{c}{v}$$

- $c$ is the speed of light in a vacuum, in metres per second (m/s);
- $v$ is the speed of light in the material, in metres per second (m/s).

A refractive index greater than 1 means the light is slower in the material. The
refractive index has no unit, because it is a ratio of two speeds.

## The wave equation for light

Light is a wave, so it obeys the wave equation from Chapter 9:

$$c = f\lambda$$

- $c$ is the speed of light, in metres per second (m/s);
- $f$ is the frequency, in hertz (Hz);
- $\lambda$ is the wavelength, in metres (m).

Because $c$ is the same for every family, the frequency and the wavelength are
locked together: a short wavelength means a high frequency, and a long wavelength
means a low frequency.

**Example 1.** The Sun is $1.5 \times 10^{11}$ m from the Earth. Calculate the
time sunlight takes to reach the Earth.

$$t = \frac{d}{c} = \frac{1.5 \times 10^{11}}{3.0 \times 10^{8}} = 500\,\text{s}$$

where $t$ is the time the light takes and $d$ is the distance it travels.

That is 8.3 minutes. The sunlight you see left the Sun about eight minutes ago.

**Example 2.** A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz. Calculate the wavelength of
the radio waves.

First change the frequency into hertz: $100\,\text{MHz} = 1.0 \times 10^{8}$ Hz.
Then rearrange $c = f\lambda$:

$$\lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{8}}{1.0 \times 10^{8}}
= 3.0\,\text{m}$$

**Example 3.** Light travels through a vacuum at $3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.
Calculate its speed in a glass of refractive index 1.50.

$$v = \frac{c}{n} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{8}}{1.50} = 2.0 \times 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$$

## The light year

The distances between stars are enormous, so astronomers use a larger unit. A
**light year** is the distance light travels through a vacuum in one year.

One year is about $3.15 \times 10^{7}$ s, so

$$1\,\text{light year} = c \times t = 3.0 \times 10^{8} \times 3.15 \times 10^{7}
= 9.5 \times 10^{15}\,\text{m}$$

That is about $9.5 \times 10^{12}$ km. The nearest star after the Sun, Proxima
Centauri, is about 4.2 light years away, so its light takes 4.2 years to reach
us.

A light year is a **distance**, not a time. The name is misleading, so check the
unit in your answer.

## How the speed was measured

For a long time nobody knew whether light travelled instantly or took time. Two
measurements settled it.

- In 1676 the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer timed the eclipses of Io, a moon of
  Jupiter. The timing drifted as the Earth moved nearer to and further from
  Jupiter. The drift matched the extra distance the light had to travel, so light
  must take time to cross space.
- In 1849 the French physicist Hippolyte Fizeau sent a beam of light through a
  gap in a spinning toothed wheel to a mirror and back. The returning beam was
  blocked by a tooth for some wheel speeds and passed for others. The wheel speed
  and the distance gave the speed of light.

Modern measurements use the frequency and the wavelength of a stable laser, with
$c = f\lambda$. The value $3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s is accurate enough for this
course.

> **Watch out.** Do not mix up the speed of a wave with its frequency or its
> wavelength. When light enters glass, the speed drops and the wavelength
> shortens, but the **frequency does not change**. The frequency is set by the
> source. If you use a new speed with the old frequency and expect the old
> wavelength, the numbers will not fit.

## You should now be able to

- state the speed of light in a vacuum, and say what is special about it;
- use $n = \dfrac{c}{v}$ to compare the speed in a material with the speed in a
  vacuum;
- use $c = f\lambda$ to find a speed, a frequency or a wavelength;
- calculate the time light takes to travel a distance;
- explain what a light year is, and why it is a distance;
- describe one way the speed of light was measured.
