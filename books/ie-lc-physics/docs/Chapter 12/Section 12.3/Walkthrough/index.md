# 12.3 Energy in electromagnetic radiation — Learn

**Prerequisites:** energy and power from Chapter 4; the wave equation for light,
$c = f\lambda$, from Section 12.2.

**Objectives:** explain that electromagnetic radiation carries energy; define
irradiance and use $I = \dfrac{P}{A}$; state the solar constant; calculate a power
and an energy from an irradiance; explain why irradiance falls with the square of
the distance; explain why high-frequency radiation is ionising.

By the end of this section, you can work out how much power and how much energy a
surface receives from electromagnetic radiation.

## Radiation carries energy

You can feel the energy in sunlight on your face. A solar panel turns that energy
into electrical energy, and a plant uses it to make food. Electromagnetic
radiation carries energy from one place to another, just as a sound wave does.

The amount of energy arriving each second is a **power**, measured in watts. A
surface does not receive all of that power unless it covers the whole beam, so we
compare surfaces using the power falling on each square metre.

## Irradiance

The **irradiance** is the power of the radiation falling on unit area.

$$I = \frac{P}{A}$$

- $I$ is the irradiance, in watts per square metre (W/m$^2$);
- $P$ is the power falling on the surface, in watts (W);
- $A$ is the area of the surface, in square metres (m$^2$).

The unit is the watt per square metre. Irradiance is not the same as power. A
small panel and a large panel in the same sunlight have the same irradiance, but
the large panel receives more power.

**Example 1.** A solar panel has an area of 2.0 m$^2$. The irradiance of the
sunlight on it is 1000 W/m$^2$. Calculate the power the panel receives.

$$P = IA = 1000 \times 2.0 = 2000\,\text{W}$$

That is 2.0 kW. The unit works out as well:
$\text{W/m}^2 \times \text{m}^2 = \text{W}$.

**Example 2.** The same panel receives this power for 5.0 hours. Calculate the
energy it receives.

Five hours is $5.0 \times 3600 = 18000$ s. Then

$$E = Pt = 2000 \times 18000 = 3.6 \times 10^{7}\,\text{J}$$

where $E$ is the energy transferred, $P$ the power and $t$ the time.

The hours must be turned into seconds first, because the watt is a joule per
second.

## The solar constant

The Sun radiates energy in every direction, and only a tiny share of it reaches
the Earth. The **solar constant** is the irradiance of the Sun at the Earth,
measured above the atmosphere. It is about

$$1.4\,\text{kW/m}^2$$

At ground level the value is lower, because the atmosphere absorbs and scatters
some of the radiation. On a clear day in Ireland the irradiance on a flat surface
facing the Sun is often about 1000 W/m$^2$, which is the value used in the chapter
problem.

## Irradiance and distance

A lamp sends its energy out in all directions. Imagine a sphere around the lamp.
All the power crosses the surface of that sphere, and the area of a sphere grows
with the square of its radius. So the same power is spread more thinly as you
move away.

> **The inverse square law.** For a point source, the irradiance falls with the
> square of the distance from the source.
>
>
> $$I \propto \frac{1}{d^2}$$

Double the distance and the irradiance falls to a quarter. Treble the distance
and it falls to a ninth.

**Example 3.** The irradiance of the sunlight at the Earth is 1000 W/m$^2$.
Calculate the irradiance at a distance twice as far from the Sun.

$$I_2 = I_1 \left(\frac{d_1}{d_2}\right)^2 = 1000 \times \left(\frac{1.0}{2.0}\right)^2
= 1000 \times \frac{1}{4} = 250\,\text{W/m}^2$$

where $I_1$ and $I_2$ are the irradiances at distances $d_1$ and $d_2$ from the
source, and $d$ is the distance from the source.

## Energy in one photon

Electromagnetic radiation arrives in packets called **photons**. The energy in one
photon depends on its frequency: a higher frequency means more energy per photon.

- A gamma-ray photon carries far more energy than a radio photon.
- A photon with enough energy can knock an electron out of an atom. Radiation that
  can do this is **ionising**.

This is why the short-wavelength families are the dangerous ones. X-rays and gamma
rays are strongly ionising, and ultraviolet is ionising but less strongly. Radio
waves, microwaves, infrared and visible light do not have enough energy per photon
to ionise an atom. The relationship between the energy of a photon and its
frequency is developed in a later chapter.

> **Watch out.** Keep irradiance and power apart. Irradiance is measured in
> W/m$^2$ and power in W. A question about a panel usually gives you the
> irradiance and the area, and asks for the power, so multiply them. The inverse
> square law applies to a small source far away, such as a lamp. It does not mean
> the irradiance varies across a solar panel, because the Sun is so far away that
> its rays are almost parallel.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The school roof panel is 2.0 m long and 1.0 m wide.
The irradiance of the sunlight is 1000 W/m$^2$.

1. Calculate the area of the panel.
2. Calculate the power the panel receives.
3. Calculate the energy the panel receives in 5.0 hours.
4. The student says the panel would work just as well if it were twice as far
   from the Sun. Explain why this is wrong, and state what the irradiance would
   be at twice the distance.

Keep your answers. Section 12.4 uses a lens to concentrate the light.

## You should now be able to

- explain that electromagnetic radiation carries energy;
- define irradiance and use $I = \dfrac{P}{A}$ in any direction;
- state the solar constant and explain why the value at the ground is lower;
- calculate a power and an energy from an irradiance;
- explain the inverse square law for a point source, and use a ratio of distances;
- explain why high-frequency radiation is ionising.
