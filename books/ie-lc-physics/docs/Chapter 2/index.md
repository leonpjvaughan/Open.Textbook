# Chapter 2: Describing motion

A dropped stone, a sprinting runner and a braking bus all move in a straight line. Each
one can be described by the same three quantities: where it is, how fast it is going, and
how quickly its speed is changing. In this chapter you learn to measure those quantities
and to read them from a graph.

## The chapter problem

**The drone delivery.** A delivery drone lifts off from a field. It starts from rest and
speeds up at 2.0 m/s$^2$ for 4.0 s. It then slows down at 4.0 m/s$^2$ until it stops. At the
top of its rise it releases a parcel, which falls from rest.

By the end of the chapter you can describe the whole journey with one set of equations,
read the height from a velocity-time graph, and work out how long the parcel takes to
reach the ground and how fast it lands.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- tell a scalar from a vector, and explain the difference between distance and
  displacement;
- calculate an average speed, an average velocity and an acceleration;
- read a distance-time graph and a velocity-time graph for their gradient and their
  area;
- choose and use the equations of motion for constant acceleration;
- describe free fall, and use $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$ in the equations of motion;
- derive the equations of motion from a velocity-time graph.

## Where it is used

Speed limits and stopping distances, sprint times, lifts and cranes, a phone falling from
a pocket, and the parachute that opens on a skydive. Every later chapter about forces
uses the language of motion set out here.

## Your turn to connect it

Think of a journey you make often. A bus to school, a cycle to a match, a lift to the top
floor, or a basketball falling through a hoop.

Choose one. Write down where it speeds up, where it moves steadily and where it slows
down. Keep it. By the end of the chapter you can sketch the velocity-time graph of that
journey and read a distance from it.
