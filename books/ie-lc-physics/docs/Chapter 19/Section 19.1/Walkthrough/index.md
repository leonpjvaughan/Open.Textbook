# 19.1 From Thomson to Rutherford — Learn

**Prerequisites:** electric charge and electric fields from Chapter 13; the electron
from Section 17.1; scientific models from Chapter 1.

**Objectives:** describe Thomson's model of the atom; describe the alpha-particle
scattering experiment; explain what the observations show; state the nuclear model and
compare the size of the nucleus with the size of the atom.

By the end of this section, you can describe the experiment that revealed the nucleus
and explain what each observation means.

## The first model of the atom

By 1900 the electron was known. An atom is neutral overall, so the rest of the atom
must carry positive charge.

J. J. Thomson proposed that the atom is a ball of positive charge, with the electrons
spread through it. This is **Thomson's model** of the atom. It is sometimes called the
plum-pudding model.

In this model the positive charge fills the whole atom. It is not gathered in one
place. That detail is what the next experiment tested.

## The scattering experiment

In 1909 Ernest Rutherford worked with Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden. They fired
**alpha particles** at a very thin sheet of gold foil.

An alpha particle is a helium nucleus. It carries a positive charge of $+2e$, and it
is heavy compared with an electron.

The apparatus had three parts.

- A source of alpha particles, aimed at the foil.
- A sheet of gold foil, only a few hundred atoms thick.
- A fluorescent screen that gives a tiny flash where an alpha particle strikes it.

The screen could be moved around the foil. That let the team count the alpha particles
that came off at any angle.

If Thomson's model were right, the positive charge would be spread thinly through the
whole atom. An alpha particle would then feel only a weak force, so it would pass
through with a small deflection at most.

## What they saw

The team found three things.

- Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil, with no deflection.
- Some were deflected through large angles.
- A very few bounced straight back towards the source.

The last observation was the surprise. A particle that bounces back must have hit
something very small, very heavy and very positive.

## What the observations mean

Each observation points to one feature of the atom.

- Most particles pass straight through, so most of the atom is **empty space**.
- Some are deflected through large angles, so they pass close to a **concentrated
  positive charge**.
- A very few bounce back, so the positive charge and almost all of the mass sit in a
  **tiny nucleus**.

Rutherford put the three ideas together into the **nuclear model** of the atom.

> **The nuclear model.** The atom has a tiny central nucleus. The nucleus carries the
> positive charge and almost all of the mass. The electrons move around outside the
> nucleus. The rest of the atom is empty space.

## How small is the nucleus?

The nucleus is far smaller than the atom. A typical atom has a diameter of about
$1 \times 10^{-10}$ m, and its nucleus has a diameter of about $1 \times 10^{-15}$ m.

That is a factor of about $10^{5}$, or one hundred thousand. If the atom were a
football stadium 200 m across, the nucleus would be about 2 mm across.

**Example 1.** A gold atom has a diameter of about $2.9 \times 10^{-10}$ m. Its
nucleus has a diameter about $1.0 \times 10^{5}$ times smaller. Calculate the diameter
of the nucleus.

$$
d = \frac{2.9 \times 10^{-10}}{1.0 \times 10^{5}}
$$

$$
d = 2.9 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{m}
$$

**Example 2.** A gold atom has a diameter of about $2.9 \times 10^{-10}$ m and its
nucleus has a diameter of about $1.4 \times 10^{-14}$ m. Calculate the ratio of the
diameter of the atom to the diameter of the nucleus.

$$
\text{ratio} = \frac{2.9 \times 10^{-10}}{1.4 \times 10^{-14}}
$$

$$
\text{ratio} = 2.1 \times 10^{4}
$$

The atom is about twenty thousand times wider than its nucleus. Almost all of the
atom is empty space.

**Example 3.** In one experiment $1.0 \times 10^{6}$ alpha particles are fired at a
gold foil. Of these, 125 are deflected through more than $90^\circ$. Calculate the
fraction deflected through more than $90^\circ$.

$$
\text{fraction} = \frac{125}{1.0 \times 10^{6}}
$$

$$
\text{fraction} = 1.3 \times 10^{-4}
$$

About one alpha particle in ten thousand comes close enough to a nucleus to be turned
back. That is why the nucleus must be so small.

## What the nuclear model could not explain

The nuclear model explains the scattering, but it leaves two problems.

An electron that moves in a circle around the nucleus is accelerating. A charged
particle that accelerates gives out energy. So the electron should lose energy, spiral
inwards, and the atom should collapse. Atoms do not collapse.

The model also says nothing about the light that atoms give out. A gas gives light only
at certain fixed wavelengths. A nuclear model with electrons in any orbit gives no
reason for those particular wavelengths. Section 19.2 shows how Bohr answered both
problems.

> **Watch out.** "Most alpha particles pass straight through" does not mean the atom
> has no charge. It means the charge is concentrated in a very small nucleus, so most
> particles miss it. Also, an alpha particle is not deflected by the electrons. An
> electron is about 7000 times lighter than an alpha particle, so it has almost no
> effect on the alpha particle's path.
