# 19.1 From Thomson to Rutherford — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the charge on an electron, in coulombs?
2. What is a scientific model, and why does a model get replaced?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C. The size of the charge is $e$, and the minus sign is what
   makes the electron negative.
2. A model is a simplified picture that explains what we observe and predicts what
   will happen next. A model is replaced when a new observation does not fit it.

The second answer is the whole story of this section. Thomson's model fitted the
evidence he had, and Rutherford's experiment did not fit it.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** An alpha particle carries a charge of $+2e$. State its charge in coulombs, and
explain why a gold nucleus repels it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What is the sign of the charge on a gold nucleus? Like charges do what?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The charge is $2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C. A gold nucleus is positive.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$q = 2e = 2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.2 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$$

The gold nucleus carries positive charge, and the alpha particle is also positive.
Two positive charges repel each other, so the nucleus pushes the alpha particle away.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A gold atom has a diameter of about $3.0 \times 10^{-10}$ m and its nucleus has
a diameter of about $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ m. Calculate the ratio of the diameter of the
atom to the diameter of the nucleus.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the diameter of the atom by the diameter of the nucleus.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{3.0 \times 10^{-10}}{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{ratio} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{-10}}{1.0 \times 10^{-14}} = 3.0 \times 10^{4}$$

The atom is about thirty thousand times wider than its nucleus. Almost all of the atom
is empty space.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** In one experiment $2.0 \times 10^{6}$ alpha particles are fired at a gold foil,
and 200 of them are deflected through more than $90^\circ$. Calculate the fraction
deflected through more than $90^\circ$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the number deflected by the total number fired.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{200}{2.0 \times 10^{6}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{fraction} = \frac{200}{2.0 \times 10^{6}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$$

One alpha particle in ten thousand is turned back through a large angle. The nucleus
must be very small, because almost every particle misses it.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Explain why most alpha particles pass straight through the gold foil with no
deflection.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Where is the positive charge in the nuclear model?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The positive charge sits in a tiny nucleus.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

In the nuclear model the positive charge and almost all of the mass sit in a tiny
nucleus. The rest of the atom is empty space.

An alpha particle passes through the empty space, far from any nucleus. With no
positive charge nearby, there is almost no force on it, so it carries on in a straight
line.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain what the alpha particles that are deflected through large angles show
about the atom.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What kind of force turns a fast, heavy particle through a large angle?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A large angle needs a strong force, and a strong force needs a nearby charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A large-angle deflection needs a strong repulsive force. That force comes from positive
charge, and it is strong only when the alpha particle passes very close to it.

So the positive charge is not spread through the atom. It is concentrated in one very
small place, the nucleus.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** State two things that the nuclear model could not explain.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about an electron moving in a circle, and about the light a gas gives out.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

An accelerating charge gives out energy. A gas gives out light at fixed wavelengths.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The first problem is that an electron moving in a circle is accelerating, so it should
give out energy, spiral inwards and let the atom collapse. Atoms do not collapse.

The second problem is that a gas gives out light only at certain fixed wavelengths. The
nuclear model gives no reason for those particular wavelengths.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 13

**7.** Two charged plates make a uniform electric field of strength
$2.0 \times 10^{4}$ N/C. Calculate the electric force on an alpha particle between
them. Take the charge on the alpha particle as $+3.2 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The force on a charge in a uniform field is $F = qE$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = 3.2 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{4}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = qE = 3.2 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{4} = 6.4 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{N}$$

The force points along the field, because the alpha particle is positive. This is the
same rule that the scattering experiment uses: the nucleus pushes the positive alpha
particle away from it.

</details>
</details>
</details>
