# 19.3 Emission and absorption spectra — Learn

**Prerequisites:** energy levels and photon emission from Section 19.2; the photon
energy equation from Section 18.1; waves and the wave equation from Chapter 9.

**Objectives:** describe a continuous spectrum, an emission line spectrum and an
absorption line spectrum; explain how each one forms from energy levels; explain how a
spectrum identifies an element.

By the end of this section, you can explain the lines in a spectrum and say what they
reveal about the atoms that produced them.

## Three kinds of spectrum

Spread out the light from a source with a prism or a diffraction grating, and you see
its **spectrum**. There are three kinds.

- A **continuous spectrum** shows every wavelength. The colours run into each other
  with no gaps. A hot solid or a hot dense liquid gives a continuous spectrum.
- An **emission line spectrum** shows bright lines on a dark background. Each line has
  its own wavelength. A hot gas at low pressure gives an emission line spectrum.
- An **absorption line spectrum** shows dark lines on a continuous background. The
  dark lines sit at particular wavelengths. A continuous source seen through a cooler
  gas gives an absorption line spectrum.

The three look different, and each one comes from the same idea: the energy levels of
the atoms.

## Where the lines come from

Section 19.2 gives the rule. An atom absorbs or emits one photon when an electron moves
between two levels, and the photon energy equals the gap between them.

**Emission.** A gas is heated, or a current is passed through it. Atoms gain energy and
their electrons move up to higher levels. An electron then falls back to a lower level
and the atom gives out a photon. The photons leave in all directions. Only the
wavelengths that match a gap appear, so the spectrum is a set of bright lines.

**Absorption.** Light from a hot source passes through a cooler gas. An atom in the gas
can take in a photon only when the photon energy equals a gap between two of its
levels. That photon is removed from the beam. An electron moves up to the higher level.
The wavelengths that match a gap are missing from the beam, so they appear as dark
lines.

Because the gaps are the same in both cases, an element absorbs at exactly the
wavelengths where it emits. Sodium, for example, has a strong line at 589 nm in both
its emission spectrum and its absorption spectrum.

## A fingerprint for each element

Every element has its own set of energy levels, so every element has its own set of
lines. No two elements share the same pattern. The pattern is a **fingerprint** for the
element.

That makes a spectrum a way to identify an element without touching it. Compare the
lines from an unknown source with the lines of elements measured in a laboratory, and a
match names the element.

Astronomers use this method on the light from stars and galaxies. The spectrum of the
Sun shows dark lines that match hydrogen, helium, sodium, calcium and iron. That is how
we know what the Sun is made of.

**Example 1.** A cool gas of sodium absorbs light at a wavelength of 589 nm. Calculate
the energy of one absorbed photon. Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and
$c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s.

First change the wavelength to metres: $589\,\text{nm} = 5.89 \times 10^{-7}$ m. Then

$$
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

$$
E = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{5.89 \times 10^{-7}}
$$

$$
E = 3.4 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}
$$

**Example 2.** Calculate the frequency of the light absorbed at 589 nm.

$$
c = f\lambda
$$

$$
f = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{8}}{5.89 \times 10^{-7}}
$$

$$
f = 5.1 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}
$$

**Example 3.** The dark line at 589 nm comes from an electron moving between two energy
levels in the sodium atom. Calculate the energy difference between the levels, in
electronvolts.

$$
\Delta E = \frac{3.4 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}
$$

$$
\Delta E = 2.1\,\text{eV}
$$

**Example 4.** Two energy levels in an atom differ by 2.1 eV. Calculate the wavelength
of the photon emitted when an electron drops from the higher level to the lower one.

$$
\Delta E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

$$
\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E} = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}
{2.1 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}
$$

$$
\lambda = 5.9 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}
$$

That is 590 nm, in the yellow part of the spectrum. It matches the sodium line.

> **Watch out.** The dark lines in an absorption spectrum are not a different set from
> the bright lines of the same element. They are at the same wavelengths. The gas
> removes the photons it would have emitted. Also, the dark lines are not "shadow"; the
> glass and the slit do not make them. They come from photons that the atoms absorbed.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The hydrogen lamp gives a bright red line at 656 nm,
because of the $n = 3$ to $n = 2$ transition. A cool cloud of hydrogen in front of a
hot star shows a dark line at the same wavelength.

Write two sentences. In the first, explain why the lamp gives separate bright lines
instead of a continuous rainbow. In the second, explain why the same hydrogen shows a
dark line at 656 nm when it absorbs light instead of emitting it. You have now answered
the chapter problem.

- explain why an element emits and absorbs at the same wavelengths;
- use $\Delta E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ to move between a photon energy and a
  wavelength;
- explain how the spectrum of a star shows which elements it contains.
