# Chapter 19: Models of the atom

An atom is far too small to see. Everything we know about the inside of an atom comes
from experiments that fired something at it and watched where it went. In this chapter
you follow the model of the atom as each new experiment forced a change.

## The chapter problem

**The lamp that gives only some colours.** A hydrogen discharge lamp gives a bright red
line at a wavelength of 656 nm. It gives a few other lines at their own wavelengths.
Between the lines there is darkness, not a continuous rainbow.

By the end of the chapter you can find the energy of one photon of the red light, match
that energy to a jump between two energy levels of the hydrogen atom, and explain why
the lamp gives separate lines instead of every colour.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- describe Thomson's model of the atom, and the evidence that replaced it;
- describe the alpha-particle scattering experiment and explain how it led to the
  nuclear model;
- explain what an energy level is, and why an atom emits light at fixed wavelengths;
- use the energy levels of hydrogen and $\Delta E = hf$ to find a photon energy, a
  frequency or a wavelength;
- tell a continuous spectrum from an emission spectrum and an absorption spectrum, and
  explain how each one forms;
- explain how the spectrum of a distant star shows which elements the star contains.

## Where it is used

Spectroscopy identifies elements in a laboratory, in a furnace and in a star.
Astronomers read the spectrum of a star or a galaxy to find out what it is made of.
Discharge lamps, such as the sodium lamps on some roads, work by the same bright lines.
