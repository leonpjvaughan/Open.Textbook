# Chapter 19: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The lamp that gives only some colours.** A hydrogen discharge lamp gives a bright red
line at a wavelength of 656 nm. It gives a few other lines at their own wavelengths,
and darkness between them.

**Step 1: the energy of one photon.** Change the wavelength to metres,
$656\,\text{nm} = 6.56 \times 10^{-7}$ m, and use $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$:

$$E = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{8}}{6.56 \times 10^{-7}} = 3.0
\times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$$

In electronvolts this is $\dfrac{3.0 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 1.9$ eV.

**Step 2: the transition that produced the line.** The energy levels of hydrogen are
$E_n = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}$ eV. For the $n = 3$ to $n = 2$ transition:

$$\Delta E = E_3 - E_2 = -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89\,\text{eV}$$

The photon from this transition carries 1.89 eV, which is 1.9 eV to two significant
figures. It matches the energy of the red line, so the red line comes from the
$n = 3$ to $n = 2$ transition.

**Step 3: why the lamp gives lines and not a rainbow.** The atom has a fixed set of
energy levels, so an electron can make only certain jumps. Each jump gives a photon of
one fixed energy, and therefore one fixed wavelength. The lamp gives bright lines at
those wavelengths and darkness between them.

**Step 4: why the same hydrogen absorbs at 656 nm.** A cool hydrogen atom can take in a
photon only when the photon energy equals a gap between two levels. The $n = 2$ to
$n = 3$ gap is the same 1.89 eV. So the atom absorbs light at 656 nm, and that
wavelength appears as a dark line.

**What it tells you about the atom.** The nucleus is tiny, and it carries the positive
charge and almost all of the mass. The electrons sit outside it, in fixed energy
levels. Every element has its own set of levels, so its spectrum is a fingerprint.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies**
and say why. Some of them join this chapter to the electron from Chapter 17 and the
photon from Chapter 18.

1. State the observation in the alpha-particle scattering experiment that showed the
   atom has a tiny, massive nucleus, and explain why it does.
2. A gold atom has a diameter of $2.9 \times 10^{-10}$ m and its nucleus has a diameter
   of $1.4 \times 10^{-14}$ m. Calculate the ratio of the two diameters.
3. Use $E_n = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}$ eV to find the energy of the $n = 2$ level of
   hydrogen, then find the energy of the photon emitted in the $n = 2$ to $n = 1$
   transition.
4. A hydrogen atom emits a photon of energy 10.2 eV. Calculate the wavelength of the
   photon. Take $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $c = 3.0 \times 10^{8}$ m/s and
   $1\,\text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
5. Explain why the energy of an electron in a hydrogen atom is written as a negative
   number.
6. A gas gives bright lines at 486 nm and 656 nm, and darkness between them. Explain
   what these lines show about the atoms in the gas.
7. Explain why a cool gas absorbs light at exactly the wavelengths at which the same
   hot gas emits.
8. An electron in a hydrogen atom absorbs a photon and moves from the ground state to
   the $n = 3$ level. Calculate the energy of the absorbed photon.
9. The spectrum of a distant star shows a set of dark lines. Explain how astronomers
   use the lines to find which elements the star contains.
10. You must find the ionisation energy of hydrogen. Would you use the bright lines of
    a discharge lamp, the energy-level formula, or the alpha-particle scattering
    experiment? Say why, and state what each of the other two methods can give you.

## What you can now do

- describe Thomson's model of the atom and the evidence that replaced it;
- describe the alpha-particle scattering experiment and explain the nuclear model;
- state what an energy level is and use $E_n = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}$ eV for hydrogen;
- use $\Delta E = E_{\text{higher}} - E_{\text{lower}} = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ to
  find a photon energy, a frequency or a wavelength;
- tell a continuous spectrum from an emission line spectrum and an absorption line
  spectrum, and explain how each one forms;
- explain why an element emits and absorbs at the same wavelengths;
- explain how the spectrum of a star shows which elements it contains.

## Thinking back

Look at the lamp you chose at the start of the chapter — a neon sign, a sodium street
lamp, a firework, or a discharge tube in the laboratory.

You can now say where its colours come from. The gas inside has a set of energy levels,
and each colour is a jump between two of them. A sodium lamp is yellow because sodium
has a strong line at 589 nm.

Write down one colour you have seen from your lamp, and estimate its wavelength. Then
work out the energy of one photon of that colour, in electronvolts. If your estimate is
rough, write that down too. Saying how rough an estimate is belongs in the physics, and
it is exactly what the Physics In Practice Investigation asks you to do.
