# 11.1 Sound as a wave — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. State what a wave carries from one place to another.
2. Name the two families of waves, and say which one needs a medium.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. A wave carries **energy** from one place to another without carrying the material
   with it. The particles of the medium vibrate about their own positions and pass the
   disturbance on.
2. A **mechanical wave** needs a medium, and an **electromagnetic wave** does not. Sound
   is mechanical, so it needs a medium; light is electromagnetic, so it can cross a
   vacuum.

The second answer is the key to this section. Sound is mechanical, and that single fact
explains why a vacuum is silent.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** State the kind of wave that sound is, and describe how the particles of the medium
move as the sound passes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think of the spring from Section 9.3. The coils moved along the spring, not across it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The particles vibrate back and forth **along** the direction the sound travels.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Sound is a **longitudinal** wave. The particles of the medium vibrate back and forth
along the direction the sound travels. They bunch together into **compressions** and
spread apart into **rarefactions**, and those regions travel away from the source.

The particles themselves stay near their own positions, so the energy travels but the
medium does not.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A sound wave travels through air at 340 m/s. Calculate the distance it travels in
2.0 s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Distance is speed multiplied by time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$s = vt = 340 \times 2.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$s = vt = 340 \times 2.0 = 680\,\text{m}$$

The unit check: $\text{m/s} \times \text{s} = \text{m}$, which is a distance. Correct.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A sound wave has a frequency of 500 Hz and travels through air at 340 m/s.
Calculate its wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $v = f\lambda$ for the wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f} = \dfrac{340}{500}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{340}{500} = 0.68\,\text{m}$$

The unit check: $\dfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{Hz}} = \dfrac{\text{m/s}}{\text{s}^{-1}} =
\text{m}$, which is a length. Correct.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** The speed of sound in water is 1500 m/s. Calculate the time sound takes to travel
900 m through the water.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $s = vt$ for the time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$t = \dfrac{s}{v} = \dfrac{900}{1500}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$t = \frac{s}{v} = \frac{900}{1500} = 0.60\,\text{s}$$

Compare this with the 2.6 s the same distance would take in air. Sound travels much
faster in water, because the particles are closer together.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain why sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A vacuum has no particles. What does a sound wave need in order to travel?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Sound is a mechanical wave, so it needs particles to vibrate.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Sound is a mechanical wave. It travels by making the particles of a medium vibrate and
pass the disturbance on. A vacuum contains no particles, so there is nothing to vibrate
and nothing to pass the sound on. Sound therefore cannot cross a vacuum.

The evidence is a bell jar. An electric bell becomes fainter as the air is pumped out,
and it cannot be heard once the jar is evacuated. The bell is still visible, so the
sound needed the air and the light did not.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A siren makes a note of 700 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate
the wavelength of the note.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\lambda = \dfrac{340}{700}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{340}{700} = 0.49\,\text{m}$$

That is 49 cm, about the width of a car seat. Section 11.4 returns to this siren and asks
what happens to the wavelength when the ambulance moves.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 9

**7.** A wave has a frequency of 25 Hz and a wavelength of 1.2 m. Calculate its speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply the frequency by the wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = f\lambda = 25 \times 1.2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = f\lambda = 25 \times 1.2 = 30\,\text{m/s}$$

This is the same wave equation that Section 11.1 uses for sound. Nothing about it changes
when the wave is a sound wave; only the speed, which is set by the medium, is different.

</details>
</details>
</details>
