# 11.1 Sound as a wave — Learn

**Prerequisites:** what a wave is, from Section 9.1; transverse and longitudinal waves,
from Section 9.3; the wave equation, from Section 9.2.

**Objectives:** describe sound as a longitudinal wave; explain that sound needs a medium;
describe the compressions and rarefactions in a sound wave; state the speed of sound in
air, and say how it depends on the medium.

By the end of this section, you can describe how sound travels and what it needs in order
to travel.

## What makes a sound

Touch a loudspeaker cone while music plays and you feel it buzzing. Strike a tuning fork
and you see it blur. Something is vibrating, and the sound starts at that vibration.

A vibration on its own is not a sound you can hear across a room. The vibrating object
must pass the disturbance on to its surroundings. That is the job of a wave.

Sound is a **mechanical wave**. It travels by making the particles of a medium vibrate,
and it needs that medium to be there. The medium is usually air, but it can be water, a
solid, or any other material with particles in it.

## Sound is a longitudinal wave

A sound wave is **longitudinal**. The particles of the medium vibrate back and forth
along the direction the sound travels, not across it.

Push the end of a stretched spring and the coils bunch together and spread apart. Sound
does the same to the air. The regions where the particles are squeezed closer together
are **compressions**, and the regions where they are farther apart are **rarefactions**.

A compression followed by a rarefaction, then another compression, travels away from the
source. The air particles themselves stay near their own positions. They pass the
disturbance to their neighbours and settle back, so the energy travels but the air does
not.

> **Watch out.** A sound wave is not a wavy line with crests and troughs. That picture
> belongs to a transverse wave. The correct picture of sound shows bands of compressed
> air and spread-out air travelling away from the source. Drawing sound as a wavy line is
> the mistake that makes the Doppler effect hard to picture later in this chapter.

## Sound needs a medium

Sound travels by making particles vibrate, so it needs particles. A **vacuum** has none,
so sound cannot cross a vacuum.

The evidence is a bell jar. An electric bell rings inside a sealed glass jar, and you
hear it. As a pump removes the air, the bell becomes fainter. Once most of the air
is gone, you can see the hammer striking the bell but you cannot hear it. Let the air
back in and the sound returns.

Light from the bell is visible throughout, so the missing sound is not a failure of your
eyes or the jar. The sound needed the air, and the light did not.

## Try it: the table tap

**Predict.** You will have a partner tap the far end of a table while you listen twice:
first with your ear pressed to the table, and then sitting up with your ear in the air.
Before either tap, write down which one you expect to sound louder.

**Test.** Press your ear to the table and have your partner tap lightly at the far end.
Sit up and have your partner tap just as lightly again, and listen through the air.

**Explain.** Write two or three sentences. Say which tap sounded louder, and explain it
using the particles of the two media. Your explanation should mention how close the
particles are and how quickly the disturbance passes from one to the next.

## The speed of sound

The speed of sound depends on the medium, and on its temperature. In air at about
$20\,^\circ\text{C}$ it is about **340 m/s**. That value is worth remembering, and it is
the one this book uses unless a question says otherwise.

Sound travels faster when the particles of the medium are held closer together and pull
on one another, so the disturbance passes on more quickly.

- In **steel**, the particles are packed close and bound together, and sound travels at
  about 5000 m/s.
- In **water**, the particles are close but free to slide, and sound travels at about
  1500 m/s.
- In **air**, the particles are far apart and meet only occasionally, and sound travels
  at about 340 m/s.

The order is solid, then liquid, then gas. In a gas the particles are not bound to one
another, so the disturbance is passed on by collisions, which is a slower process.

Sound also travels a little faster in warm air than in cold air, because the particles
move faster and carry the disturbance on sooner. The effect is small, so a change of a
few degrees makes little difference to a calculation.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A sound wave travels through air at 340 m/s. Calculate how far it travels
in 2.0 s.

$$
s = vt
$$

where $s$ is the distance the sound travels, $v$ the speed of sound and $t$ the
time.

$$
s = 340 \times 2.0
$$

$$
s = 680\,\text{m}
$$

**Example 2.** A siren makes a note of frequency 700 Hz. The speed of sound in air is
340 m/s. Calculate the wavelength of the note.

Rearranging $v = f\lambda$ for the wavelength:

$$
\lambda = \frac{v}{f}
$$

$$
\lambda = \frac{340}{700}
$$

$$
\lambda = 0.49\,\text{m}
$$

**Example 3.** You see a flash of lightning and hear the thunder 5.0 s later. The speed
of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate your distance from the lightning.

$$
s = vt
$$

$$
s = 340 \times 5.0
$$

$$
s = 1700\,\text{m}
$$

The light reached you almost instantly, because light travels about a million times
faster than sound. The delay was the time the sound took.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The ambulance siren reaches your ear across the road.

1. State the kind of wave the sound is, and describe how the air particles move as it
   passes you.
2. State what the air does to carry the sound, and what would happen to the sound if the
   air were removed.
3. The siren makes 700 Hz and the sound travels at 340 m/s. Calculate the wavelength.

Keep your answers. Section 11.2 uses the speed of sound to measure the distance to a
reflecting wall.

## You should now be able to

- describe sound as a mechanical, longitudinal wave;
- explain why sound needs a medium, and describe the bell-jar evidence;
- name a compression and a rarefaction, and say what the particles do in each;
- state that the speed of sound in air is about 340 m/s, and that it is larger in water
  and larger again in steel;
- explain why sound travels faster when the particles of the medium are closer together;
- use $v = f\lambda$ and $s = vt$ to find a wavelength, a distance or a time.
