# 11.5 Calculating with the Doppler effect — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write the wave equation, and say what each symbol means.
2. A source makes a note of 640 Hz and the speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate
   the wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $v = f\lambda$. The speed $v$ is in metres per second, the frequency $f$ is in hertz,
   and the wavelength $\lambda$ is in metres.
2. $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f} = \dfrac{340}{640} = 0.53\,\text{m}$.

The wavelength is the spacing of the wavefronts when the source is at rest. The Doppler
formula tells you the frequency a moving source produces at a listener.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A car approaches a stationary listener at 20 m/s with its horn sounding at 500 Hz.
The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Use $f' = \dfrac{fc}{c - u}$ to calculate the
frequency the listener hears.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The source approaches, so use the minus sign in the denominator.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$f' = \dfrac{500 \times 340}{340 - 20} = \dfrac{170000}{320}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$f' = \frac{fc}{c - u} = \frac{500 \times 340}{340 - 20} = \frac{170000}{320}
= 531\,\text{Hz}$$

The listener hears 531 Hz, which is higher than the 500 Hz the horn makes. The answer is
larger than the source frequency, as it must be for an approaching source.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A source moves away from a stationary listener at 25 m/s and makes a note of
600 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Use $f' = \dfrac{fc}{c + u}$ to calculate
the frequency the listener hears.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The source moves away, so use the plus sign in the denominator.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$f' = \dfrac{600 \times 340}{340 + 25} = \dfrac{204000}{365}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$f' = \frac{fc}{c + u} = \frac{600 \times 340}{340 + 25} = \frac{204000}{365}
= 559\,\text{Hz}$$

The listener hears 559 Hz, which is lower than the 600 Hz the source makes. A receding
source always gives an observed frequency below the source frequency.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A source approaches a stationary listener and makes a note of 800 Hz. The listener
hears 900 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the speed of the source.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $f' = \dfrac{fc}{c - u}$ for $c - u$ first, then find $u$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$c - u = \dfrac{fc}{f'} = \dfrac{800 \times 340}{900}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$c - u = \frac{fc}{f'} = \frac{800 \times 340}{900} = 302.2\,\text{m/s}$$

Then

$$u = 340 - 302.2 = 37.8\,\text{m/s}$$

Check the answer by substituting it back:
$\dfrac{800 \times 340}{340 - 37.8} = 900\,\text{Hz}$, which is the frequency the
listener heard.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A source makes a note of 500 Hz and moves towards a listener at 20 m/s. The speed
of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the observed wavelength in front of the source,
using $\lambda' = \dfrac{c - u}{f}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The wavefronts are squeezed together, so the observed wavelength is shorter than the
stationary wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\lambda' = \dfrac{340 - 20}{500} = \dfrac{320}{500}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda' = \frac{c - u}{f} = \frac{340 - 20}{500} = \frac{320}{500} = 0.64\,\text{m}$$

The stationary wavelength would be $\dfrac{340}{500} = 0.68\,\text{m}$, so the wavefronts
in front are squeezed from 0.68 m to 0.64 m. A shorter wavelength at the same speed of
sound gives the higher observed frequency, 531 Hz, from question 1.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** The Doppler formula is $f' = \dfrac{fc}{c \pm u}$. Explain how to choose the sign
in the denominator, and what each choice does to the frequency heard.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A smaller denominator makes a fraction larger. Which sign gives the smaller denominator?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Use the minus sign for an approaching source, and the plus sign for a receding source.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Use the **minus** sign when the source moves towards the listener, so the denominator is
$c - u$. This denominator is smaller than $c$, so the fraction is larger than $f$, and the
observed frequency is higher than the source frequency.

Use the **plus** sign when the source moves away, so the denominator is $c + u$. This
denominator is larger than $c$, so the observed frequency is lower than the source
frequency.

Choosing the wrong sign gives an answer on the wrong side of the source frequency, which
is a quick way to check your work.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A source approaches a stationary listener at 34 m/s and makes a note of 1000 Hz.
The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the frequency the listener hears.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the minus sign in the denominator.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$f' = \dfrac{1000 \times 340}{340 - 34} = \dfrac{340000}{306}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$f' = \frac{fc}{c - u} = \frac{1000 \times 340}{340 - 34} = \frac{340000}{306}
= 1111\,\text{Hz}$$

The listener hears 1111 Hz, which is 111 Hz above the source frequency. The shift is about
a tenth of the source frequency, and the source speed is a tenth of the speed of sound.
For small speeds the fractional shift is roughly the ratio of the two speeds.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 2

**7.** A car travels at a steady 30 m/s for 4.0 s. Calculate the distance it travels.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Distance is speed multiplied by time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$s = vt = 30 \times 4.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$s = vt = 30 \times 4.0 = 120\,\text{m}$$

That is the distance the ambulance in the chapter problem covers in 4.0 s. In that time
the sound it makes has travelled much further, because sound is about ten times faster
than the ambulance.

</details>
</details>
</details>
