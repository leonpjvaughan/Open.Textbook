# 11.4 The Doppler effect — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the wavelength and frequency of a wave from Section 9.2; sound as a
longitudinal wave from Section 9.3; the speed of sound from Section 11.1.

**Objectives:** describe the Doppler effect for a moving source; explain why the observed
frequency is higher as the source approaches and lower as it recedes; describe the
wavefronts in front of and behind a moving source; give real-life applications of the
effect.

By the end of this section, you can explain why a passing siren changes note without any
change in the siren itself.

## The observation

Stand at the roadside as an ambulance goes past with its siren sounding. As it comes
towards you, the note sounds high. At the moment it passes, the note drops. As it moves
away, the note sounds low.

The driver hears none of this. The siren makes one steady note throughout. Something about
the motion of the source changes the note that reaches **you**.

That change in the observed frequency, caused by the motion of the source or the listener,
is the **Doppler effect**.

## What happens to the wavefronts

A **wavefront** is a surface on which all the particles are doing the same thing at the
same instant. For sound in air, each compression is a wavefront travelling outwards from
the source.

When the source is at rest, the wavefronts spread out as evenly spaced circles, and the
distance between neighbouring ones is the wavelength. The listener receives them at the
same frequency as the source.

Now let the source move.

- In front of the moving source, each new wavefront is sent from a point **closer** to
  the listener than the one before. The wavefronts are squeezed together, so the distance
  between them is **shorter** than the stationary wavelength.
- Behind the moving source, each new wavefront is sent from a point **farther** from the
  listener than the one before. The wavefronts are stretched apart, so the distance
  between them is **longer** than the stationary wavelength.

The source still makes the same number of wavefronts every second. Moving it does not
change how many it produces; it changes where each one starts.

## Why the note changes

The listener hears a wavefront every time one arrives. If the wavefronts arrive more
often, the observed frequency is higher, and the note sounds higher in pitch.

- **Approaching source:** the wavefronts are squeezed closer together, so they arrive
  more often. The observed frequency is **higher** than the source frequency, and the
  note sounds higher.
- **Receding source:** the wavefronts are stretched farther apart, so they arrive less
  often. The observed frequency is **lower** than the source frequency, and the note
  sounds lower.

The change happens at the moment the source passes, because that is when its motion stops
being towards the listener and starts being away.

## The speed of sound does not change

It is worth being clear about what does change and what does not.

- The **source frequency** stays the same. The siren makes one steady note.
- The **speed of sound** in the air stays the same. It is set by the air, not by the
  source. The source moving does not push the sound along faster.
- The **observed frequency** changes, and so does the **observed wavelength**.

Because the speed of sound is unchanged, a higher observed frequency must go with a
shorter observed wavelength. Their product, $v = f\lambda$, stays the same.

## Try it: a source in a circle

**Predict.** Tie a small buzzer or a phone playing a steady tone to a string, and swing it
in a horizontal circle above your head at arm's length. You will listen as it comes
towards you and as it goes away. Before you start, write down what you expect to hear as
it comes towards you: a note higher than the tone, or lower?

**Test.** Swing the source steadily and listen through one full circle. Then have a
partner listen from the other side, and compare what each of you hears.

**Explain.** Write two or three sentences. Say what you heard as the source came towards
you and as it went away. Explain it using the wavefronts, and say why the pitch rose and
fell twice in each full circle. Your explanation is the answer to the chapter problem.

## Where it is used

The Doppler effect is used wherever motion changes a frequency that someone measures.

- **Doppler speed traps.** A radar unit sends microwaves at a car and detects the wave
  reflected from it. The reflected wave has a slightly different frequency, and the size
  of the shift gives the speed of the car.
- **Doppler ultrasound.** A probe measures the speed of blood flowing in an artery, by
  the frequency shift of the ultrasound reflected from the moving blood cells.
- **Weather radar.** The shift in the radar echo from rain shows how fast the rain is
  moving, which helps to forecast storms.
- **Red shift of galaxies.** The light from distant galaxies is shifted towards longer
  wavelengths. This is the Doppler effect for light, and it is evidence that the galaxies
  are moving away from us and that the universe is expanding.

> **Watch out.** The Doppler effect does not change the frequency the source makes, and it
> does not change the speed of the sound in the air. It changes only the frequency and
> the wavelength that the **listener** observes. A common mistake is to say that the
> siren's frequency rises; the siren's frequency is steady, and the observed frequency is
> what rises.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. You stand at the roadside as the ambulance passes at 30 m/s.

1. Explain why the note you hear is higher while the ambulance approaches, using the
   wavefronts in front of it.
2. Explain why the note drops at the moment it passes you.
3. State what the driver hears, and explain your answer.

Keep your answers. Section 11.5 uses the Doppler formula to calculate the two frequencies
you hear.

## You should now be able to

- describe the Doppler effect for a moving source;
- describe how the wavefronts bunch in front of the source and spread behind it;
- explain why the observed frequency is higher as the source approaches and lower as it
  recedes;
- state that the source frequency and the speed of sound are unchanged;
- explain why the driver of the ambulance hears no change;
- describe two applications of the Doppler effect, including the red shift of galaxies.
