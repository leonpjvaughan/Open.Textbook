# 11.4 The Doppler effect — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write the wave equation, and say what each symbol means.
2. A sound wave has a frequency of 850 Hz and travels through air at 340 m/s. Calculate
   its wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $v = f\lambda$. The speed $v$ is in metres per second, the frequency $f$ is in hertz,
   and the wavelength $\lambda$ is in metres.
2. $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f} = \dfrac{340}{850} = 0.40\,\text{m}$.

The wavelength is the spacing of the wavefronts. Everything in this section is about what
happens to that spacing when the source moves.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A source of sound moves towards a listener. Describe what happens to the
wavefronts in front of the source.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The source sends out a wavefront every time it completes one vibration. Where is it when
it sends the next one?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The source has moved forward, so the next wavefront starts from a point closer to the
listener.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The source moves towards the listener between one wavefront and the next. Each new
wavefront is therefore sent from a point **closer** to the listener than the one before.

The wavefronts in front of the source are squeezed closer together. The distance between
neighbouring wavefronts is the wavelength, so the wavelength reaching the listener is
shorter than the wavelength the source makes.

The source still makes the same number of wavefronts every second. Only the spacing
changes.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A source of sound moves away from a listener. Explain why the frequency the
listener hears is lower than the frequency the source makes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The wavefronts behind the source are stretched apart. How often do they arrive?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A longer distance between wavefronts means they arrive less often.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The source moves away from the listener between one wavefront and the next, so each
wavefront is sent from a point **farther** from the listener than the one before.

The wavefronts are stretched farther apart, so the wavelength reaching the listener is
longer than the wavelength the source makes. A longer distance between wavefronts means
they arrive **less often**, so the observed frequency is lower and the note sounds lower.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** State what the driver of a moving ambulance hears from the siren, and explain your
answer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Is the driver moving towards the siren, or away from it?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The driver and the siren move together, so there is no relative motion between them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The driver hears the steady note that the siren makes, with no change in pitch.

The driver and the siren are in the same vehicle, so they move together. The driver is
not moving towards the siren or away from it, so there is no relative motion along the
line between them. The Doppler shift depends on that relative motion, and here it is
zero.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A stationary siren makes a note of 700 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s.
Calculate the wavelength of the note.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\lambda = \dfrac{340}{700}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{340}{700} = 0.49\,\text{m}$$

This is the spacing of the wavefronts when the source is at rest. When the ambulance
moves, the spacing in front becomes shorter than this value, and the spacing behind
becomes longer.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A siren sounds in still air. Explain why the speed of the sound in the air does not
change as the ambulance moves.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What sets the speed of sound in air: the source, or the air itself?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The speed of sound in air is set by the air, its temperature and its pressure.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The speed of sound in air is set by the air, its temperature and its pressure. It is not
set by the motion of the source.

When the ambulance moves, the source is in a different place for each wavefront, so the
spacing of the wavefronts changes. The wavefronts still travel through the air at
340 m/s. The observed frequency and the observed wavelength both change, but their
product, the speed, stays the same.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Describe one real-life application of the Doppler effect, and explain how the
effect is used in it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think of something that measures the speed of a moving object, or of light from a distant
galaxy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A speed trap sends a wave at a car and detects the wave reflected from it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A Doppler speed trap sends microwaves at a car and detects the wave reflected from it.
The reflected wave has a slightly different frequency from the wave that was sent. The
size of that frequency shift gives the speed of the car, so the unit can display a speed
without touching the car.

The red shift of the light from distant galaxies uses the same effect. The light is
shifted towards longer wavelengths, which shows that the galaxies are moving away from
us. That is evidence that the universe is expanding.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 9

**7.** Explain the difference between a transverse wave and a longitudinal wave, and give
one example of each.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the direction the particles vibrate with the direction the wave travels.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

In a transverse wave the vibration is at right angles to the wave. In a longitudinal wave
the vibration is along the wave.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

In a **transverse wave**, the particles vibrate at right angles to the direction the wave
travels. A wave on a rope is transverse, and so is light.

In a **longitudinal wave**, the particles vibrate along the direction the wave travels.
Sound is longitudinal, and it has compressions and rarefactions instead of crests and
troughs.

The Doppler effect works for both kinds. The red shift is the Doppler effect for light,
which is transverse.

</details>
</details>
</details>
