# 11.2 The speed of sound and its measurement — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write the wave equation, and say what each symbol means.
2. A wave has a frequency of 8.0 Hz and a wavelength of 0.50 m. Calculate its speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $v = f\lambda$. The speed $v$ is in metres per second, the frequency $f$ is in hertz,
   and the wavelength $\lambda$ is in metres.
2. $v = f\lambda = 8.0 \times 0.50 = 4.0\,\text{m/s}$.

The second answer is the whole of the wave equation. Both methods in this section end by
using it, once a wavelength is known.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A student claps once and hears the echo from a wall 0.60 s later. The speed of
sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the distance from the student to the wall.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The sound travels to the wall and back. Which distance is twice the other?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The distance covered by the sound is $340 \times 0.60$. The distance to the wall is half
of that.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$d = \frac{vt}{2} = \frac{340 \times 0.60}{2} = \frac{204}{2} = 102\,\text{m}$$

The wall is 102 m away. Check the sense of it: the sound covers 204 m in 0.60 s, so half
of that is the one-way distance.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A wall is 255 m from a student. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate
the time between the clap and the echo.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The sound covers twice the distance to the wall.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$t = \dfrac{2d}{v} = \dfrac{2 \times 255}{340}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$t = \frac{2d}{v} = \frac{2 \times 255}{340} = \frac{510}{340} = 1.5\,\text{s}$$

One and a half seconds is long enough to time by hand, which is why a wall about 250 m
away suits the echo method.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A tuning fork of frequency 425 Hz gives its first resonance at an air-column
length of 0.20 m and its second at 0.60 m. Calculate the wavelength of the sound.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The difference between the two lengths is half a wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\lambda = 2(L_2 - L_1) = 2(0.60 - 0.20)$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = 2(L_2 - L_1) = 2(0.60 - 0.20) = 2 \times 0.40 = 0.80\,\text{m}$$

where $L_1$ and $L_2$ are the first and second resonance lengths.

The first resonance puts a quarter of a wavelength in the tube and the second puts three
quarters, so the extra 0.40 m of air is half a wavelength.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Use the wavelength 0.80 m and the frequency 425 Hz to calculate the speed of
sound in the air in the tube.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the wave equation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = f\lambda = 425 \times 0.80$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = f\lambda = 425 \times 0.80 = 340\,\text{m/s}$$

That is the accepted value for air at about $20\,^\circ\text{C}$, so the measurements
agree with the theory. The resonance method reaches this value without timing anything by
hand.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain why the resonance tube uses the **difference** between two resonance
lengths, rather than one length on its own.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the open end of the tube. Does the first compression reflect exactly at the
top of the tube?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The open end adds a small extra length, the same amount at each resonance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The air just outside the open end vibrates too, so each resonance length is a little
longer than the theory predicts. This extra amount is the **end correction**, and it is
the same at the first and the second resonance.

Subtracting the first length from the second removes the end correction:

$$L_2 - L_1 = \frac{\lambda}{2}$$

If only one length were used, the end correction would be left in the answer and would
make the wavelength too large.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A sound travels 1020 m through the air. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s.
Calculate the time the sound takes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $s = vt$ for the time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$t = \dfrac{s}{v} = \dfrac{1020}{340}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$t = \frac{s}{v} = \frac{1020}{340} = 3.0\,\text{s}$$

A three-second delay is about the longest you would hear in ordinary surroundings. Over
1020 m the sound has had time to spread and weaken, which is why an echo from this
distance is faint.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 2

**7.** A car travels 340 m in 20 s. Calculate its average speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Average speed is distance divided by time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \dfrac{s}{t} = \dfrac{340}{20}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \frac{s}{t} = \frac{340}{20} = 17\,\text{m/s}$$

The car covers 340 m in 20 s, and sound covers the same 340 m in one second. Sound is
about twenty times faster than a car on a road.

</details>
</details>
</details>
