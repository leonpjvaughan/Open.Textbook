# 11.2 The speed of sound and its measurement — Learn

**Prerequisites:** speed as distance divided by time, from Chapter 2; the wave equation
from Section 9.2; stationary waves and resonance from Section 9.5.

**Objectives:** state the speed of sound in air and say how it depends on the medium;
measure the speed of sound in air by the echo method; measure it with a resonance tube;
use $v = f\lambda$ and $s = vt$ in either direction.

By the end of this section, you can measure the speed of sound in air in two different
ways and explain which measurements each one needs.

## Why the speed matters

Sound takes time to travel. You see a flash of lightning at once and hear the thunder
seconds later. A shout across a valley comes back as an echo. The delay is the travel
time of the sound, and it gives you a way to measure the speed.

In air at about $20\,^\circ\text{C}$ the speed of sound is about **340 m/s**. That is
roughly the length of a football pitch every second. Sound is fast, but light is far
faster, which is why the light from the lightning arrives first.

## Method 1: the echo

An echo is a sound wave reflected from a wall, a cliff or a building. The sound travels
to the reflecting surface and back, so the distance it covers is **twice** the distance
to the wall.

To measure the speed:

1. Measure a straight distance $d$ to a large flat surface, using a tape. Choose at least
   100 m, so that the echo takes a time you can measure.
2. Clap once, and time from the clap to the echo. A phone or a stopwatch will do.
3. Repeat the timing several times and take the average.
4. Calculate the speed from $v = \dfrac{2d}{t}$.

**Example 1.** A student claps once and hears the echo from a wall 0.80 s later. The
speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the distance to the wall.

The sound travels to the wall and back, so the distance to the wall is half the distance
the sound covers:

$$
d = \frac{vt}{2}
$$

$$
d = \frac{340 \times 0.80}{2}
$$

$$
d = 136\,\text{m}
$$

The wall is 136 m away. If the student had forgotten to divide by two, the answer would
be 272 m, which is twice as far as the wall.

## Method 2: the resonance tube

The second method uses a stationary wave, which you met in Section 9.5. A tube is closed
at the bottom by water and open at the top. A tuning fork of known frequency is held over
the open end, and the length of the air column is changed until the sound becomes
suddenly loud. That is **resonance**.

At the first resonance a quarter of a wavelength fits in the tube. At the second
resonance three quarters fit. The difference between the two lengths is half a
wavelength:

$$L_2 - L_1 = \frac{\lambda}{2} \qquad \text{so} \qquad \lambda = 2(L_2 - L_1)$$

where $L_1$ and $L_2$ are the two resonance lengths and $\lambda$ is the wavelength.

The frequency is printed on the tuning fork, so once you have the wavelength you can find
the speed from the wave equation:

$$v = f\lambda$$

Measuring the **difference** between the two lengths is the point of taking two readings.
The open top of the tube makes each resonance length a little longer than the theory
predicts. The same small end correction is added to both readings, so subtracting them
removes it.

**Example 2.** A tuning fork of frequency 500 Hz gives its first resonance at an
air-column length of 0.16 m and its second at 0.50 m. Calculate the speed of sound.

$$
\lambda = 2(L_2 - L_1)
$$

$$
\lambda = 2(0.50 - 0.16)
$$

$$
\lambda = 0.68\,\text{m}
$$

Then

$$
v = f\lambda
$$

$$
v = 500 \times 0.68
$$

$$
v = 340\,\text{m/s}
$$

## Comparing the two methods

Both methods give the speed from a distance and a time, or from a frequency and a
wavelength. They differ in what makes them uncertain.

- In the **echo method**, the largest uncertainty is your own reaction time. A person
  takes about 0.2 s to start and stop a stopwatch, which is a fifth of the 0.80 s in
  Example 1. Averaging several readings reduces this, but it does not remove it.
- In the **resonance-tube method**, there is no reaction time. The loudest point is found
  by ear, and the lengths are read from a metre stick. The uncertainty is smaller, and
  the result is usually closer to the accepted value.

> **Watch out.** In the echo method the sound travels to the wall **and back**, so the
> distance covered is twice the distance to the wall. Dividing the time by two, instead
> of the distance, gives an answer that is out by a factor of four. Write the factor of
> two into the equation, $v = \dfrac{2d}{t}$, before you substitute any numbers.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The ambulance is coming towards you, and you hear its siren.

1. The siren takes 2.0 s to reach you after the driver sounds it. Calculate your distance
   from the ambulance.
2. State why you see the ambulance's blue light before you hear the siren.
3. Explain why the speed of the sound in the air is set by the air and not by the
   ambulance.

Keep your answers. Section 11.3 looks at why the note is louder when the ambulance is
near, and Section 11.4 looks at why its pitch changes.

## You should now be able to

- state that the speed of sound in air is about 340 m/s, and that it is larger in water
  and larger again in steel;
- describe how to measure the speed of sound by the echo method, and use
  $v = \dfrac{2d}{t}$;
- describe how a resonance tube measures the wavelength of a sound, and use
  $\lambda = 2(L_2 - L_1)$;
- explain why the difference between the two resonance lengths is used, rather than one
  length on its own;
- say why the echo method is limited by reaction time and the resonance method is not.
