# Chapter 11: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The siren that changes note.** An ambulance siren makes a steady note of 700 Hz. The
ambulance travels at 30 m/s along a straight road past you, and you stand at the roadside.
The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s.

In these formulas $\lambda$ is the wavelength, $v$ and $c$ are both the speed of
sound, $f$ the frequency the source makes and $s$ the distance the sound travels.
Also $f'$ is the observed frequency and $u$ the speed of the source.

**Step 1: what carries the sound.** The siren sound is a longitudinal wave. The air
particles vibrate back and forth along the direction the sound travels, bunching into
compressions and spreading into rarefactions. The wave carries energy from the siren to
your ear, and the air stays where it is. Sound is a mechanical wave, so it needs the air;
in a vacuum you would hear nothing.

**Step 2: the wavelength, and the time.** The siren makes 700 Hz and the sound travels at
340 m/s, so the wavefronts are spaced

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{340}{700} = 0.49\,\text{m}$$

apart while the ambulance is stationary. If the siren sounds and the sound takes 2.0 s to
reach you, the distance is

$$s = vt = 340 \times 2.0 = 680\,\text{m}$$

**Step 3: why it is loud.** The loudness is set by the amplitude of the sound wave. When
the ambulance is near, the wave has not spread far and its amplitude is larger, so it
carries more energy to your ear and sounds louder. The pitch is set by the frequency, so
the two properties change independently.

**Step 4: why the note changes.** This is the Doppler effect. As the ambulance approaches,
each wavefront is sent from a point closer to you than the one before, so the wavefronts
are squeezed together. They arrive more often, so the frequency you hear is higher than
700 Hz. As the ambulance moves away, each wavefront is sent from a point farther from you,
so the wavefronts are stretched apart and arrive less often. The frequency you hear is
then lower than 700 Hz. The drop happens at the moment it passes, because that is when
its motion changes from towards you to away from you.

The speed of sound in the air stays at 340 m/s, and the siren keeps making 700 Hz. The
driver hears that steady 700 Hz, because the driver and the siren move together and there
is no relative motion between them.

**Step 5: the two frequencies.** For the approaching ambulance, use the minus sign:

$$f' = \frac{fc}{c - u} = \frac{700 \times 340}{340 - 30} = \frac{238000}{310}
= 768\,\text{Hz}$$

For the receding ambulance, use the plus sign:

$$f' = \frac{fc}{c + u} = \frac{700 \times 340}{340 + 30} = \frac{238000}{370}
= 643\,\text{Hz}$$

**The answer.** The sound reaches you as a longitudinal wave through the air. The
wavefronts are 0.49 m apart when the ambulance is at rest, and the siren you hear at
680 m takes 2.0 s to reach you. The note is louder when the ambulance is near, because the
amplitude is larger. As it approaches you hear 768 Hz, and after it passes you hear
643 Hz, even though the siren makes one steady 700 Hz and the driver hears no change.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies** and
say why. Several questions come from earlier chapters, and one asks you to explain rather
than calculate.

1. A sound wave travels through air at 340 m/s. Calculate the distance it travels in
   4.0 s.
2. A siren makes a note of 850 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the
   wavelength.
3. A wave has a frequency of 250 Hz and a wavelength of 1.2 m. Calculate its speed. Which
   chapter does this method come from?
4. A student claps once and hears the echo from a wall 0.50 s later. The speed of sound is
   340 m/s. Calculate the distance to the wall.
5. A tuning fork of frequency 425 Hz gives its first resonance at an air-column length of
   0.20 m and its second at 0.60 m. Calculate the speed of sound.
6. A violin and a flute play the same note at the same loudness. Explain why they sound
   different.
7. A car approaches a stationary listener at 25 m/s with its horn sounding at 640 Hz. The
   speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the frequency the listener hears.
8. The car has passed the listener and now moves away at 25 m/s. Calculate the frequency
   the listener hears now. Compare it with your answer to question 7.
9. A spring of spring constant 40 N/m is stretched by 0.15 m. Calculate the force needed.
   Which relationship did you use, and why?
10. A car starts from rest and accelerates at 2.5 m/s$^2$ for 6.0 s. Calculate its final
    speed and the distance it travels. Which chapter does this method come from?

## What you can now do

- explain that sound is a mechanical, longitudinal wave, and describe the compressions
  and rarefactions it makes;
- state that sound needs a medium, and describe the bell-jar evidence for it;
- state the speed of sound in air as about 340 m/s, and say how it compares in water and
  in steel;
- measure the speed of sound in air by the echo method, using $v = \dfrac{2d}{t}$;
- measure it with a resonance tube, using $\lambda = 2(L_2 - L_1)$ and $v = f\lambda$;
- relate the pitch to the frequency, the loudness to the amplitude, and the quality to
  the overtones present;
- state the frequency limits of human hearing, and describe infrasound and ultrasound;
- describe the Doppler effect, and explain why the wavefronts bunch in front of a moving
  source and spread behind it;
- explain why a moving source changes the note a stationary listener hears, while the
  driver hears no change;
- use $f' = \dfrac{fc}{c \pm u}$ to find an observed frequency, a source speed or an
  observed wavelength.

## Thinking back

Return to the sound you chose at the start of the chapter — the passing siren, the car on
a wet road, the train, the motorbike or the aircraft.

You can now explain the change in the note with the wavefronts. Write two or three
sentences: say what happens to the spacing of the wavefronts as the source comes towards
you, and what happens to the spacing as it moves away. Then say what the listener hears
in each case.

If you can estimate the speed of the source, you can put a number on the shift. A car on
a road travels at about 30 m/s and a train at about 50 m/s, and the speed of sound is
340 m/s. Use $f' = \dfrac{fc}{c \pm u}$ to work out the two frequencies you would hear
from a 700 Hz source at that speed. Compare the two answers with the 768 Hz and 643 Hz of
the chapter problem, and say which source gives the larger change.

If your estimate is rough, write that down as well. Saying how rough an estimate is
belongs in the physics, and it is what the Physics In Practice Investigation asks you to
do.
