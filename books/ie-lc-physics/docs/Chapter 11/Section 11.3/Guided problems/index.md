# 11.3 Pitch, loudness and quality — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. State what is meant by the amplitude of a wave.
2. The energy a wave carries is proportional to the square of its amplitude. One wave has
   twice the amplitude of another. How many times more energy does it carry?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The amplitude is the maximum displacement of a particle from its rest position. It is
   a distance, and it is always positive.
2. Four times as much energy, because $2^2 = 4$.

The second answer is the link between the size of a sound wave and its loudness. A larger
amplitude carries more energy to the ear.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** State which feature of a sound wave sets the pitch of a note, and which feature
sets its loudness.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One feature is measured in hertz and the other is a distance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The pitch goes with the frequency, and the loudness goes with the amplitude.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The **frequency** sets the pitch. A higher frequency means more vibrations each second,
so the note sounds higher. The frequency is measured in hertz.

The **amplitude** sets the loudness. A larger amplitude means the wave carries more
energy to your ear, so the note sounds louder.

The two are independent. A note can be high and quiet, or low and loud.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A note has a frequency of 500 Hz. Calculate its period.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The period is the reciprocal of the frequency.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$T = \dfrac{1}{500}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$T = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{500} = 0.0020\,\text{s}$$

That is 2.0 ms. The air completes 500 vibrations every second, so each one takes two
milliseconds.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the wavelength of a note of
frequency 20 kHz.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Convert the frequency to hertz first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$20\,\text{kHz} = 20000\,\text{Hz}$, then $\lambda = \dfrac{340}{20000}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{340}{20000} = 0.017\,\text{m}$$

That is 17 mm. This is the shortest wavelength you can hear, and it is the wavelength of
the highest note in the audible range.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** An ultrasound scan uses a frequency of 25 kHz in body tissue, where the speed of
sound is 1500 m/s. Calculate the wavelength in the tissue.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Convert the frequency to hertz, then use the wave equation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$25\,\text{kHz} = 25000\,\text{Hz}$, then $\lambda = \dfrac{1500}{25000}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{1500}{25000} = 0.060\,\text{m}$$

That is 60 mm. The wavelength is short enough to reflect from the boundaries between
different tissues, which is what builds up the image.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain why a violin and a flute can play the same note at the same loudness and
still sound different.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The pitch comes from the fundamental frequency. What else does an instrument produce
alongside it?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Each instrument produces weaker overtones at whole-number multiples of the fundamental,
and the mixture differs.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Both instruments produce the same fundamental frequency, so the notes have the same
pitch. Both are played at the same amplitude, so they have the same loudness.

Each instrument also produces weaker **overtones** at whole-number multiples of the
fundamental frequency. The violin and the flute produce different mixtures of overtones,
with different amplitudes at each multiple. That mixture is the **quality**, or timbre,
of the note, and it is what makes the two instruments recognisable.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** One note has a frequency of 300 Hz and a second has a frequency of 600 Hz. State
which note has the higher pitch, and describe how the two frequencies are related.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the two numbers. What happens to the pitch when the frequency doubles?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

600 is twice 300. Doubling the frequency raises the pitch by one octave.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The 600 Hz note has the higher pitch, because its frequency is larger. Its frequency is
exactly twice that of the 300 Hz note.

Doubling the frequency raises the pitch by one octave, so the two notes are an octave
apart. They sound pleasing together because their frequencies are in the ratio $2 : 1$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 9

**7.** The energy a wave carries is proportional to the square of its amplitude. A wave's
amplitude is trebled. By what factor does the energy it carries increase?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Square the factor by which the amplitude changed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The energy factor is $3^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$3^2 = 9$$

The wave carries nine times as much energy. Trebling the amplitude is a large change: the
energy grows with the square, so it grows much faster than the amplitude does. That is
why a small turn of a volume control can make a large change in loudness.

</details>
</details>
</details>
