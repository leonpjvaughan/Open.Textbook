# 11.3 Pitch, loudness and quality — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the amplitude of a wave from Section 9.1; frequency and the wave
equation from Section 9.2; the speed of sound from Section 11.1.

**Objectives:** relate the pitch of a note to the frequency of the sound wave; relate the
loudness to its amplitude; relate the quality to the overtones present; state the
frequency limits of human hearing, and describe ultrasound and one use of it.

By the end of this section, you can describe any note by three separate properties and
say which feature of its wave sets each one.

## Pitch and frequency

Play two notes on a piano and one sounds higher than the other. That difference is the
**pitch**.

The pitch of a note is set by the **frequency** of the sound wave, which is the number of
complete vibrations each second, measured in hertz. A higher frequency gives a higher
pitch. A frequency of 440 Hz is the A above middle C, and 880 Hz is the A an octave
higher.

Doubling the frequency raises the pitch by one **octave**. So 220 Hz, 440 Hz and 880 Hz
are all A notes, an octave apart. The two notes of an octave sound pleasing together
because their frequencies are in the simple ratio $2 : 1$.

## Loudness and amplitude

Strike a drum gently and then hard. The note has the same pitch both times, but the
second strike is louder. That difference is the **loudness**.

The loudness of a note is set by the **amplitude** of the sound wave, which is the
maximum displacement of the air particles from their rest position. A larger amplitude
means the wave carries more energy to your ear, so the note sounds louder.

The energy a wave carries is proportional to the square of its amplitude. Doubling the
amplitude gives four times the energy. That is why a small increase in amplitude makes a
noticeable increase in loudness.

Pitch and loudness are independent. You can play a note loudly or quietly without
changing its pitch, and you can play a high note or a low note at the same loudness.

## The range of hearing

A healthy young person hears frequencies from about **20 Hz** to about **20 kHz**
(20000 Hz). The upper limit falls slowly with age, so an older listener may not hear a
note near 15 kHz that a child hears without difficulty.

Frequencies outside that range still exist as sound waves, even though you cannot hear
them.

- **Infrasound** has a frequency below 20 Hz. Earthquakes and some large machines produce
  it. Elephants and whales use it to communicate over long distances.
- **Ultrasound** has a frequency above 20 kHz. Bats and dolphins use it to find their way.
  Doctors use it for scans, and engineers use it to check for cracks inside metal.

**Example 1.** A note has a frequency of 440 Hz. Calculate its period.

$$
T = \frac{1}{f}
$$

where $T$ is the period and $f$ is the frequency.

$$
T = \frac{1}{440}
$$

$$
T = 0.00227\,\text{s}
$$

The air completes 440 vibrations every second, so each one takes about 2.3 ms.

**Example 2.** The highest frequency a young person can hear is about 20 kHz. The speed
of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the wavelength of this sound.

$$
\lambda = \frac{v}{f}
$$

where $\lambda$ is the wavelength and $v$ is the speed of sound.

$$
\lambda = \frac{340}{20000}
$$

$$
\lambda = 0.017\,\text{m}
$$

That is 17 mm, a little larger than the width of your little finger.

**Example 3.** An ultrasound scan uses a frequency of 40 kHz. The speed of sound in body
tissue is 1500 m/s. Calculate the wavelength in the tissue.

$$
\lambda = \frac{v}{f}
$$

$$
\lambda = \frac{1500}{40000}
$$

$$
\lambda = 0.038\,\text{m}
$$

A short wavelength reflects from small structures inside the body, which is how an
ultrasound image shows the shape of an organ without any surgery.

## Quality and overtones

A violin and a flute can play the same note at the same loudness, and still sound
different. That difference is the **quality**, or timbre, of the note.

Most instruments do not produce a single frequency. They produce the **fundamental**
frequency, which sets the pitch, together with weaker **overtones** at whole-number
multiples of it. A note of 440 Hz may also contain 880 Hz, 1320 Hz and 1760 Hz at lower
amplitudes. The mixture of overtones is different for each instrument, and that mixture
is what you hear as the quality.

A pure tone from a signal generator has no overtones, and it sounds thin and plain. A
violin note has a rich mixture, and it sounds full. The pitch is the same.

> **Watch out.** Loudness and pitch are not the same thing. A loud low note and a quiet
> high note differ in both properties at once. Keep them separate: the frequency sets
> the pitch, and the amplitude sets the loudness. When a question asks why a note sounds
> higher, look at the frequency; when it asks why the note is louder, look at the
> amplitude.

1. The siren is louder when the ambulance is near. State what happens to the amplitude of
