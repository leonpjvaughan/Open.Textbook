# Chapter 7: Circular motion

A washing machine spins a wet towel at 1400 revolutions per minute. The towel stays
pressed against the wall of the drum, and the water does not. Something must push the
towel inwards, all the way round, many times every second. In this chapter you find out
what that push is, how large it must be, and why it points towards the centre.

## The chapter problem

**The spin cycle.** A washing machine drum has a radius of 0.24 m. During the spin cycle
it turns at 1400 revolutions per minute. A wet towel of mass 0.20 kg lies against the
wall of the drum.

These are the facts to use all the way through the chapter.

- the radius of the drum is **0.24 m**;
- the drum turns at **1400 revolutions per minute**;
- the mass of the wet towel is **0.20 kg**;
- the gravitational field strength is **9.8 N/kg**.

By the end of the chapter you can find the period of one turn, the angular velocity of
the drum, the speed of the drum wall, the centripetal acceleration of the towel, and the
force the wall must exert on it. You can also say how slowly the drum could turn before
the towel would stop staying against the top of the drum.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- define the period and the frequency of a circular motion, and convert between them;
- define angular velocity and use $\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$;
- use $v = r\omega$ to link the speed of a point to the angular velocity;
- explain why an object moving in a circle at a constant speed is accelerating;
- use $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$ and state the direction of the acceleration;
- use $F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$ and name the force that supplies it;
- explain why there is no outward force on an object moving in a circle;
- solve vertical-circle and banked-bend problems by choosing the forces that act towards
  the centre.

## Where it is used

Spin dryers and centrifuges separate water from clothes by spinning it out. Cars and
trains need friction to turn a bend, and racing tracks bank the corners to help. The
hammer throw and the discus leave the athlete's hand along the tangent. Satellites and
the Moon stay in orbit because gravity bends their path. In Chapter 15 you will meet an
electron steered round a circle by a magnetic field.
