# 7.3 Centripetal force and its sources — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Newton's second law from Chapter 3; weight and $W = mg$ from
Section 6.1; the centripetal acceleration from Section 7.2.

**Objectives:** use $F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$; name the force that supplies the
centripetal force in different situations; explain why there is no outward force on an
object moving in a circle.

By the end of this section, you can work out how large the inward force must be, and say
which real force provides it.

## A force is needed

An object that accelerates needs a resultant force. The centripetal acceleration points
towards the centre, so the resultant force must point towards the centre as well. That
resultant force is called the **centripetal force**.

Newton's second law, $F = ma$, with $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ gives

$$F = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

and with $a = \omega^2 r$ it gives

$$F = m\omega^2 r$$

- $F$ is the centripetal force, in newtons (N);
- $m$ is the mass, in kilograms (kg);
- $v$ is the speed, in metres per second (m/s);
- $r$ is the radius of the circle, in metres (m).

The centripetal force is not a new kind of force. It is a job that has to be done, and
some real force does it.

## What supplies the force

The same job is done by different forces in different situations.

| Situation | Force towards the centre |
|---|---|
| A ball on a string | the tension in the string |
| A car on a bend | friction between the tyres and the road |
| A satellite in orbit | gravity |
| Clothes in a spin dryer | the push of the drum wall |
| An electron in a magnetic field | the magnetic force |

In every case you find the resultant force that points towards the centre, and you set
it equal to $\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.

**Example 1.** A 0.50 kg stone on a string of length 0.80 m moves at 3.0 m/s in a
horizontal circle. Calculate the tension in the string.

$$
F = \frac{mv^2}{r}
$$

$$
F = \frac{0.50 \times 3.0^2}{0.80} = \frac{4.5}{0.80} = 5.6\,\text{N}
$$

**Example 2.** A car of mass 1200 kg goes round a bend of radius 50 m at 20 m/s.
Calculate the friction force needed.

$$
F = \frac{mv^2}{r}
$$

$$
F = \frac{1200 \times 20^2}{50} = \frac{480000}{50} = 9.6 \times 10^{3}\,\text{N}
$$

On a wet road the friction is smaller, so the car must slow down to take the same bend.

**Example 3.** The wet towel of mass 0.20 kg lies against the wall of the drum of radius
0.24 m. The angular velocity is 146.6 rad/s. Calculate the force the wall exerts on the
towel.

$$
F = m\omega^2 r
$$

$$
F = 0.20 \times 146.6^2 \times 0.24 = 1.0 \times 10^{3}\,\text{N}
$$

The weight of the towel is only $0.20 \times 9.8 = 2.0\,\text{N}$. The wall pushes about
500 times harder than gravity pulls.

## There is no outward force

It feels as though something throws you outwards on a roundabout. No outward force acts
on you.

- The only resultant force on you points inwards, and it comes from the seat or the
  door.
- If that inward force stopped, you would move in a straight line along the tangent. You
  would not move outwards along the radius.
- The feeling of being thrown out is your body's inertia resisting the inward push.

A force pointing away from the centre is sometimes called centrifugal. There is no such
force acting on the object. The word describes the sensation, not a force.

> **Watch out.** Do not add a centripetal force to the other forces as if it were an
> extra push. The centripetal force **is** the resultant of the forces that already act.
> For a satellite it is gravity, not gravity plus a centripetal force. Drawing both
> doubles the force and gives the wrong answer.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The towel has a mass of 0.20 kg, the drum has a radius of
0.24 m, and the angular velocity is 146.6 rad/s.

1. Calculate the force the drum wall exerts on the towel, using $F = m\omega^2 r$.
2. Calculate the weight of the towel.
3. Write one sentence saying which force supplies the centripetal force on the towel.

Keep your answers. Section 7.4 uses the same method for vertical circles and banked
bends, where the direction of the weight changes as the object turns.

## You should now be able to

- use $F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ and $F = m\omega^2 r$ in either direction;
- name the force that supplies the centripetal force for a ball on a string, a car on a
  bend, a satellite and clothes in a spin dryer;
- explain that the centripetal force is the resultant force, not an extra one;
- compare a centripetal force with the weight of the object;
- explain why there is no outward force on an object moving in a circle.
