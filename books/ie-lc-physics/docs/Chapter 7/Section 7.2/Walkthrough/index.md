# 7.2 Centripetal acceleration — Learn

**Prerequisites:** velocity and acceleration from Chapter 2; work and energy from
Chapter 4; angular velocity and the rim speed from Section 7.1.

**Objectives:** explain why an object moving in a circle at a constant speed is
accelerating; use $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ and $a = \omega^2 r$; state the direction of the
centripetal acceleration.

By the end of this section, you can work out how large the acceleration of a turning
object is, and say which way it points.

## Velocity changes direction

A car going round a roundabout at a steady 20 km/h has a constant speed. It is still
accelerating, because acceleration is the rate of change of **velocity**, and velocity
is a vector.

- The size of the velocity is the speed. That stays the same.
- The direction of the velocity changes all the time, because the car keeps turning.

A change in either the size or the direction is a change in velocity, so it counts as an
acceleration. The acceleration of an object moving in a circle at a constant speed is
called the **centripetal acceleration**.

## The direction of the acceleration

Picture a stone on a string, whirled in a horizontal circle. At one instant the stone
moves along the tangent to the circle. A moment later it moves along a slightly
different tangent, turned towards the centre. The change in velocity points towards the
centre.

So the centripetal acceleration points towards the centre of the circle at every
instant. It is at right angles to the velocity. That is why the speed does not change: a
force at right angles to the motion does no work on the object.

## The size of the acceleration

The acceleration grows with the speed and falls with the radius:

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r}$$

- $a$ is the centripetal acceleration, in metres per second squared (m/s²);
- $v$ is the speed, in metres per second (m/s);
- $r$ is the radius of the circle, in metres (m).

The speed is squared, so doubling the speed makes the acceleration four times larger.
Halving the radius doubles the acceleration.

Because $v = r\omega$, there is a second form. Substituting $r\omega$ for $v$ gives

$$a = \frac{(r\omega)^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$$

Use $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ when you know the speed, and $a = \omega^2 r$ when you know the
angular velocity. The two give the same answer, because they are the same physics.

**Example 1.** A car goes round a bend of radius 50 m at 20 m/s. Calculate its
centripetal acceleration.

$$
a = \frac{v^2}{r}
$$

$$
a = \frac{20^2}{50} = \frac{400}{50} = 8.0\,\text{m/s}^{2}
$$

**Example 2.** A stone on a string of length 0.80 m moves at 3.0 m/s in a horizontal
circle. Calculate its centripetal acceleration.

$$
a = \frac{v^2}{r}
$$

$$
a = \frac{3.0^2}{0.80} = \frac{9.0}{0.80} = 11\,\text{m/s}^{2}
$$

**Example 3.** A point on the wall of the drum in the chapter problem has an angular
velocity of 146.6 rad/s. The drum has a radius of 0.24 m. Calculate the centripetal
acceleration of the point.

$$
a = \omega^2 r
$$

$$
a = 146.6^2 \times 0.24
$$

$$
a = 2.15 \times 10^{4} \times 0.24 = 5.2 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m/s}^{2}
$$

That is about 530 times the acceleration of free fall. The point on the drum wall is
pulled towards the centre far harder than gravity pulls it down.

> **Watch out.** The radius is the distance from the point to the **centre** of the
> circle, not the diameter. A roundabout with a diameter of 40 m has a radius of 20 m.
> Putting 40 into $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ gives half the correct acceleration, because the
> radius is twice as large as it should be. Check which one the question gives you.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The drum has a radius of 0.24 m, an angular velocity of
146.6 rad/s, and the wall moves at 35.2 m/s.

1. Calculate the centripetal acceleration of a point on the wall, using
   $a = \omega^2 r$.
2. Calculate the same acceleration using $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$.
3. Calculate how many times larger this acceleration is than
   $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^{2}$.

Keep your answers. Section 7.3 multiplies the acceleration by the mass of the towel to
find the force the wall must exert.

## You should now be able to

- explain why an object moving in a circle at a constant speed is accelerating;
- state that the centripetal acceleration points towards the centre;
- use $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ and $a = \omega^2 r$ in either direction;
- say why the radius must be measured to the centre, and not use the diameter;
- work out how many times larger a centripetal acceleration is than $g$;
- explain why a force at right angles to the motion does no work.
