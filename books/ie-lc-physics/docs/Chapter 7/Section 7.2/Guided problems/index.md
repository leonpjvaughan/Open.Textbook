# 7.2 Centripetal acceleration — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A car speeds up from 10 m/s to 20 m/s in 5.0 s. Calculate its acceleration.
2. A stone on a string moves at 4.0 m/s in a circle of radius 0.50 m. Calculate the
   angle it turns through in one second.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{20 - 10}{5.0} = 2.0\,\text{m/s}^{2}$.
2. $\omega = \dfrac{v}{r} = \dfrac{4.0}{0.50} = 8.0\,\text{rad/s}$, so it turns through
   8.0 radians each second.

Both ideas return here. The first is acceleration from a change in velocity, and the
second is the angular velocity that leads to the centripetal acceleration.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A car goes round a bend of radius 80 m at 16 m/s. Calculate its centripetal
acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{16^2}{80}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{16^2}{80} = \frac{256}{80} = 3.2\,\text{m/s}^{2}$$

The acceleration points towards the centre of the bend, so it points sideways across
the car.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A stone on a string of length 0.50 m moves at 4.0 m/s. Calculate its centripetal
acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The string is 0.50 m long, so the radius is 0.50 m.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{4.0^2}{0.50}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{4.0^2}{0.50} = \frac{16}{0.50} = 32\,\text{m/s}^{2}$$

That is more than three times the acceleration of free fall, and it is produced by a
piece of string.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A wheel of radius 0.60 m turns with an angular velocity of 3.0 rad/s. Calculate
the centripetal acceleration of a point on the rim.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You are given the angular velocity, so use $a = \omega^2 r$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = 3.0^2 \times 0.60$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \omega^2 r = 3.0^2 \times 0.60 = 9.0 \times 0.60 = 5.4\,\text{m/s}^{2}$$

The same point also has a speed of $v = r\omega = 0.60 \times 3.0 = 1.8\,\text{m/s}$,
and $\dfrac{1.8^2}{0.60}$ gives the same 5.4 m/s².

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A point on the rim of a wheel of radius 0.20 m has a centripetal acceleration of
45 m/s². Calculate its speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ for $v$. The speed is the square root of $ar$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \sqrt{45 \times 0.20}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \sqrt{ar} = \sqrt{45 \times 0.20} = \sqrt{9.0} = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$$

The speed is 3.0 m/s. A larger acceleration would need a larger speed, because the
acceleration grows with the square of the speed.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** The speed of an object in a circle is tripled while the radius stays the same.
State what happens to its centripetal acceleration, and explain why.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The speed appears squared in $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out $3^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The acceleration is multiplied by $3^2 = 9$, so it becomes nine times larger.

In $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$, the speed is squared. Replacing $v$ with $3v$ puts $9v^2$ in
the numerator, so the whole expression is nine times its original value. An answer of
three comes from forgetting the square.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain why an object moving in a circle at a constant speed is accelerating.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. What is changing, if the speed is
constant?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Velocity is a vector, so it has a direction as well as a size. The direction changes as
the object turns.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Velocity is a vector, so a change in direction is a change in velocity, just as a change
in speed is. The object keeps turning, so the direction of its velocity changes all the
time.

A change in velocity is an acceleration. The acceleration points towards the centre of
the circle, and it is called the centripetal acceleration. The speed stays constant
because the acceleration is at right angles to the velocity.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** A satellite orbits at $7.0 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$ from the centre of the Earth
with a speed of $7.5 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m/s}$. Calculate its centripetal acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ with both numbers in standard form.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{(7.5 \times 10^{3})^2}{7.0 \times 10^{6}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(7.5 \times 10^{3})^2}{7.0 \times 10^{6}} = \frac{5.6 \times 10^{7}}{7.0 \times 10^{6}} = 8.0\,\text{m/s}^{2}$$

The acceleration is a little less than $g$. Gravity provides it, which is why the
satellite stays in its circular path.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 7.1

**8.** A wheel of radius 0.30 m turns at 4.0 revolutions per second. Calculate its
angular velocity and the speed of a point on its rim.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $\omega = 2\pi f$, then $v = r\omega$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\omega = 2\pi \times 4.0 = 25\,\text{rad/s}$, then $v = 0.30 \times 25$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times 4.0 = 25\,\text{rad/s}$$

$$v = r\omega = 0.30 \times 25 = 7.5\,\text{m/s}$$

Those two values would then give $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \dfrac{7.5^2}{0.30} =
188\,\text{m/s}^{2}$, which is about 19 times $g$.

</details>
</details>
</details>
