# Chapter 7: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The spin cycle.** A washing machine drum has a radius of 0.24 m and turns at 1400
revolutions per minute. A wet towel of mass 0.20 kg lies against the wall of the drum.

In these formulas $f$ is the frequency, $T$ the period, $\omega$ the angular velocity,
$v$ the speed of a point on the wall and $r$ the radius of the drum. $a$ is the
centripetal acceleration, $F$ the force the wall exerts, $m$ the mass of the towel and
$g$ the gravitational field strength.

**Step 1: the frequency and the period.** The rate in revolutions per minute must be
converted into hertz first.

$$f = \frac{1400}{60} = 23.3\,\text{Hz}$$

$$T = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{23.3} = 0.043\,\text{s}$$

The drum completes one turn in 43 milliseconds.

**Step 2: the angular velocity.** One turn is $2\pi$ radians, so

$$\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times 23.3 = 1.5 \times 10^{2}\,\text{rad/s}$$

**Step 3: the speed of the drum wall.** A point on the wall is 0.24 m from the axis:

$$v = r\omega = 0.24 \times 146.6 = 35\,\text{m/s}$$

That is about 130 km/h, and the point is travelling in a circle rather than along the
road.

**Step 4: the centripetal acceleration.** The wall point turns a corner every instant:

$$a = \omega^2 r = 146.6^2 \times 0.24 = 5.2 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m/s}^{2}$$

Dividing by $g$ gives $\dfrac{5.2 \times 10^{3}}{9.8} = 5.3 \times 10^{2}$, so the
acceleration is about 530 times the acceleration of free fall. The check with
$a = \dfrac{v^2}{r}$ gives the same value, because $v = r\omega$ links the two forms.

**Step 5: the force the wall exerts.** Newton's second law turns the acceleration into a
force:

$$F = ma = 0.20 \times 5.2 \times 10^{3} = 1.0 \times 10^{3}\,\text{N}$$

The weight of the towel is only $W = mg = 0.20 \times 9.8 = 2.0\,\text{N}$. The wall
pushes on the towel with a force about 500 times larger than its weight, and the towel
pushes back on the drum with the same size of force. That is why an uneven load makes a
machine shake.

**Step 6: the slowest spin that keeps the towel on the wall.** At the top of the drum,
gravity points towards the centre and helps. The towel stays in contact when the
wall pushes with zero force, so the weight alone supplies the centripetal force:

$$mg = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

The mass cancels, giving $v = \sqrt{gr} = \sqrt{9.8 \times 0.24} = 1.53\,\text{m/s}$. The
angular velocity is $\omega = \dfrac{v}{r} = \dfrac{1.53}{0.24} = 6.4\,\text{rad/s}$, and
the frequency is $f = \dfrac{\omega}{2\pi} = \dfrac{6.4}{2\pi} = 1.02\,\text{rev/s}$,
which is **61 revolutions per minute**.

**The answer.** The drum turns at 23.3 Hz, with a period of 0.043 s and an angular
velocity of $1.5 \times 10^{2}\,\text{rad/s}$. A point on the wall moves at 35 m/s and
accelerates towards the centre at $5.2 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m/s}^{2}$. The wall pushes on
the towel with a force of $1.0 \times 10^{3}\,\text{N}$, about 500 times its weight.
Below about 61 revolutions per minute the towel would stop staying against the top of
the drum, which is why a washing machine tumbles its load at low speed and presses it to
the wall only in the spin cycle.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies**
and say why. Two of them ask you to choose between the two forms of the centripetal
force, and one asks you to decide which earlier chapter the method comes from.

1. A wheel turns 240 times in 60 s. Calculate its frequency, its period and its angular
   velocity.
2. A car goes round a bend of radius 40 m at 12 m/s. Calculate its centripetal
   acceleration.
3. A 0.30 kg ball on a string of length 0.50 m moves at 6.0 m/s. Calculate the tension
   in the string.
4. A bend of radius 60 m is banked so that no friction is needed at 20 m/s. Calculate
   the banking angle. Which equation did you use, and why does the mass of the car not
   appear in it?
5. A satellite orbits $4.22 \times 10^{7}\,\text{m}$ from the centre of the Earth.
   Calculate its speed and its period. Which earlier section does this method come
   from?
6. A 0.20 kg stone on a string of length 0.40 m moves at 5.0 m/s. Calculate the tension.
   Is it larger or smaller than your answer to question 3, and why?
7. A car of mass 1100 kg goes round a bend of radius 55 m. The friction force is 7000 N.
   Calculate the maximum speed of the car.
8. A bucket of water is swung in a vertical circle of radius 0.60 m. Calculate the
   minimum speed at the top.
9. A wheel of radius 0.25 m rolls with a rim speed of 4.0 m/s. Calculate its angular
   velocity and its period.
10. Explain why an object moving in a circle at a constant speed is accelerating, and
    why there is no outward force on it.

## What you can now do

- define the period and the frequency, and convert a rate in revolutions per minute into
  hertz;
- use $\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$ and $v = r\omega$;
- explain why a constant speed can still be an acceleration;
- use $a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$ and state that the acceleration points towards
  the centre;
- use $F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$ and name the force that supplies it;
- compare a centripetal force with the weight of the object;
- explain why there is no outward force on an object moving in a circle;
- write $T + mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ at the top of a vertical circle and
  $T - mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ at the bottom, and find the minimum speed from
  $v = \sqrt{gr}$;
- find the banking angle of a bend from $\tan\theta = \dfrac{v^2}{rg}$;
- set gravity equal to the centripetal force for a satellite and recover
  $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$.

## Thinking back

Look at the place you chose at the start of the chapter, where you felt pressed into a
seat or a wall as something turned.

You can now say which force pushed you. It pointed towards the centre of the turn, and
it came from the seat, the door or the floor. You can also say why nothing pushed you
outwards: the outward feeling was your own inertia resisting that inward push.

Write down one number for your own choice. It could be the radius of the turn, the
speed, or the time for one complete circle. Then work out the centripetal acceleration,
and compare it with $g$. If your number is a rough estimate, write that down as well.
Saying how rough an estimate is belongs in the physics, and it is what the Physics In
Practice Investigation asks you to do.
