# 22.5 Writing the report — Learn

**Prerequisites:** a question from Section 22.1, sources from Section 22.2, a
method from Section 22.3, and processed data from Section 22.4.

**Objectives:** describe the sections a report needs and what each contains; write
a conclusion that the evidence supports; give an honest reflection; relate the
result to society; use a source without plagiarism; present a table and a graph so
a reader can check them.

By the end of this section you can write the account of your investigation.

## The report format

The SEC prescribes the format of the report, and the brief and its guidance state
what is required. Read them before you write, because the requirements can change.
The sections below are the ones a scientific report normally has, and they fit the
four qualities the SEC describes.

| Section | What it contains |
|---|---|
| Title and purpose | The question, and the physics it uses |
| Background research | What the sources say, and how reliable they are |
| Hypothesis | The prediction, and the physics that supports it |
| Method | The steps another person would follow to repeat the readings |
| Results | The readings, in a table, with the quantity and unit in each heading |
| Analysis | The graph, the mean, the uncertainty and the gradient |
| Conclusion | What the evidence shows, with numbers |
| Evaluation | What limited the result, and what you would change |
| References | Every source, with enough detail to find it |

Your **investigative log** is the record you keep as you work. It holds the dates,
the readings, the problems and the decisions. The log is where the honest account
comes from, because it was written while the work was happening.

## Writing a conclusion the evidence supports

A conclusion is a claim, and the evidence must carry it. Write it with a number and
a reason, and say how certain the result is.

**Example 1.** A graph of voltage against angle has its largest value at 0°, and
the voltage falls as the angle grows. Write the conclusion.

The evidence supports the hypothesis: the voltage is largest when the cell faces
the lamp, at 0°, and it falls as the cell turns away. At 0° the mean voltage is
2.40 V, and at 60° it is 1.20 V. The uncertainty in each mean is about 0.05 V, so
the fall is much larger than the uncertainty.

The conclusion states the pattern, gives the numbers, and compares the effect with
the uncertainty. A conclusion without a number cannot be checked.

## The honest reflection

The SEC descriptors reward a reflection that says what worked and what did not,
and what you would change. An idealised account, in which everything went well,
tells the reader nothing about your understanding.

Ask three questions, and answer each with a detail from your log.

1. What limited the result most, and how large was it?
2. Which decision would you make differently, and why?
3. What would you investigate next, now that you have these readings?

An honest reflection also records the new directions the work suggested. If a
reading made you change the method halfway through, that is worth a paragraph,
because it shows how the investigation unfolded.

## Relating the result to society

The investigation is judged partly on how you connect it to the real world.
Describe what the result means for people, and weigh the factors honestly.

- **Economic.** What does the result cost or save?
- **Social.** Who is affected, and how?
- **Sustainability.** Does it reduce the use of a resource, or increase it?
- **Ethical.** Is there a question of fairness, safety or consent?

**Example 2.** Relate a result about home insulation to society.

The result shows that the heat lost from a room falls as the insulation thickens.
For a household, that means a lower heating bill and a warmer room. The insulation
costs money to fit, so the saving takes some years to repay the cost. It also
reduces the carbon dioxide from heating, which matters beyond the household. A fair
recommendation states the payback time and says who would benefit.

## Presenting a table and a graph

A reader must be able to check your work from what you present.

- Write the quantity and the unit in each column heading, such as **Time / s**.
- Use the same number of decimal places for readings from the same instrument.
- Label both axes of the graph, with the unit, and put the independent variable on
  the horizontal axis.
- Plot the readings as points, draw a line of best fit, and state the gradient with
  its unit.

## Using sources without plagiarism

Plagiarism is using somebody else's work as if it were your own. Three habits avoid
it.

1. Write the idea in your own words, then check that the meaning has not changed.
2. If you use the exact words, put them in quotation marks and keep the quotation
   short.
3. Give the source for every idea or figure you take, in the list of references.

## Planning the length

A report has a word limit, and a plan keeps the writing inside it.

**Example 3.** The limit is 1200 words. A student writes 420 words of background
research and 300 words of method. Find the percentage of the limit used.

The student has written $420 + 300 = 720$ words. As a percentage of the limit,

$$\frac{720}{1200} \times 100\% = 60\%$$

So 40% of the limit is left for the results, the analysis, the conclusion and the
evaluation. Those are the sections that carry the marks for investigating and
communicating, so they need room.

> **Watch out.** Do not write the report as if everything went to plan. A report
> that says the method worked perfectly, with no anomalous readings and no
> problems, is an idealised account, and the SEC descriptors mark it down. The
> contrast is an account that names the difficulty, says how large it was, and says
> what you did about it. That is what an honest scientific account looks like.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. Your investigation ends with the report.

1. Write the conclusion for your investigation, with a number and a reason.
2. Write the reflection: what limited the result, and what you would change.
3. Write two sentences relating the result to society.

Keep the answers. The Review returns to your investigation as a whole and asks you
to judge it against the four qualities.

## You should now be able to

- describe the sections a report needs and what each contains;
- write a conclusion that the evidence supports, with a number and a reason;
- give an honest reflection on what limited the result;
- relate the result to society, weighing economic, social and ethical factors;
- present a table and a graph that a reader can check;
- use a source without plagiarism, and give the reference.
