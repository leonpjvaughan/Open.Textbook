# 22.6 Reviewing the whole course — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. State the equation that links voltage, current and resistance.
2. Convert 2500 m to kilometres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $V = IR$, where $V$ is the voltage in volts, $I$ is the current in amperes and
   $R$ is the resistance in ohms. The equation holds for a resistor at a fixed
   temperature.
2. $2500\,\text{m} = 2.5\,\text{km}$. There are 1000 m in a kilometre, so dividing
   by 1000 converts metres to kilometres.

Both return here. The review draws on every strand, and every answer is written
with its unit.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A trolley travels 1.20 m in 0.40 s. Calculate its average speed, to the
significant figures the data support.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Speed is distance divided by time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \dfrac{1.20}{0.40}$, and both readings carry two significant figures.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \frac{d}{t} = \frac{1.20}{0.40} = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$$

Both readings carry two significant figures, so the answer does too. The answer is
written as 3.0, not 3, because the trailing zero shows the precision.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A car accelerates from rest at 2.0 m/s$^2$ for 5.0 s. Calculate its final
speed, to the significant figures the data support.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $v = u + at$, and remember that the car starts from rest.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = 0 + 2.0 \times 5.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = u + at = 0 + 2.0 \times 5.0 = 10\,\text{m/s}$$

To two significant figures the answer is $1.0 \times 10^1$ m/s. The trailing zero
must be shown, so the answer is written in scientific notation.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A resultant force of 12 N acts on a mass of 3.0 kg. Calculate the
acceleration, to the significant figures the data support.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use Newton's second law, $F = ma$, rearranged for the acceleration.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{12}{3.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{12}{3.0} = 4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The mass carries two significant figures, so the answer does too. The unit,
m/s$^2$, is the unit of the quantity the question asked for.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A force of 50 N acts at 30$^\circ$ above the horizontal. Calculate its
horizontal and vertical components.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The horizontal component uses the cosine of the angle, and the vertical component
uses the sine.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$x = 50\cos 30^\circ$ and $y = 50\sin 30^\circ$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$x = 50\cos 30^\circ = 43.3\,\text{N} \qquad y = 50\sin 30^\circ = 25\,\text{N}$$

To two significant figures the horizontal component is $4.3 \times 10^1$ N, and the
vertical component is 25 N. The two components together are equivalent to the
original force, so they add as vectors rather than as numbers.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A velocity-time graph passes through (0 s, 0 m/s), (2.0 s, 6.0 m/s) and
(4.0 s, 12 m/s). Find the gradient, with its unit, and say what it represents.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Take two points far apart, and divide the change in velocity by the change in time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{12 - 6.0}{4.0 - 2.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{gradient} = \frac{12 - 6.0}{4.0 - 2.0} = \frac{6.0}{2.0} = 3.0\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The gradient of a velocity-time graph is the acceleration. The unit of the gradient
is the unit of velocity divided by the unit of time, which is m/s$^2$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain why checking the units of an answer can show that it is wrong, and
give one example.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Every term in an equation must have the same unit.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Speed is metres per second, so an answer in metres cannot be a speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Every term in an equation must have the same unit, so a mismatch means the equation
cannot be right. For example, speed is measured in metres per second, so an answer
in metres cannot be a speed.

The check cannot prove the number is right, because a wrong value can still carry
the correct unit. It can prove a value is wrong, which is why it is worth doing.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 1

**7.** A student measures the same length five times and records 0.512 m, 0.510 m,
0.514 m, 0.513 m and 0.511 m. Calculate the mean and the uncertainty as half the
range.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the readings and divide by five. Then find the range.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The mean is $\dfrac{0.512 + 0.510 + 0.514 + 0.513 + 0.511}{5}$. The range is
$0.514 - 0.510$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The mean is

$$\frac{0.512 + 0.510 + 0.514 + 0.513 + 0.511}{5} = \frac{2.560}{5} = 0.512\,\text{m}$$

The range is $0.514 - 0.510 = 0.004$ m, so the uncertainty is half of that,
$\pm 0.002$ m. The result is $0.512\,\text{m} \pm 0.002\,\text{m}$.

</details>
</details>
