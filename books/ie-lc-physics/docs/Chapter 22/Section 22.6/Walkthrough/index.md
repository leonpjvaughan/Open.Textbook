# 22.6 Reviewing the whole course — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Chapters 1 to 21. This section draws on every strand.

**Objectives:** map the four strands and their central ideas; check an answer with
units and significant figures; choose the right model for a situation; name the
physics your investigation uses; prepare the investigative log; look forward to the
SEC investigation and the written paper.

By the end of this section you can say where your investigation sits in the course,
and use the course to support it.

## The four strands

The course has four strands of content, and an investigation can come from any of
them. Each one has a central idea that most of its questions rest on.

| Strand | Central idea | An investigation it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Forces and motion | Forces change motion | How does the drop height affect the bounce height? |
| Waves and energy | Waves carry energy, and energy is conserved | How does the tension of a string affect its pitch? |
| Electric and magnetic fields | Charge and current produce fields, and fields produce forces | How does the length of a wire affect its resistance? |
| Modern physics | Matter and energy are equivalent at the nuclear scale | How does the distance affect the count rate from a source? |

Your investigation question from Section 22.1 belongs to one of these strands. Naming
the strand is the first step in describing the physics accurately, which is the
first quality the SEC judges.

## Checking an answer with units

A unit check is the quickest way to catch a wrong answer. Every term in an equation
must have the same unit, so a mismatch means the equation cannot be right.

The seven SI base quantities are length, mass, time, electric current,
temperature, amount of substance and luminous intensity. Every other unit is built
from these. A newton is a kilogram metre per second squared, and a joule is a
newton metre.

**Example 1.** A student writes $E = \frac{1}{2}mv$ for kinetic energy, where $m$ is
a mass and $v$ is a speed. Use units to show that this cannot be right.

The unit of $mv$ is $\text{kg} \times \text{m/s} = \text{kg m/s}$. The unit of
energy is the joule, which is $\text{kg m}^2/\text{s}^2$. The two do not match, so
the equation cannot be correct. The right expression is $E = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$,
whose unit is $\text{kg m}^2/\text{s}^2$.

The check cannot prove a value is right. A wrong value can still carry the correct
unit. It can prove a value is wrong, and that is worth a minute of your time.

## Significant figures

A measurement is only as precise as the instrument that took it. The answer is
written to the number of significant figures the data support, which is usually the
smallest number of figures in the readings.

Trailing zeros after a decimal point count as significant figures, because they say
something about the precision. The number 0.1250 m has four significant figures,
and 125.0 mm has four as well. A number such as 300 is ambiguous, so a writer who
means three figures writes $3.00 \times 10^2$.

## Choosing the right model

Every question needs a model, and the model brings assumptions. Choosing well means
asking what the situation contains.

- **Is the acceleration constant?** If so, the equations of motion apply. If not,
  the motion needs a graph or an energy method.
- **Is a force balanced?** If so, the resultant is zero and the object stays at
  rest or moves at a steady speed.
- **Is energy conserved?** If friction and drag can be neglected, an energy method
  is often quicker than a force method.
- **Is the resistance constant?** A filament lamp heats up, so its resistance
  changes, and Ohm's law applies only at a fixed temperature.

**Example 2.** A block slides down a rough slope and stops at the bottom. Which
model suits the question about the distance it travels?

The block slows down, so the acceleration is not constant unless the friction is
constant, and the speed changes. An energy model is the better choice: the
gravitational potential energy at the top becomes kinetic energy and heat. The
assumption is that the friction force is constant, so the heat is the friction force
times the distance.

## Looking forward to the SEC investigation

The investigation you have planned in this chapter is the one the SEC will mark. The
brief is published in term two of year one, the report is submitted in Year 2, and
the work is expected to take up to 20 hours.

Keep the investigative log as you go. It holds the readings, the decisions and the
problems, and it is what makes the reflection honest. A log written at the end is a
memory, and the SEC descriptors reward a record.

The same habits serve the written paper. Section A of the paper asks you to
describe experiments and their errors, and every strand's questions reward a reader
who checks units and significant figures.

> **Watch out.** Do not treat a formula as a fact that stands on its own. A formula
> carries assumptions, and the assumptions decide when it applies. The contrast is
> $V = IR$, which holds for a resistor at a fixed temperature and fails for a
> filament lamp as it heats. Naming the assumption is what makes a model useful
> rather than misleading.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. Your investigation is now planned, carried out and
written. This is the last piece of the chapter.

1. Name the strand your investigation belongs to, and two ideas from the course it
   uses.
2. Write one entry for your investigative log: the date, what you did, and one
   problem you met.
3. State one assumption your method makes, and say what would happen if it failed.

Keep the answers. The Review returns to your investigation as a whole and resolves
the chapter problem.

## You should now be able to

- name the four strands and a central idea from each;
- check an answer with units, and say what the check can and cannot prove;
- write an answer to the significant figures the data support;
- choose a model for a situation and state its assumptions;
- name the strand and the physics your investigation uses;
- keep an investigative log that supports an honest reflection.
