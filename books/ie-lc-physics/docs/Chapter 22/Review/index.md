# Chapter 22: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**Your investigation.** You choose an issue, research it, gather your own data on
it, and write an honest account of what happened. The chapter gave you the method,
so the Review shows the method on one complete investigation, and then hands it
back to you for your own.

The worked example is the solar-cell investigation from the sections.

**Step 1: choose the question.** The brief gives a context. Inside it, the question
is: *How does the angle of a solar cell to the lamp affect the voltage it
produces?* It is testable, because the angle can be changed. It is measurable,
because a voltmeter reads the voltage. It is manageable, because the equipment is a
lamp, a cell, a protractor and a voltmeter. The question belongs to the strand on
waves and energy, because it is about light falling on a cell.

**Step 2: research it.** Two sources bear on the question. A textbook says that a
cell produces a voltage from the light falling on it. A data sheet from the maker
gives the voltage at full sunlight, but it is the maker's own test, so it is used
as a comparison rather than as evidence. The research sharpens the hypothesis.

**Step 3: write the hypothesis.** *If the cell faces the lamp directly, then the
voltage is largest, because turning the cell away spreads the light over a larger
area.* The hypothesis predicts a direction, so the readings can test it.

**Step 4: design the experiment.** The independent variable is the angle of the
cell. The dependent variable is the voltage. The control variables are the
distance from the lamp, the lamp itself and the room light. The method sets the
angle with a protractor, reads the voltage, repeats three times, then changes the
angle.

**Step 5: gather the data.** The mean voltage at each angle is in the table. Each
mean comes from three readings.

| Angle / degrees | Mean voltage / V |
|---|---|
| 0 | 2.40 |
| 20 | 2.26 |
| 40 | 1.84 |
| 60 | 1.20 |
| 80 | 0.42 |

The repeats at 0° are 2.41 V, 2.40 V and 2.39 V. The range is
$2.41 - 2.39 = 0.02$ V, so the uncertainty in the mean is $\pm 0.01$ V.

**Step 6: process the data.** The voltage falls as the angle grows. A graph of
voltage against the cosine of the angle straightens the points into a line through
the origin. Its gradient is about 2.4 V, and its intercept is close to 0 V.

The intercept being close to zero supports the model: at an angle of 90° no light
falls on the cell, so the voltage should be zero. A non-zero intercept would have
shown a zero error in the voltmeter.

**Step 7: test the hypothesis.** The evidence supports the hypothesis. The voltage
is largest at 0°, where the cell faces the lamp, and it has fallen to half by 60°.
At 80° it is 0.42 V. The uncertainty in each mean is about 0.01 V, so the fall is
far larger than the uncertainty.

**Step 8: evaluate the method.** The largest source of error is setting the angle,
because the protractor is read to the nearest degree and the cell must be lined up
with it. The lamp distance was controlled, so it did not change the result. A
better experiment would use a holder that fixes the cell at each angle, and more
repeats at the angles where the voltage changes fastest.

**Step 9: write the report and relate it to society.** The report states the
question, the sources, the hypothesis, the method, the readings, the graph, the
conclusion and the evaluation. Relating it to society: a solar panel on a roof
produces most when it faces the Sun, so a fixed mount is a compromise between
cost and output. A tracking mount follows the Sun and produces more, but it costs
more and needs power to move. The payback time decides which choice is fair to the
household.

**Now your investigation.** Apply the same nine steps to the question you wrote in
Section 22.1. The steps are the method, and the numbers are yours.

1. Write the question, and check it against the three tests.
2. Find two sources, and say how reliable each one is.
3. Write the hypothesis as an if, then, because sentence.
4. List the independent, dependent and control variables.
5. Record the readings in a table, with the quantity and unit in each heading.
6. Find the mean and the uncertainty, and plot the graph.
7. State whether the evidence supports the hypothesis, with a number and a reason.
8. Say what limited the result, and what you would change.
9. Write the conclusion, and relate it to society.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which idea applies**
before you calculate: a conversion, an uncertainty, an equation of motion, a
circuit quantity, a wave, or an argument to be judged.

1. Convert 3.5 km to metres, and 0.50 m$^3$ to litres.
2. A block has a mass of 0.80 kg and a volume of 0.000 20 m$^3$. Calculate its
   density, and write the answer to the significant figures the data justify.
3. A ball falls 1.80 m from rest. Use $s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$ to find the fall time
   and the landing speed. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.
4. Four timings of the same fall are 0.62 s, 0.66 s, 0.60 s and 0.64 s. Find the
   mean and the uncertainty as half the range.
5. A spring of spring constant 250 N/m is stretched by 0.032 m. Calculate the force.
6. A 12 V supply drives a current of 0.50 A through a resistor. Calculate the
   resistance and the power.
7. A sound wave has a frequency of 250 Hz and travels at 340 m/s. Calculate its
   wavelength.
8. A transformer has 200 turns on the primary coil and 50 turns on the secondary.
   The primary voltage is 240 V. Calculate the secondary voltage.
9. A radioactive source has a half-life of 6.0 hours. What fraction of the source
   remains after 24 hours?
10. One fission of a uranium-235 nucleus releases about 200 MeV. Convert this to
    joules, taking $1\,\text{MeV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}$ J.
11. A source gives a value for the efficiency of a device and does not say how it
    was measured. Explain how you would judge the value before using it.
12. Write a hypothesis for this question: *How does the tension of a guitar string
    affect the pitch of the note?* Say what physics supports it.

## What you can now do

- read the SEC brief and turn an interest into a testable question;
- write a hypothesis that physics supports, and say how the readings will test it;
- judge a secondary source for reliability and bias, and record it;
- design a fair investigation, with the variables chosen and the errors controlled;
- gather primary data, with repeats, and record it in a clear table;
- process the data, with uncertainties, a graph and a gradient;
- test the hypothesis against the evidence, with a number and a reason;
- evaluate the method and say what you would change;
- write the report, relate the result to society, and give an honest reflection;
- check any answer with units and significant figures.

## Thinking back

At the start of the chapter you wrote down something you had wondered about in
physics, and that nobody had given you a number for.

You can now give it a number. Take that sentence and turn it into the question you
would investigate, the hypothesis you would test, and the measurement you would
take. If the measurement needs equipment you do not have, say what you would use
instead, and what that change would cost the result.

The investigation is the largest single piece of work in the course, and it is
marked as science rather than as an examination. An honest account of what happened
is worth more than a tidy one that hides the problems.
