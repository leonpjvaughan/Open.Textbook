# 22.4 Processing and evaluating your data — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A distance-time graph is a straight line through (0 s, 0 m) and (4.0 s, 12 m).
   Find its gradient.
2. State the SI unit of force, and say what the gradient of a force-extension
   graph represents.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $\dfrac{12 - 0}{4.0 - 0} = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$. The gradient of a distance-time graph
   is the speed.
2. The unit of force is the newton, N. The gradient of a force-extension graph is
   the spring constant, in N/m. A gradient always carries the unit of the vertical
   quantity divided by the unit of the horizontal quantity.

Both return here. A gradient is the main way a graph answers a question.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A student records the time for ten swings of a pendulum as 14.2 s, 14.3 s,
14.2 s, 14.4 s and 14.4 s. Calculate the mean and the uncertainty as half the
range.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the readings and divide by five. Then find the range.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The mean is $\dfrac{14.2 + 14.3 + 14.2 + 14.4 + 14.4}{5}$. The range is
$14.4 - 14.2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The mean is

$$\frac{14.2 + 14.3 + 14.2 + 14.4 + 14.4}{5} = \frac{71.5}{5} = 14.3\,\text{s}$$

The range is $14.4 - 14.2 = 0.2$ s, so the uncertainty is half of that,
$\pm 0.1$ s. The result is $14.3\,\text{s} \pm 0.1\,\text{s}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A graph of voltage against current passes through (0.10 A, 1.2 V),
(0.20 A, 2.4 V) and (0.30 A, 3.6 V). Calculate the gradient, with its unit, and
say what it represents.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Take two points far apart on the line, and divide the change in voltage by the
change in current.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{3.6 - 1.2}{0.30 - 0.10}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{gradient} = \frac{3.6 - 1.2}{0.30 - 0.10} = \frac{2.4}{0.20} = 12\,\text{V/A}$$

A volt per amp is an ohm, so the gradient is the resistance: $12\,\Omega$. The unit
of the gradient tells you what the number means, so always work it out.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A graph of extension against force for a spring is a straight line through
the points (0 N, 0.00 m), (2.0 N, 0.04 m) and (4.0 N, 0.08 m). Find the intercept
on the extension axis, and say what it means.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The intercept is the value of the extension when the force is zero.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Read the extension at a force of 0 N.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The line passes through the origin, so the intercept is $0\,\text{m}$. It means
that a force of zero gives an extension of zero: the spring is unstretched when
nothing pulls it.

If the intercept were not zero, it would show a zero error. A ruler read from the
wrong end, for example, would shift every extension by the same amount.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A graph of force against extension passes through (0 m, 0 N), (0.02 m, 4 N),
(0.04 m, 8 N) and (0.06 m, 12 N). Use the trapezium rule to find the area under
the graph, and say what the area represents.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Split the area into three trapezia, one between each pair of neighbouring points.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Each trapezium has width 0.02 m. Its area is $\dfrac{\text{width} \times (\text{first height} + \text{second height})}{2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{area} = \frac{0.02 \times (0 + 4)}{2} + \frac{0.02 \times (4 + 8)}{2} + \frac{0.02 \times (8 + 12)}{2}$$

$$\text{area} = 0.04 + 0.12 + 0.20 = 0.36\,\text{J}$$

The area under a force-extension graph is the energy stored in the spring. The
unit is the newton metre, which is the joule.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A force of 8.0 N is measured with an uncertainty of 3%, and it moves an
object 0.25 m with an uncertainty of 2%. Calculate the work done and its absolute
uncertainty.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the work first, then add the percentage uncertainties.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = Fd = 8.0 \times 0.25$, and the percentage uncertainties give $3\% + 2\% = 5\%$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = Fd = 8.0 \times 0.25 = 2.0\,\text{J}$$

The percentage uncertainties add, because the work is a product:

$$3\% + 2\% = 5\%$$

The absolute uncertainty is 5% of 2.0 J, which is 0.10 J. So
$W = 2.0\,\text{J} \pm 0.10\,\text{J}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** An investigation gives the gradient of a distance-time-squared graph as
9.5 m/s$^2$, and the accepted value of $g$ is 9.8 m/s$^2$. State whether the
evidence supports the model, and give a reason.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the two values, and compare the difference with the uncertainty.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The difference is about 3%. Is that bigger than the uncertainty in the timings?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The measured value is 9.5 m/s$^2$, which is about 3% below the accepted
9.8 m/s$^2$. The uncertainty in the timings is larger than 3%, so the two values
agree within the uncertainty.

The evidence supports the model. If the difference had been much larger than the
uncertainty, the evidence would not support it, and the report would look for a
systematic error or a broken assumption.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 1

**7.** A student's readings are all about 0.02 s too large, and they scatter by
about 0.01 s. Identify the type of each error, and say what to do about each.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One error moves every reading the same way. The other moves readings above and
below the true value.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A reading that is always too large is systematic. Scatter is random.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The reading that is always too large is a systematic error, because it pushes every
reading the same way. Repeating the measurement does not remove it, so the student
must check the instrument or the method.

The scatter of about 0.01 s is a random error, because it moves the readings above
and below the true value. Repeating and averaging reduces it.

</details>
</details>
</details>
