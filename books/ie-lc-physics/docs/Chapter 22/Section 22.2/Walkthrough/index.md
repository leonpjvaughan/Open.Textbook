# 22.2 Researching from secondary sources — Learn

**Prerequisites:** a question for your investigation from Section 22.1, and the
idea of a scientific model from Section 1.3.

**Objectives:** tell primary data from secondary data; find sources that bear on
your question; judge a source for reliability and bias; record a source so you can
cite it; use what you learn to sharpen your hypothesis.

By the end of this section you can find what is already known about your question,
and say how far each source can be trusted.

## Two kinds of data

**Primary data** are readings you take yourself. **Secondary data** are readings
that somebody else took and wrote down. Your investigation uses both, and the two
phases of the work match them.

| Kind of data | Who took the reading | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | You | The voltage you read from a voltmeter |
| Secondary | Somebody else | The efficiency quoted on a data sheet |

The research phase works on secondary data. It tells you what is already known, so
that your experiment adds something rather than repeating it. A source may also
give you a value to compare your own readings against.

## Where to look

A good search starts with the physics, not with a search engine. Name the quantity
you will measure, and the quantity you will change. Then look for those words.

- **A textbook or the specification.** Reliable for the physics, and written for
  your level.
- **A review or a report.** Useful for a summary of many measurements, if it says
  where they came from.
- **A data sheet.** Written by the maker of the device, so it is exact about that
  device but not independent.
- **Public data.** Met Éireann, the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland and the
  Central Statistics Office publish measurements anyone can check.
- **A news report.** A starting point for a topic, and rarely enough on its own,
  because the measurement is several steps away from the article.

## Judging a source

Ask four questions of every source. They take a minute and they change what you
write.

1. **Who wrote it, and why?** A maker selling a product has a reason to favour it.
2. **When was it published?** Old data may no longer describe the world.
3. **Does it say how the data were collected?** A figure with no method cannot be
   checked.
4. **Has anyone else checked it?** A claim that many independent people accept is
   stronger than a claim made once.

A source is **biased** when it selects or presents evidence to favour one
conclusion. Bias is not always a lie. A maker may quote the test that flattered its
product and leave out the rest.

**Example 1.** A manufacturer states that a lamp is 20% efficient. An independent
test finds 18%. Which value should go in the report?

The manufacturer's value comes from a test the manufacturer chose, and the company
sells the lamp. The independent value comes from a test by someone with no product
to sell. Prefer the independent value, and report the manufacturer's value beside
it.

The difference is worth a number:

$$\text{percentage difference} = \frac{20 - 18}{20} \times 100\% = 10\%$$

A difference of 10% is large enough to mention, because it is bigger than the
uncertainty of a good measurement.

## Signs of a weak source

- A percentage with no number of measurements behind it.
- A claim that the source itself describes as "studies show", with no study named.
- A date that is missing, or more than ten years old for a fast-changing subject.
- A graph with no axis labels or units.
- A page that sells the product it is describing.

**Example 2.** A website says a device "cuts your electricity bill by up to 40%".
Is this usable?

The words "up to" mean the 40% is the best of several results, not the typical one.
No method is given, and the site sells the device. The claim is a starting point
for a question, not evidence. A usable version would say how many homes were
tested, over what period, and what the typical saving was.

## Recording a source

Record a source the first time you read it, not at the end. For each one, write
down four things.

- Who wrote it, including the organisation if no person is named.
- The title.
- When it was published or last updated.
- Where you found it, as a web address or a page reference.

That list becomes your references. It also lets you go back to check a number you
half-remember, which is how a report avoids a wrong figure.

> **Watch out.** A headline is not a measurement. "Solar power now cheaper than
> coal" is a conclusion, and the measurement behind it may be a single country in a
> single year. The contrast is to follow the claim back to its source. If the
> source names the method and the sample, you can judge it. If the trail stops at
> the headline, you cannot, and the claim does not belong in your report as
> evidence.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. Your investigation needs the research phase before the
experiment.

1. Find two sources that bear on your question from Section 22.1.
2. For each one, write a sentence saying how reliable it is, and why.
3. Say what the sources tell you about the hypothesis you wrote.

Keep the three answers. Section 22.3 uses what the sources say to choose the
variables, and Section 22.5 turns the sources into your reference list.

## You should now be able to

- tell primary data from secondary data;
- find sources that bear on a question, starting from the physics;
- judge a source by its author, its date, its method and its independence;
- explain what bias is and give signs of it;
- record a source so that you can cite it later;
- use what the sources say to sharpen a hypothesis.
