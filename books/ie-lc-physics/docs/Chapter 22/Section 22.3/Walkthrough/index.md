# 22.3 Designing the investigation and gathering primary data — Learn

**Prerequisites:** a question from Section 22.1, the research from Section 22.2,
and planning an investigation from Section 1.4.

**Objectives:** turn a hypothesis into variables; design a fair test; choose
equipment whose resolution suits the measurement; write a method another person
could follow; record the readings in a clear table; explain why repeats are taken
and how to find the uncertainty in a mean.

By the end of this section you can plan an experiment that produces primary data
worth analysing.

## From a hypothesis to variables

A hypothesis names a prediction. To test it, you turn the prediction into three
sets of variables.

- The **independent variable** is the one you change on purpose.
- The **dependent variable** is the one you measure. It depends on the independent
  variable.
- A **control variable** is anything else that could affect the result, kept the
  same in every trial.

A **fair test** changes only the independent variable. If two things change at
once, the readings cannot tell you which one caused the effect.

**Example 1.** The hypothesis is: if the cell faces the lamp directly, then the
voltage is largest. Name the variables.

The independent variable is the angle of the cell, because that is what the
hypothesis is about. The dependent variable is the voltage, because that is what
changes in response. The control variables include the distance from the lamp, the
lamp itself and the room light.

## Choosing the equipment

Equipment is chosen for two things: the **range** and the **resolution**. The range
is the largest and smallest reading the instrument can take. The resolution is the
smallest change it can show.

A voltmeter that reads to 0.1 V cannot settle a change of 0.05 V. A metre stick
marked in centimetres cannot settle a change of 5 mm. Match the resolution to the
effect you expect, or the experiment will not see it.

The **reading uncertainty** of a single reading is half the smallest division.
For a metre stick marked in millimetres, the smallest division is 0.001 m, so the
reading uncertainty is $\pm 0.0005$ m.

**Example 2.** A voltage is measured as 2.50 V, and the voltmeter reads to 0.1 V.
Find the reading uncertainty and the percentage uncertainty.

The reading uncertainty is half of the smallest division: $\pm 0.05$ V. Then

$$\text{percentage uncertainty} = \frac{0.05}{2.50} \times 100\% = 2\%$$

So the voltage is $2.50\,\text{V} \pm 2\%$. The percentage is the useful form,
because it says how much the uncertainty matters beside the reading.

## Repeats and the uncertainty in a mean

A single reading tells you very little. Random errors scatter readings above and
below the true value, and repeating the measurement reduces their effect.

Take at least three readings at each setting, then find the mean. The uncertainty
in the mean is half the range, which is half the difference between the largest and
the smallest reading.

**Example 3.** A student times the same fall five times: 0.72 s, 0.70 s, 0.74 s,
0.71 s and 0.73 s. Find the mean and the uncertainty.

The mean is

$$\text{mean} = \frac{0.72 + 0.70 + 0.74 + 0.71 + 0.73}{5} = \frac{3.60}{5} = 0.72\,\text{s}$$

The range is $0.74 - 0.70 = 0.04$ s, so the uncertainty is half of that,
$\pm 0.02$ s. The result is $0.72\,\text{s} \pm 0.02\,\text{s}$.

An **anomalous** reading sits far from the others. Repeat that trial and look for a
mistake. Leave the reading out of the mean only when you can say what went wrong.

## Writing the method

The method is a numbered list that another person could follow to get the same
readings. It names the equipment, the range of the independent variable, and the
number of repeats.

1. Set the independent variable to its first value, and measure it with the
   instrument you named.
2. Read the dependent variable. Record the reading as it appears on the
   instrument.
3. Repeat step 2 twice more, without changing anything else.
4. Change the independent variable to its next value, and repeat from step 2.
5. Record every reading in a table, with the quantity and its unit in the column
   heading.

A results table is part of the experiment. Write the unit in the heading, such as
**Voltage / V**, and use the same number of decimal places for readings from the
same instrument.

> **Watch out.** Do not record the mean and throw the readings away. The mean hides
> the scatter, and the scatter is what shows how reliable the result is. The
> contrast is a table with every reading and a mean column. Then a reader can see
> an anomalous value for themselves, and you can explain it in the report.

## Safety and integrity

- Follow the safety rules for the equipment: hot lamps, mains electricity, sharp
  edges and heavy masses each have one.
- Take only the readings you actually took. Do not tidy a value to fit a pattern.
- If a reading looks wrong, repeat the trial rather than delete it. Say what you
  did in the report.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. Your investigation now needs a method that produces
primary data.

1. Write the independent, dependent and one control variable for your question.
2. Write the method as numbered steps, naming the equipment and the range.
3. State how many repeats you will take, and how you will find the uncertainty.

Keep the answers. Section 22.4 processes the readings, and the Review uses the
method to resolve the chapter problem.

## You should now be able to

- turn a hypothesis into independent, dependent and control variables;
- explain what makes a test fair;
- choose equipment for its range and its resolution;
- find the reading uncertainty and the percentage uncertainty of a measurement;
- find the mean of repeats and the uncertainty as half the range;
- write a method another person could follow;
- record readings in a table with the quantity and unit in the heading.
