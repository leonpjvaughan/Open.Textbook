# 22.3 Designing the investigation and gathering primary data — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Convert 0.40 m to millimetres.
2. A voltage is measured as 3.0 V with an uncertainty of $\pm 0.1$ V. Calculate the
   percentage uncertainty.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $0.40\,\text{m} = 0.40 \times 1000 = 400\,\text{mm}$. There are 1000 mm in a
   metre, so multiplying by 1000 converts metres to millimetres.
2. $\dfrac{0.1}{3.0} \times 100\% = 3.3\%$ to two significant figures. The
   percentage uncertainty says how much the uncertainty matters beside the reading.

Both return here. A method records its readings in millimetres or metres, and the
percentage uncertainty is what decides whether a change in the readings is real.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A student investigates how the mass on a spring affects its extension. State
the independent variable, the dependent variable and one control variable.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Ask what is changed, what is measured, and what else could affect the extension.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The mass is changed. The extension is measured. The spring itself could change the
result.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The independent variable is the mass on the spring, because the student changes it.
The dependent variable is the extension, because that is what is measured. A
control variable is the spring itself, which is kept the same in every trial.

Another control variable is the position the extension is measured from. If the
zero of the ruler moves, the readings cannot be compared.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A metre stick is marked in millimetres. State the reading uncertainty of a
single reading from it, in metres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The reading uncertainty is half the smallest division.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The smallest division is 1 mm, which is 0.001 m.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The smallest division is $1\,\text{mm} = 0.001\,\text{m}$. The reading uncertainty
is half of that:

$$\frac{0.001}{2} = 0.0005\,\text{m}$$

So a single reading is quoted as, for example, $0.234\,\text{m} \pm 0.0005\,\text{m}$.
The uncertainty is quoted with the same unit as the reading.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A student times the same fall four times and records 0.62 s, 0.66 s, 0.60 s
and 0.64 s. Calculate the mean and the uncertainty as half the range.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the readings and divide by four. Then find the range.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The mean is $\dfrac{0.62 + 0.66 + 0.60 + 0.64}{4}$. The range is
$0.66 - 0.60$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The mean is

$$\frac{0.62 + 0.66 + 0.60 + 0.64}{4} = \frac{2.52}{4} = 0.63\,\text{s}$$

The range is $0.66 - 0.60 = 0.06$ s, so the uncertainty is half of that,
$\pm 0.03$ s. The result is $0.63\,\text{s} \pm 0.03\,\text{s}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Explain why a student repeats each reading three times and takes the mean.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about random errors, and about what a set of agreeing readings shows.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Random errors scatter the readings. The mean of several readings is a better
estimate than one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Repeating reduces the effect of random errors, which scatter the readings above and
below the true value. The mean of several readings is a better estimate than any
single reading.

Repeated readings that agree with one another also show that the result is
reliable. If they scatter widely, the method itself is not precise enough, and the
report must say so.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Describe the steps you would take to gather primary data for an
investigation into how the angle of a solar cell affects the voltage it produces.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Name the equipment, the range of angles, the reading, and the repeats.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Set the angle, read the voltage, repeat, then change the angle and start again.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

1. Set the cell at the first angle, measured with a protractor, with the lamp at a
   fixed distance.
2. Read the voltage from the voltmeter, and record it as it appears.
3. Repeat step 2 twice more, without changing the angle or the lamp.
4. Turn the cell to the next angle, and repeat from step 2.
5. Record every reading in a table headed **Angle / degrees** and **Voltage / V**.

The lamp distance is a control variable, so it is kept the same in every trial. The
repeats let the uncertainty in the mean voltage be found.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 5

**6.** A spring has a spring constant of 50 N/m. Calculate the extension when a
force of 3.0 N is applied.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use Hooke's law, $F = kx$, and rearrange it for the extension.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$x = \dfrac{F}{k} = \dfrac{3.0}{50}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$x = \frac{F}{k} = \frac{3.0}{50} = 0.060\,\text{m}$$

The extension is 0.060 m, which is 60 mm. This is a calculation you could use in an
investigation into springs, where the gradient of a force-extension graph gives the
spring constant.

</details>
</details>
</details>
