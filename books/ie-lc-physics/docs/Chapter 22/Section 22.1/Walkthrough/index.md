# 22.1 Reading the brief and choosing a question — Learn

**Prerequisites:** scientific models from Section 1.3, and planning an
investigation from Section 1.4.

**Objectives:** state what the SEC brief provides and what it leaves to you;
describe the two phases of the investigation; turn an interest into a testable
question; write a hypothesis that physics supports; check that a question is
testable, measurable and manageable.

By the end of this section you can take something you have wondered about and turn
it into a question you can investigate.

## What the brief gives you

The **Physics In Practice Investigation** is worth 40% of your Leaving Certificate
mark. The State Examinations Commission (SEC) publishes a **common brief** each
year, in term two of year one. The brief sets the context and gives you stimulus
material. It does not give you the question.

That is the point of the investigation. You choose the issue inside the brief, you
design the experiment, and you take the readings. The report is submitted in Year
2, in the format the SEC prescribes, and the work is expected to take up to 20
hours.

The investigation has two phases.

1. **Research.** You gather, process and evaluate information from secondary
   sources. This is what other people have already written or measured.
2. **Experiment.** You form a hypothesis, plan and design an experiment, carry it
   out, and gather primary data. These are the readings you take yourself.

The knowledge from the research phase informs the experiment. If a source says the
efficiency of a cell depends on the angle of the light, that claim is a reason to
design an experiment about angle.

## What the examiner looks for

The SEC judges the investigation against four qualities. They are worth knowing
before you choose a question, because a question that cannot show one of them is a
question that will cost you marks.

| Quality | What it means |
|---|---|
| Knowledge and understanding | You describe the purpose and the physics accurately |
| Investigating | You design a fair test and gather good-quality primary data |
| Communicating | You present and analyse the data so a reader can check it |
| Relating to society | You connect the result to the real world |

A question that produces a single yes or no answer shows none of these. A question
that produces a set of readings against a changed quantity can show all four.

## From an interest to a question

An interest is a topic. A question is a measurement. The step between them is the
hardest part of the investigation, and three tests make it easier.

- **Testable.** You can change one thing and measure what happens.
- **Measurable.** The quantity you measure comes from an instrument.
- **Manageable.** The readings fit the time, equipment and safety rules you have.

**Example 1.** A student is interested in solar panels. Turn that interest into a
question that passes the three tests.

Start with the topic: solar panels. Ask what the panel does, and what could change
it. A panel produces a voltage when light falls on it. The light reaching it
depends on the angle between the panel and the lamp.

That gives a question: *How does the angle of a solar cell to the lamp affect the
voltage it produces?*

Check the three tests. The angle can be changed, so the question is testable. The
voltage is read from a voltmeter, so it is measurable. A protractor, a lamp and a
voltmeter are all available, so it is manageable.

## Writing a hypothesis

A **hypothesis** is a testable prediction. It is not a guess, because physics
supports it. Write it as an *if, then, because* sentence, so the prediction and its
reason are both visible.

**Example 2.** Write a hypothesis for the solar-cell question.

The physics: light falling on a cell produces a voltage. When the cell turns away
from the lamp, the same light spreads over a larger area, so less light falls on
each part of the cell.

That gives the hypothesis: *If the cell faces the lamp directly, then the voltage
is largest, because turning the cell away spreads the light over a larger area.*

The hypothesis predicts a direction. The readings can then show whether the voltage
falls as the angle grows, which is what makes the hypothesis testable.

## Planning the time

Twenty hours is a budget, and a report written in the last two of them shows it.
Plan the phases before you start.

**Example 3.** A student plans to spend 25% of 20 hours on research and 45% on the
experiment. Find the time left for the report.

Research: $25\%$ of 20 hours is 5 hours. Experiment: $45\%$ of 20 hours is 9 hours.
Time left: $20 - 5 - 9 = 6$ hours for the analysis and the report.

Six hours is enough for a report that was planned. It is not enough for one that
was left until the end.

> **Watch out.** A question with no variable is not an investigation. "Is physics
> interesting?" asks for an opinion, and no measurement can settle it. "Does a
> pendulum swing?" has an answer that is already known, so it is a demonstration
> rather than an investigation. The contrast is a question of the form *how does
> the length of a pendulum affect the time for one swing?* Here one quantity is
> changed, a second is measured, and the answer is not known until the readings
> are taken.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. Your investigation begins with a question you choose.

1. Write your first version of the question, in one sentence.
2. Check it against the three tests: is it testable, measurable and manageable?
3. Write a hypothesis for it as an if, then, because sentence.

Keep all three answers. Section 22.2 helps you research the question, Section 22.3
turns the hypothesis into an experiment, and the Review uses the question to
resolve the chapter problem.

## You should now be able to

- state what the SEC brief provides and what it leaves to you;
- describe the two phases of the investigation, research and experiment;
- name the four qualities the investigation is judged against;
- turn an interest into a testable question;
- check a question against the three tests;
- write a hypothesis as an if, then, because sentence, supported by physics.
