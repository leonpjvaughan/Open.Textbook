# 22.1 Reading the brief and choosing a question — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is a scientific model?
2. A student investigates how the drop height of a ball affects its fall time.
   State the independent variable and one control variable.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. A scientific model is a simplified representation of a real system. It keeps
   the features that matter for the question and leaves out the rest. The
   falling-ball model keeps gravity and leaves out the air.
2. The independent variable is the drop height, because the student changes it. A
   control variable is the ball, which is kept the same in every trial. A control
   variable is anything that could affect the result and is held fixed.

Both ideas return here. A model gives you a reason for a hypothesis, and the
variables are what make the question testable.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A student is interested in how a bicycle slows down on a hill. Turn that
interest into a question that passes the three tests.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Ask what the bicycle does, and what could change it. Then write the question as
*how does one quantity affect another?*

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The bicycle rolls down a slope and speeds up or slows down. The steepness of the
slope is one quantity that could change the motion.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A question that passes the three tests is: *How does the steepness of a slope
affect the time a bicycle takes to roll a fixed distance?*

The steepness can be changed, so the question is testable. The time is read from a
stopwatch, so it is measurable. A ramp, a bicycle and a stopwatch are all
available, so it is manageable. A question that names the changed quantity and the
measured quantity is usually ready to test.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A student asks, "Does a solar cell work better when it is clean?" Explain
why this question is weak, and rewrite it so that it can be investigated.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look for the quantity that is changed and the quantity that is measured.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

"Clean" is not a quantity, and "better" is not a measurement. Replace each with
something an instrument can read.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The question is weak because "clean" is not a quantity you can change by a measured
amount, and "better" is not a quantity you can read from an instrument. The answer
would be an opinion, not a measurement.

A stronger question is: *How does the voltage from a solar cell change as a layer
of dust is added to its surface?* The dust can be added in measured amounts, and
the voltage is read from a voltmeter. The same physics is being tested, but the
readings can now settle the question.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Write a hypothesis for this question: *How does the mass on a spring affect
the extension of the spring?* Take $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the if, then, because form. The physics is Hooke's law, and the force on the
spring is the weight of the mass.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The weight of the mass is $W = mg$. The spring stretches until its force balances
the weight.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The hypothesis is: *If the mass on the spring increases, then the extension
increases in proportion, because the weight $W = mg$ increases in proportion and
Hooke's law says the extension is proportional to the force.*

The hypothesis is testable, because a graph of extension against mass should be a
straight line through the origin. Its gradient would be $g/k$, where $k$ is the
spring constant. The predicted shape is what the readings will test.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** The investigation is expected to take up to 20 hours. A student plans to
spend 20% of the time on research and 50% on the experiment. Calculate the number
of hours left for the report.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find each share of 20 hours first, then subtract both from 20.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Research: $20\%$ of 20 hours. Experiment: $50\%$ of 20 hours.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Research: $0.20 \times 20 = 4$ hours. Experiment: $0.50 \times 20 = 10$ hours.
Time left: $20 - 4 - 10 = 6$ hours.

So 6 hours remain for the analysis and the report. Planning the phases first is
what makes the remaining time enough.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** State what makes a question suitable for a scientific investigation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the three tests: testable, measurable and manageable.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say what each test means, and why a question that fails one of them cannot be
investigated.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A suitable question is testable, so one quantity can be changed and another
measured. It is measurable, so the quantity measured comes from an instrument. It
is manageable, so the readings fit the time, the equipment and the safety rules
available.

A question that asks for an opinion fails the first test. A question whose answer
cannot be read from an instrument fails the second.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 1

**6.** State the assumption that the simplest falling-ball model makes about the
air, and explain what the model predicts for the distance fallen.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The model keeps one force. Name it, and name the one it leaves out.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The model ignores air resistance. The only force is gravity, so the acceleration is
constant at $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The simplest model ignores air resistance, so gravity is the only force. The
acceleration is then constant at $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$ downwards, and the distance
fallen from rest is

$$s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$$

The assumption is what makes the model simple enough to solve. It is also the
reason the model fails for a light object with a large surface area, such as a
feather. Naming the assumption is how you judge whether the model suits your
investigation.

</details>
</details>
</details>
