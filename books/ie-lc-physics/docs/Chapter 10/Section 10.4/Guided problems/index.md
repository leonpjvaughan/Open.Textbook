# 10.4 Diffraction and interference — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Two pulses meet on a rope. One has a displacement of $+3.0\,\text{cm}$ and the
   other has a displacement of $-2.0\,\text{cm}$. State the resultant displacement.
2. A light wave has a frequency of $5.0 \times 10^{14}$ Hz and a wavelength of
   $6.0 \times 10^{-7}$ m. Calculate its speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The resultant displacement is $+3.0 - 2.0 = +1.0\,\text{cm}$, because the
   displacements add.
2. $v = f\lambda = 5.0 \times 10^{14} \times 6.0 \times 10^{-7} =
   3.0 \times 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$.

The first answer is superposition, and the second is the wave equation. Interference
is superposition applied to two continuous waves, and every fringe is found from the
wavelength.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** In a two-slit experiment the path difference to a point on the screen is
$2.6\,\mu\text{m}$ and the wavelength is 650 nm. Calculate the path difference in
wavelengths, and state whether the point is bright or dark.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the path difference by the wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{2.6 \times 10^{-6}}{6.5 \times 10^{-7}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{\text{path difference}}{\lambda} = \frac{2.6 \times 10^{-6}}
{6.5 \times 10^{-7}} = 4$$

The path difference is 4 wavelengths. A whole number of wavelengths means the waves
arrive in step, so the point is a bright fringe.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** In a two-slit experiment the path difference to a point on the screen is
$1.95\,\mu\text{m}$ and the wavelength is 650 nm. Calculate the path difference in
wavelengths, and state whether the point is bright or dark.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the path difference by the wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{1.95 \times 10^{-6}}{6.5 \times 10^{-7}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{\text{path difference}}{\lambda} = \frac{1.95 \times 10^{-6}}
{6.5 \times 10^{-7}} = 3$$

The path difference is 3 wavelengths, which is a whole number, so the point is a
bright fringe. This is the third bright fringe from the centre.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** In a two-slit experiment the path difference to a point on the screen is
$0.975\,\mu\text{m}$ and the wavelength is 650 nm. Calculate the path difference in
wavelengths, and state whether the point is bright or dark.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the path difference by the wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{9.75 \times 10^{-7}}{6.5 \times 10^{-7}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{\text{path difference}}{\lambda} = \frac{9.75 \times 10^{-7}}
{6.5 \times 10^{-7}} = 1.5$$

The path difference is 1.5 wavelengths, which is a half number, so the point is a
dark fringe. The two waves arrive out of step and cancel.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Explain why you can hear a conversation through an open doorway, even when
you are not in line with the gap, but you cannot see around the corner.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the wavelength of sound with the wavelength of light.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A doorway is about a metre wide. The wavelength of sound is about a metre, and the
wavelength of light is less than a millionth of a metre.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Sound spreads out by diffraction when it passes through the doorway, because the
width of the doorway is about the same as the wavelength of the sound. The sound
bends around the edges and reaches you. The wavelength of light is far smaller than
the doorway, so light spreads only a very little at the edges and travels almost
straight on. That is why you can hear around the corner but not see around it.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain what is meant by two coherent sources, and why coherent sources are
needed for a steady interference pattern.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the frequency and the phase difference of the two sources.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

What would happen to the bright and dark positions if the phase difference kept
changing?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Two sources are coherent when they have the same frequency and a constant phase
difference. If the phase difference changed at random, the positions of the bright
and dark fringes would move from moment to moment and no steady pattern would be
seen. With coherent sources the waves arrive in step at some points and out of step
at others, so the bright and dark fringes stay in the same place.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** White light is used in a two-slit experiment. Describe what is seen on the
screen, and explain why the fringes are coloured.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

White light contains a range of wavelengths.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The position of a bright fringe depends on the wavelength. Which colour has the
larger angle?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A white central fringe is seen, with coloured fringes on each side. The position of a
bright fringe depends on the wavelength, so each colour has its maximum at a
different angle. Away from the centre the colours separate and overlap, giving
coloured fringes. The centre fringe stays white, because every wavelength has a
maximum there.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** Explain why a radio signal can be received behind a hill, but the television
signal from the same transmitter is much weaker there.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the two wavelengths with the size of the hill.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Radio waves have wavelengths of metres and television waves have wavelengths of
centimetres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Both signals diffract when they pass the hill. The radio wave has a much longer
wavelength than the television wave. Diffraction is greatest when the wavelength is
comparable to the size of the obstacle, so the radio wave bends around the hill far
more than the television wave. The radio signal therefore reaches the far side, and
the television signal does not.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 9.4

**8.** Two waves meet at a point. At that point one wave has a displacement of
$+4.0\,\text{mm}$ and the other has a displacement of $+4.0\,\text{mm}$. State the
resultant displacement, and name the type of interference.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the two displacements.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The two crests are in step with each other.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$4.0 + 4.0 = +8.0\,\text{mm}$$

The resultant displacement is $+8.0$ mm. Two crests have met, so the displacements
add and the interference is constructive. In a two-slit experiment this is the
condition at a bright fringe.

</details>
</details>
</details>
