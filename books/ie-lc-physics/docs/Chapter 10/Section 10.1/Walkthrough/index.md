# 10.1 Reflection of waves — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the parts of a wave from Section 9.1; angles and triangles from
earlier work.

**Objectives:** state the law of reflection; measure angles from the normal; find an
angle of incidence or reflection; explain the difference between a smooth reflection
and a rough one; describe one use of the reflection of sound.

By the end of this section, you can find where a reflected ray goes and say why a
mirror gives an image while a wall does not.

## A wave can bounce

Hold a mirror in sunlight and a bright patch lands on the wall. Sound a clap and an
echo comes back. A radar pulse leaves a ship, meets a plane, and returns. In each
case a wave met a surface and turned back. This is **reflection**.

To describe reflection, draw a line at right angles to the surface at the point
where the ray arrives. That line is the **normal**. Every angle in this section is
measured from the normal, not from the surface.

- The **angle of incidence** $i$ is the angle between the incoming ray and the
  normal.
- The **angle of reflection** $r$ is the angle between the outgoing ray and the
  normal.

> **The law of reflection.** The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection:
> $$i = r$$
> The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the same plane.

The law holds for every wave and for every surface, whether the surface is a mirror,
a wall or the surface of water.

## Finding the angles

The angles are always measured from the normal, so the first step is often a
subtraction.

**Example 1.** A ray of light makes an angle of 55° with the surface of a plane
mirror. Calculate the angle of incidence.

The normal is at 90° to the surface, so

$$i = 90^\circ - 55^\circ = 35^\circ$$

**Example 2.** A ray of light makes an angle of 25° with the surface of a plane
mirror. Calculate the angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray.

The angle of incidence is

$$i = 90^\circ - 25^\circ = 65^\circ$$

The angle of reflection is also 65°, and the two rays lie on opposite sides of the
normal. The angle between them is therefore

$$65^\circ + 65^\circ = 130^\circ$$

**Example 3.** A ray is reflected so that the incident ray and the reflected ray are
at right angles. Calculate the angle of incidence.

The two angles add to 90° and they are equal, so

$$i = \frac{90^\circ}{2} = 45^\circ$$

## Smooth surfaces and rough surfaces

A mirror gives a clear image because its surface is smooth. Rays that arrive
parallel reflect in the same direction, so the pattern of the image is preserved.
This is **specular reflection**.

A white wall is rough on the scale of the wavelength of light. The normal points in
a different direction at every point, so parallel rays leave in many directions.
This is **diffuse reflection**. The wall looks bright, but it gives no image.

Both are reflection, and both obey the law of reflection at each point. The
difference is the shape of the surface. A surface reflects light like a mirror only
when its roughness is small compared with the wavelength.

The same idea explains why you can see your face in a still lake but not in a
choppy one. The wind roughens the surface, and the reflection becomes diffuse.

## Reflection of sound and other waves

Sound reflects from hard, flat surfaces, and the reflection is heard as an **echo**.
The sound travels to the surface and back, so the distance is half the total
distance travelled.

**Example 4.** A student claps and hears the echo from a wall 0.80 s later. The speed
of sound in air is 340 m/s. Calculate the distance to the wall.

$$s = \frac{vt}{2} = \frac{340 \times 0.80}{2} = 136\,\text{m}$$

where $s$ is the distance to the wall, $v$ the speed of sound and $t$ the time for the
echo to return.

Ultrasound works the same way. A pulse is sent into water, and the echo is timed to
find the depth. Sonar, radar and medical ultrasound imaging all measure a distance
from the time a reflected pulse takes to return.

> **Watch out.** The angle is measured from the **normal**, not from the surface. A
> ray that makes 20° with the surface has an angle of incidence of 70°, not 20°. If
> you use the angle to the surface, every reflected ray is drawn in the wrong
> direction.
> **Re-check:** mark the normal first, then measure both angles from it.

## You should now be able to

- state the law of reflection and say what the normal is;
- find the angle of incidence from the angle a ray makes with a surface;
- find the angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray;
- explain the difference between specular reflection and diffuse reflection, and
  give an example of each;
- calculate the distance to a surface from the time an echo takes to return.
