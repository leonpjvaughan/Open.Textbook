# 10.5 The diffraction grating — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. In a two-slit experiment the path difference to a point on the screen is
   2 wavelengths. State whether the point is bright or dark.
2. A light wave has a frequency of $4.6 \times 10^{14}$ Hz and a wavelength of
   $6.5 \times 10^{-7}$ m. Calculate its speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The point is bright, because a whole number of wavelengths means the waves arrive
   in step.
2. $v = f\lambda = 4.6 \times 10^{14} \times 6.5 \times 10^{-7} =
   3.0 \times 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$.

The path difference decides where the maxima are, and the wave equation links the
wavelength to the speed. Both return in the grating equation.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A diffraction grating has 400 lines per millimetre. Calculate the distance
between neighbouring slits.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide 1 mm by the number of lines, and convert the millimetre to metres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$d = \dfrac{0.001}{400}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$d = \frac{0.001\,\text{m}}{400} = 2.5 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$$

Each slit is 2.5 μm from the next one. The slit spacing is often called the grating
constant.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A diffraction grating has 250 lines per millimetre. Calculate the distance
between neighbouring slits.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide 1 mm by the number of lines, and convert the millimetre to metres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$d = \dfrac{0.001}{250}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$d = \frac{0.001\,\text{m}}{250} = 4.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$$

Fewer lines per millimetre means a larger slit spacing. A larger spacing gives
smaller angles for the same wavelength.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Light of wavelength 589 nm falls on a grating with 300 lines per millimetre.
Calculate the angle of the first-order maximum.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the slit spacing first, then use $n\lambda = d\sin\theta$ with $n = 1$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$d = \dfrac{0.001}{300} = 3.33 \times 10^{-6}$ m, and
$\sin\theta = \dfrac{5.89 \times 10^{-7}}{3.33 \times 10^{-6}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$d = \frac{0.001}{300} = 3.33 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$$

$$\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{d} = \frac{5.89 \times 10^{-7}}
{3.33 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.1767$$

$$\theta = 10.2^\circ$$

This wavelength is the yellow light from a sodium lamp, which is used as a standard
in spectrometry.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** For the same grating and the same light as question 3, calculate the angle of
the second-order maximum.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $n = 2$ in the grating equation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\sin\theta = \dfrac{2 \times 5.89 \times 10^{-7}}{3.33 \times 10^{-6}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\sin\theta = \frac{2\lambda}{d} = \frac{2 \times 5.89 \times 10^{-7}}
{3.33 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.3534$$

$$\theta = 20.7^\circ$$

The second-order maximum is at about twice the angle of the first-order maximum,
because the angles are small enough for $\sin\theta$ to be close to $\theta$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Light of wavelength 600 nm falls on a grating with 500 lines per millimetre.
Calculate the largest order of maximum that can be seen.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the slit spacing, then divide it by the wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$d = \dfrac{0.001}{500} = 2.0 \times 10^{-6}$ m, and
$\dfrac{d}{\lambda} = \dfrac{2.0 \times 10^{-6}}{6.00 \times 10^{-7}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{d}{\lambda} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-6}}{6.00 \times 10^{-7}} = 3.33$$

The order must be a whole number, and $\sin\theta$ cannot exceed 1. The largest
whole number below 3.33 is 3, so three orders are visible on each side of the centre.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Light of unknown wavelength falls on a grating with 500 lines per millimetre.
The first-order maximum is at 15.0°. Calculate the wavelength of the light.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange the grating equation for $\lambda$, with $n = 1$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$d = 2.0 \times 10^{-6}$ m, and $\lambda = d\sin\theta$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = d\sin\theta = 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \times \sin 15.0^\circ
= 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.2588 = 5.18 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$$

That is 518 nm, which is green light.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** Explain why a grating produces sharper maxima than two slits.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Count the slits, and think about what happens to the waves when they are in step and
when they are slightly out of step.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

With many slits, moving slightly away from a maximum takes the waves out of step.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A grating has many hundreds of slits, so light from all of them interferes. At the
angle where the waves from every slit arrive in step, they all add to give a very
bright maximum. Move a little to one side and the waves are no longer in step, so
they cancel and the region between maxima is dark. The maximum is therefore narrow
and sharp. Two slits give only one path difference, so the fringes are broad.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 10.4

**8.** Explain why the centre of a diffraction pattern is bright when light passes
through two slits.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the path difference at the centre of the screen.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Both slits are the same distance from the centre of the screen.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

At the centre of the screen the two slits are the same distance away, so the path
difference is zero. Zero is a whole number of wavelengths, so the waves arrive in
step and interfere constructively. The centre of the pattern is therefore a bright
fringe for every wavelength, which is why it is white when white light is used.

</details>
</details>
</details>
