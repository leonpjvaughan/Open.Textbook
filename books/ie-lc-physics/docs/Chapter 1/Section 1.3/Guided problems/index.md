# 1.3 Scientific models and the Nature of Science — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the SI unit of time?
2. A time is measured as 0.50 s $\pm$ 0.02 s. Calculate the percentage uncertainty.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The second, s.
2. $\dfrac{0.02}{0.50} \times 100\% = 4\%$.

Both return in this section. A model predicts a time, and the uncertainty decides
whether a difference between prediction and measurement means anything.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A ball is dropped from rest and falls for 0.30 s. Use $s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$ to
find the distance fallen. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Square the time before you multiply.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$s = 0.5 \times 9.8 \times 0.30^2 = 0.5 \times 9.8 \times 0.090$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2 = 0.5 \times 9.8 \times 0.090 = 0.44\,\text{m}$$

To two significant figures, $s = 0.44\,\text{m}$. The unit is metres, because
$\text{m/s}^2 \times \text{s}^2 = \text{m}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Use the same model to find the speed of the ball after 0.30 s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The speed is $v = gt$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = 9.8 \times 0.30$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = gt = 9.8 \times 0.30 = 2.9\,\text{m/s}$$

To two significant figures, $v = 2.9\,\text{m/s}$. This is the speed at that moment,
not the average speed over the fall.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A ball is dropped 0.80 m. Use $t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2s}{g}}$ to find the fall time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out the fraction under the square root first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 0.80}{9.8}} = \sqrt{0.163}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$t = \sqrt{\frac{2s}{g}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.60}{9.8}} = \sqrt{0.163} = 0.40\,\text{s}$$

So a ball dropped 0.80 m takes about 0.40 s in the model.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** The model predicts a fall time of 0.40 s. A student measures 0.44 s. Calculate
the percentage difference.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the difference by the predicted value.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{0.44 - 0.40}{0.40} \times 100\%$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{0.44 - 0.40}{0.40} \times 100\% = \frac{0.04}{0.40} \times 100\% = 10\%$$

The measurement is 10% longer than the prediction.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** State two assumptions of the falling-ball model, and say what happens to the
model's accuracy if a table-tennis ball is used instead of a steel ball.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What does the model leave out?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The model assumes no air resistance and a constant $g$. A table-tennis ball is light.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Two assumptions are:

- air resistance is ignored;
- $g$ is constant over the whole fall.

A table-tennis ball has a small mass and a large surface area, so air resistance is
a large fraction of its weight. The real ball falls more slowly than the model
predicts, so the model becomes less accurate. A steel ball of the same size is much
heavier, so air resistance matters less and the model works better.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A model of the atom was replaced when new evidence arrived. Explain, in one or
two sentences, why a scientific model can be replaced even though it made useful
predictions.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about what a model is, and what new evidence can do to it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A model is a simplified picture. New evidence can show that a simplification fails
somewhere.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A model is a simplified representation of a real system, so it holds only within the
range where its assumptions are good. When new evidence shows that the model cannot
account for a system, scientists revise it or replace it. The old model was not
useless; it was the best account the earlier evidence supported.

This is the Nature of Science: knowledge is provisional and changes as evidence
arrives.

</details>
</details>

## The chapter problem, measured

**7.** The model predicts a fall time of 0.51 s for a drop of 1.25 m. A student
measures 0.55 s. The stopwatch adds a reaction time of about 0.20 s. Say whether the
reaction time could explain the whole difference, and what else might contribute.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the difference (0.04 s) with the reaction time (0.20 s).

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The difference is smaller than the reaction time, and the reaction time can add at
the start, at the stop, or at both.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The difference is $0.55 - 0.51 = 0.04\,\text{s}$, which is much smaller than the
$0.20\,\text{s}$ reaction time. The reaction time is large enough to explain the whole
difference on its own, and it could even be larger than the difference if the student
anticipates the stop.

Air resistance also makes the real fall slightly slower than the model predicts, so
it pushes the measured time up as well. The two effects push the same way, and the
timing uncertainty is the larger of the two.

The honest conclusion is that this stopwatch cannot separate the two effects. A
better experiment uses a light gate, which removes the reaction time and leaves the
model's own error visible.

</details>
</details>
</details>
