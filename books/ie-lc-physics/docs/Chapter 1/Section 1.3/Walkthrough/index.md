# 1.3 Scientific models and the Nature of Science — Learn

**Prerequisites:** measurement, units and uncertainty from Sections 1.1 and 1.2.

**Objectives:** explain what a scientific model is; state the assumptions of the
falling-ball model; use $s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$ to predict a fall time and a landing
speed; compare a prediction with a measurement; describe how models are tested and how
they change.

By the end of this section, you can use a model to make a prediction and say what the
prediction assumes.

## What a model is

A **scientific model** is a simplified representation of a real system. It can be
written in words, in a diagram, in numbers or in an equation. No model is the real
thing. A model keeps the features that matter for the question and leaves out the
rest.

The model of a falling ball keeps one force, gravity, and leaves out the air. That
makes the mathematics simple enough to solve, and it makes the prediction sharp enough
to test.

## The falling-ball model

Near the surface of the Earth, a falling object speeds up at about
$g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. The simplest model says the ball is dropped from rest, so its
initial speed is zero. Then the distance fallen in a time $t$ is

$$s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$$

and its speed at that moment is

$$v = gt$$

**Example 1.** A ball is dropped 1.25 m. Calculate the time it takes to fall, in the
model.

Rearrange $s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$ for $t$:

$$t = \sqrt{\frac{2s}{g}}$$

$$t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.25}{9.8}} = \sqrt{0.255} = 0.51\,\text{s}$$

**Example 2.** Calculate the speed at which the ball lands.

$$v = \sqrt{2gs} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 1.25} = \sqrt{24.5} = 4.9\,\text{m/s}$$

So the model answers the chapter problem: the ball lands at about $5.0\,\text{m/s}$.

## The assumptions the model makes

Every model rests on **assumptions**. The falling-ball model assumes:

- the ball is dropped from rest, not thrown;
- gravity is the only force, so air resistance is ignored;
- $g$ is constant over the whole fall;
- the ball is a point, so its shape and spin do not matter.

Assumptions are not a weakness. They are what makes the prediction possible. They
also tell you where the model will stop working. For a light ball such as a
table-tennis ball, air resistance is large, and the real fall is slower than the
model predicts.

## Testing the model

A model earns its place by predicting something you can measure. Then you compare the
two.

$$\text{percentage difference} = \frac{\text{measured} - \text{predicted}}{\text{predicted}} \times 100\%$$

**Example 3.** The model predicts a fall time of 0.45 s. A student measures 0.50 s.
Find the percentage difference.

$$\text{percentage difference} = \frac{0.50 - 0.45}{0.45} \times 100\% = 11.1\%$$

The measurement is about 11% longer than the prediction. Now the interesting work
begins: which part of that 11% is the model's fault, and which part is the
measurement's?

- The model ignores air resistance, which makes the real fall **slower**. That part
  is the model's fault.
- A stopwatch adds a reaction time, which makes the measured time **longer**. That
  part is the measurement's fault.

Both effects push the same way here, so the measurement is longer than the model
predicts. Untangling them is what Sections 1.4 and 1.5 are for.

> **Watch out.** A model is not "wrong" because one measurement disagrees with it.
> The disagreement is evidence, and it might point at the model or at the
> measurement. Check the measurement and its uncertainty before you blame the model.

## Models change as evidence arrives

The Nature of Science is the way scientific knowledge is built, tested and shared.

- Scientists make **models** and use them to make predictions.
- A prediction is tested against **evidence** from observation or experiment.
- If the evidence disagrees, the model is **revised** or replaced.
- Results are shared, checked by other scientists (**peer review**), and repeated.
  A result that cannot be reproduced is not yet accepted.
- Scientific knowledge is **provisional**. It is the best account the evidence
  supports so far, and it can change when better evidence arrives.

The model of the atom is the clearest example in this course. Thomson's model,
Rutherford's model and Bohr's model each explained the evidence of their time, and
each was replaced when new evidence arrived. You meet that story in Chapter 19.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The model says a ball dropped 1.25 m takes 0.51 s. Your
measurement gives 0.55 s.

Write down the percentage difference. Then write two sentences: one saying how much
of the difference you think is the model's fault, and one saying how much is your
measurement's. Use the reaction-time uncertainty from Section 1.2 in your answer.
