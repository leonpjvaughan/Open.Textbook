# Chapter 1: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**How fast does a dropped ball actually fall?** You drop a ball and time the fall, then
compare the measurement with the simplest model of free fall.

**The model.** The simplest model ignores air resistance and takes gravity as the only
force. For a ball dropped from rest:

$$s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \qquad v = gt$$

For a drop of 1.25 m, with $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$:

$$t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.25}{9.8}} = 0.51\,\text{s} \qquad v = \sqrt{2gs} = 4.9\,\text{m/s}$$

So the model says the ball lands at about $5.0\,\text{m/s}$ after about $0.51\,\text{s}$.

**The measurement.** A student times the same fall at 0.55 s. The difference is
$0.55 - 0.51 = 0.04\,\text{s}$, which is about 8%.

**Which part is which.** The model's own error comes from ignoring air resistance,
which makes the real fall slightly slower. That is small here, perhaps a few per cent.
The measurement's error comes mostly from the reaction time: a hand-timed stopwatch
adds up to about 0.20 s, which is much larger than the 0.04 s difference.

The graph settles it. Plotting $s$ against $t^2$ straightens the data, and the
gradient of that line is $\frac{1}{2}g$. A gradient of $4.9\,\text{m/s}^2$ gives
$g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$, and a gradient of $4.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ gives $9.6\,\text{m/s}^2$.
Both are close to the accepted value, so the model is good. Most of the gap between
the measured time and the predicted time is the timing, not the physics.

**What a better experiment would show.** A light gate removes the reaction time. With
the timing fixed, the remaining difference is the model's own error, and that is small
enough to measure.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which idea applies** and say
why. Some ask for a quantity, and some ask for a judgement.

1. Convert 3.5 km to metres, and 0.50 m$^3$ to litres.
2. A block has a mass of 0.80 kg and a volume of 0.00020 m$^3$. Calculate its density,
   and write the answer to the significant figures the data justify.
3. A ruler is marked in millimetres. State the reading uncertainty, and calculate the
   percentage uncertainty in a reading of 0.060 m.
4. A ball falls 1.80 m. Use $s = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$ to find the fall time and the
   landing speed. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.
5. The model predicts 0.61 s and the measurement is 0.66 s. Calculate the percentage
   difference, and say which is more likely to explain it: air resistance or reaction
   time.
6. A student records 0.72 s, 0.71 s, 0.73 s and 0.98 s for the same fall. Identify the
   anomalous reading, find the mean of the other three, and find the uncertainty as
   half the range.
7. A distance-time graph passes through (0, 1.0 m) and (4.0 s, 9.0 m). Find the
   gradient with its unit and the intercept. Say what the intercept means.
8. A force-extension graph is a straight line through the origin and the point
   (0.15 m, 9.0 N). Find the area under the graph, and say what the area represents.
9. Explain why a model that ignores air resistance can still be useful, and give one
   situation where the model would fail badly.
10. A student plots $s$ against $t^2$ and gets a straight line through the origin with
    gradient 5.1 m/s$^2$. Find $g$, and say whether the graph supports the
    falling-ball model.

## What you can now do

- write a measurement as a value, a unit and an uncertainty;
- convert between prefixes, including squared and cubed units;
- write an answer to the significant figures the data justify;
- combine uncertainties for sums and for products, and find the uncertainty in a mean;
- tell precision from accuracy, and random from systematic error;
- explain what a scientific model is, state its assumptions, and test it against
  evidence;
- plan a fair investigation with the right variables and suitable equipment;
- read a gradient, an intercept and an area from a graph, with their units;
- straighten a curve into a line whose gradient gives a physical constant.

## Thinking back

Return to the number you chose at the start of the chapter — the weather forecast,
the sprint time, the battery percentage.

You can now write it the way a physicist would: as a value with a unit and a range.
Write down the number, the unit, and the uncertainty you think it really has. If the
number was quoted with more figures than the measurement can support, say so.

An estimate with a range is more useful than a bare number, because it says how much
you trust it. That habit is the whole of this chapter, and it is exactly what the
Physics In Practice Investigation asks you to show.
