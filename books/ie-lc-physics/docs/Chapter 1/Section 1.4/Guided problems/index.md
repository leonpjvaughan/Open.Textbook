# 1.4 Planning and evaluating an investigation — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Find the mean of 0.42 s, 0.40 s and 0.41 s.
2. Find the range of those three readings.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $\dfrac{0.42 + 0.40 + 0.41}{3} = \dfrac{1.23}{3} = 0.41\,\text{s}$.
2. $0.42 - 0.40 = 0.02\,\text{s}$.

A mean and a range are the two things a set of repeats gives you. This section uses
both.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A student investigates how the time for a ball to fall depends on the height
it is dropped from. State the independent variable and the dependent variable.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One is changed on purpose; the other is measured.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The student chooses the heights. The timer gives the fall times.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The independent variable is the drop height, because the student changes it. The
dependent variable is the fall time, because it is measured and it depends on the
height.

Naming them the right way round matters when you plot a graph: the independent
variable goes on the horizontal axis.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** For the same investigation, name two control variables and say why each must
be kept the same.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What else could change the fall time besides the height?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The ball and the release method are both candidates.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Two control variables are:

- the ball, because a heavier or larger ball falls differently;
- the release method, because a ball that is pushed starts with a speed and falls
  faster.

If either one changes while the height changes, the result cannot be attributed to
the height. The test would not be fair.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A student records four timings of a ball fall: 0.61 s, 0.63 s, 0.62 s and
0.62 s. Find the mean and the uncertainty as half the range.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the range first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Range $= 0.63 - 0.61 = 0.02\,\text{s}$. Half of that is the uncertainty.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{mean} = \frac{0.61 + 0.63 + 0.62 + 0.62}{4} = \frac{2.48}{4} = 0.62\,\text{s}$$

$$\text{uncertainty} = \frac{0.63 - 0.61}{2} = \frac{0.02}{2} = 0.01\,\text{s}$$

The result is $0.62\,\text{s} \pm 0.01\,\text{s}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A student measures a force as 5.0 N, with an uncertainty of $\pm 0.2$ N.
Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the uncertainty by the reading.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{0.2}{5.0} \times 100\%$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{0.2}{5.0} \times 100\% = 4\%$$

So the force is $5.0\,\text{N} \pm 4\%$. A percentage uncertainty lets you compare
one measurement with another, even when the readings have different sizes.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A student records five timings for the same fall: 0.44 s, 0.45 s, 0.44 s,
0.61 s and 0.45 s. Identify the anomalous reading and say what the student should do
about it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which reading sits far from the other four?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

0.61 s is much larger than the others.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The reading of 0.61 s is anomalous, because the other four sit between 0.44 s and
0.45 s. The student should repeat that trial and check for a mistake in starting or
stopping the timer.

Leave the reading out of the mean only when you can say what went wrong. If the
repeat gives a value near 0.45 s, the original reading was a mistake. If it gives
0.61 s again, then the effect is real and needs explaining.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A student times a ball fall by hand and reports a time of 0.50 s. Explain why
the reaction time limits this measurement, and suggest one improvement.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The reaction time is about 0.20 s. Compare it with the reading.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$0.20\,\text{s}$ is a large fraction of $0.50\,\text{s}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A hand-timed stopwatch adds the reaction time at the start, at the stop, or at both.
If the reaction time is about 0.20 s, then the percentage uncertainty is
$\dfrac{0.20}{0.50} \times 100\% = 40\%$. A 40% uncertainty is far too large to test
the falling-ball model.

The improvement is to use a light gate, which starts and stops the timer without a
person. That removes the reaction time and leaves the model's own error visible.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 1.3

**7.** A ball falls 0.45 m in 0.30 s. Use the model $t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2s}{g}}$ to
predict the fall time, and compare it with the measured time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Substitute $s = 0.45$ m and $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$t = \sqrt{\dfrac{0.90}{9.8}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 0.45}{9.8}} = \sqrt{0.092} = 0.30\,\text{s}$$

The model predicts 0.30 s and the measurement is 0.30 s, so here they agree. With a
fall time of only 0.30 s, though, the reaction time is a large fraction of the
reading, so the agreement is partly good luck.

This is why the evaluation matters as much as the number. A result that agrees with a
model is only as convincing as the measurement behind it.

</details>
</details>
</details>
