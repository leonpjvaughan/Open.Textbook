# 1.4 Planning and evaluating an investigation — Learn

**Prerequisites:** measurement and uncertainty from Sections 1.1 and 1.2, and the
falling-ball model from Section 1.3.

**Objectives:** identify the independent, dependent and control variables; plan a fair
test; choose suitable equipment; record readings in a clear table; repeat and average;
evaluate sources of error and suggest improvements.

By the end of this section, you can plan an investigation and say how good it was.

## The three variables

Every investigation has one variable you change, one you measure, and the rest you
hold fixed.

- The **independent variable** is the one you change on purpose.
- The **dependent variable** is the one you measure. It depends on the independent
  variable.
- A **control variable** is anything that could affect the result and is kept the
  same.

A **fair test** changes only the independent variable. If two things change at once,
you cannot tell which one caused the effect you see.

> **Watch out.** A variable you forget to control does not disappear. If the drop
> height changes but the ball changes too, the result is unreadable. List the control
> variables before you start, and check each one as you go.

## Planning the ball-drop investigation

The chapter problem asks how fast a dropped ball falls. Here is how to plan the
measurement.

**The question.** How does the fall time depend on the drop height?

**The variables.** The independent variable is the drop height. The dependent
variable is the fall time. The control variables include the ball, the release
method, the surface below, and the way the timer is started and stopped.

**The apparatus.** A metre stick or a tape measure, a ball, a stopwatch, and a
release point you can hold at the same place each time. A light gate would time the
fall without a reaction time, and it is the better instrument when one is available.

**The method.**

1. Set the release point at a measured height above the floor.
2. Release the ball without pushing it.
3. Time the fall. Repeat each height at least three times.
4. Record every reading in a table, then find the mean for each height.
5. Change the height and repeat.

## Recording the readings

A results table is part of the experiment, not paperwork after it. A good table has:

- the quantity and its unit in the column heading, for example **Fall time / s**;
- one row for each trial;
- the same number of decimal places for readings from the same instrument.

| Drop height / m | Fall time 1 / s | Fall time 2 / s | Fall time 3 / s | Mean time / s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.40 | 0.29 | 0.31 | 0.30 | 0.30 |
| 0.80 | 0.42 | 0.40 | 0.41 | 0.41 |
| 1.20 | 0.51 | 0.49 | 0.50 | 0.50 |

Writing the unit in the heading keeps the numbers clean and makes the table readable
at a glance.

## Repeats and reliability

A single reading tells you very little. Repeating the measurement and taking the mean
reduces the effect of random error. Readings that agree with one another show that the
result is **reliable**.

A reading that sits far from the others is **anomalous**. Repeat that trial and look
for a mistake. Leave the anomalous reading out of the mean only when you can say what
went wrong.

## Evaluating the investigation

An evaluation is not a list of apologies. It is an honest account of what limited the
result, and what you would change.

Ask three questions:

1. **What were the main sources of error?** Reaction time, parallax when reading the
   metre stick, and the release method all matter here.
2. **How big was each one?** A reaction time of 0.20 s is large beside a fall time of
   0.50 s, so it limits the result badly.
3. **What would improve it?** A light gate removes the reaction time. A clamp at the
   release point fixes the height and the release method. More repeats reduce the
   random scatter.

This is the shape of the write-up for the **Physics In Practice Investigation**,
which is 40% of your Leaving Certificate mark. The skills are the same: take the
readings, record them well, and be honest about what limited them.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. Write a short plan for timing a ball dropped from a
range of heights.

State the independent variable, the dependent variable and two control variables.
Then name one source of error and one improvement. Keep the plan: Section 1.5 turns
your readings into a graph, and the Review uses the plan to answer the chapter
problem properly.

## You should now be able to

- identify the independent, dependent and control variables in an investigation;
- say what makes a test fair;
- choose equipment that suits the quantity being measured;
- record readings in a table with the quantity and unit in the heading;
- explain why repeats are taken and what makes a result reliable;
- identify an anomalous reading and say what to do about it;
- evaluate an investigation and suggest a concrete improvement.
