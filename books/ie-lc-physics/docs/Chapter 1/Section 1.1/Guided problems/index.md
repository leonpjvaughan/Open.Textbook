# 1.1 Physical quantities and SI units — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the SI unit of length?
2. How many metres are in 3 km?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The metre, m. It is one of the seven base units.
2. $3 \times 1000 = 3000\,\text{m}$. The prefix kilo means $10^3$.

Both answers come back in this section: a quantity is a number with a unit, and a
prefix is a power of ten.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** Convert 4.2 km to metres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The prefix kilo means $10^3$. Are you going to a smaller unit or a larger one?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Going from kilometres to metres means multiplying by 1000.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$4.2\,\text{km} = 4.2 \times 1000 = 4200\,\text{m}$$

A kilometre is a thousand metres, so the number of metres is larger. If your answer
is smaller than 4.2, you divided instead of multiplying.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Convert 850 g to kilograms.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The gram is the smaller unit. Will the number go up or down?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Divide by 1000.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$850\,\text{g} = 850 \div 1000 = 0.85\,\text{kg}$$

The number of kilograms is smaller, because a kilogram is a larger unit. A quick
check: 0.85 kg is a bit less than 1 kg, and 850 g is a bit less than 1000 g. It
agrees.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Convert 50 cm$^2$ to m$^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

$1\,\text{cm} = 10^{-2}\,\text{m}$. What does that give for an area?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$1\,\text{cm}^2 = (10^{-2})^2 = 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$50\,\text{cm}^2 = 50 \times 10^{-4} = 0.0050\,\text{m}^2$$

The factor for an area is $10^4$, not $10^2$, because the prefix is squared along with
the unit. Writing 0.50 m$^2$ here is the common slip.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A student walks 1800 m in 600 s. Calculate the average speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Average speed is distance divided by time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \dfrac{1800}{600}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \frac{d}{t} = \frac{1800}{600} = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$$

The unit comes from the division: metres divided by seconds gives m/s. Both readings
carry two significant figures, so 3.0 m/s is the right way to write it.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A metal block has a mass of 2.4 kg and a volume of 0.00030 m$^3$. Calculate its
density.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Density is mass divided by volume.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\rho = \dfrac{2.4}{0.00030}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{2.4}{0.00030} = 8000\,\text{kg/m}^3$$

To two significant figures that is $8.0 \times 10^{3}\,\text{kg/m}^3$. The unit
kg/m$^3$ is a derived unit: kilograms divided by cubic metres.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** The equation $v = u + at$ links speed $v$, initial speed $u$, acceleration $a$
and time $t$. Show that the units on both sides match.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out the unit of each term separately, then compare.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v$ and $u$ are speeds, in m/s. What is the unit of $at$?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$v$ has the unit m/s, and so does $u$. The term $at$ is acceleration times time, so
its unit is $\text{m/s}^2 \times \text{s} = \text{m/s}$.

All three terms have the unit m/s, so the equation is dimensionally consistent. That
does not prove the equation is correct, but it does show it could be. An equation
whose terms have different units cannot be right.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## The chapter problem begins here

**7.** You plan to drop a ball from a height of 1.20 m. Write down the height in SI
units, and state the unit you will use for the fall time. Then estimate the fall time
before you measure anything.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The SI unit of length is the metre and the SI unit of time is the second.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

1.20 m is already in metres. A fall of about a metre takes less than a second.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The height is 1.20 m, already an SI quantity. The fall time is measured in seconds.

A reasonable estimate is a few tenths of a second. A ball falling 1 m is moving
quickly by the end, but it starts from rest, so the fall takes about half a second.
Section 1.3 gives the model that turns this estimate into a prediction, and Section
1.4 shows you how to time it fairly.

</details>
</details>
</details>
