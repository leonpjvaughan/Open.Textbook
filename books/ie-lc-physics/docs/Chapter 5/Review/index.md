# Chapter 5: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The spring that stopped behaving.** You hang weights on a spring and measure how far
it stretches. Up to a point each extra newton stretches it by the same amount; then it
starts stretching further for the same extra force.

**What the graph shows.** Force plotted against extension rises as a straight line
through the origin while the spring obeys Hooke's law. The gradient of that line is the
spring constant:

$$k = \frac{\Delta F}{\Delta x}$$

An experiment giving 2.0 N at 0.05 m and 6.0 N at 0.15 m has

$$k = \frac{6.0 - 2.0}{0.15 - 0.05} = \frac{4.0}{0.10} = 40\,\text{N/m}$$

The line stops being straight at the **limit of proportionality**. Past that point the
extension is no longer proportional to the force, so the graph curves upwards: each
extra newton gives a larger extension than the one before. The spring's material is
being stretched beyond what it can spring back from.

Stretch it further and it reaches the **elastic limit**, past which it does not return
to its natural length when the load is removed. The limit of proportionality comes
first; the elastic limit is the point of no return.

**Why the energy formula stops working there too.** The energy stored is the area under
the graph. Below the limit that area is a triangle, which is where $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$
comes from. Past the limit the shape is no longer a triangle, so the formula no longer
gives the energy — you would have to find the area some other way.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies**
and say why. One of them is deliberately a question you should refuse to answer with the
formula you have been using.

1. A spring of constant 60 N/m is stretched by 0.15 m. Calculate the force and the
   energy stored.
2. A force of 9.0 N stretches a spring by 0.030 m. Calculate its spring constant, then
   the extension a force of 15 N would give.
3. Two springs of 80 N/m and 20 N/m are joined in series, and then in parallel.
   Calculate both combined spring constants. Check each against the size of the two.
4. A spring's graph is straight up to 0.20 m and curves afterwards. A force of 20 N
   gives an extension of 0.25 m. Explain why you cannot use $F = kx$ here, and say what
   you would do instead.
5. A 250 g mass hangs on a spring and stretches it by 0.049 m. Calculate the spring
   constant. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.
6. An extension is measured as 0.020 m on a metre stick read to the nearest millimetre.
   Calculate the percentage uncertainty, and compare it with the same uncertainty on a
   0.100 m extension.
7. A spring stores 3.0 J when stretched by 0.10 m. Calculate the energy stored at
   0.30 m.
8. A student plots force against **total length** instead of extension. Describe what
   is wrong with the graph and how it would show up in the shape of the line.
9. Two identical springs in series hold up a 2.0 kg mass. Each has a spring constant of
   400 N/m. Calculate the extension of the combination.
10. Explain, in terms of what stays the same, why springs in parallel have a larger
    combined spring constant than either spring on its own.

## What you can now do

- state Hooke's law and use $F = kx$ in any direction, with units;
- tell an extension from a length, and say why the difference matters;
- find the combined spring constant of springs in series and in parallel, and check the
  answer against the size of the individual constants;
- calculate elastic potential energy, and explain why doubling the extension multiplies
  it by four;
- find a spring constant from the gradient of a force-extension graph, and say which
  part of the graph to use;
- describe the sources of error in the investigation and calculate the percentage
  uncertainty in a reading.

## Thinking back

Look at the object you chose at the start of the chapter — a bungee cord, a mattress, a
pen clip, a shoe sole.

You can now say something quantitative about it. Estimate the force it carries and the
distance it moves, and work out a spring constant in N/m. Then answer the question you
started with: what happens if it is stretched too far?

If your estimate is rough, that is worth writing down too. Saying how rough an estimate
is — the uncertainty — is part of the physics, not an apology for it. It is also exactly
what the Physics In Practice Investigation asks you to do.
