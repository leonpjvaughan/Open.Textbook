# 5.3 Elastic potential energy — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Calculate the work done when a constant force of 8.0 N moves an object 0.50 m.
2. Work out $0.06^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $W = Fd = 8.0 \times 0.50 = 4.0\,\text{J}$.
2. $0.0036$. Squaring a decimal smaller than 1 makes it **smaller**, not larger — a
   trap worth knowing before you substitute into $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$.

Both reappear in this section: work is what stretching a spring does, and the extension
is squared.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A spring of spring constant 100 N/m is stretched by 0.20 m. Calculate the energy
stored.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

$E = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$. Square the extension first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 0.5 \times 100 \times 0.20^2 = 0.5 \times 100 \times 0.040$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = 0.5 \times 100 \times 0.040 = 2.0\,\text{J}$$

The unit: $\text{N/m} \times \text{m}^2 = \text{N}\,\text{m} = \text{J}$. Correct.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A spring of spring constant 400 N/m is stretched by 0.05 m. Calculate the energy
stored.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

$0.05^2 = 0.0025$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 0.5 \times 400 \times 0.0025$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = 0.5 \times 400 \times 0.0025 = 0.50\,\text{J}$$

A stiff spring stretched a little stores a modest amount. Compare with question 1: a
softer spring stretched four times as far stores four times the energy.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A spring of spring constant 250 N/m is stretched by 0.12 m. Calculate the energy
stored.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

$0.12^2 = 0.0144$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 0.5 \times 250 \times 0.0144$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = 0.5 \times 250 \times 0.0144 = 1.8\,\text{J}$$

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A spring stores 2.0 J when stretched by 0.10 m. Calculate the energy stored when
it is stretched by 0.30 m.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The extension is three times bigger. The energy depends on $x^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The energy multiplies by $3^2 = 9$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = 9 \times 2.0 = 18\,\text{J}$$

If you want to check it the long way: $E = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$ gives
$2.0 = 0.5 \times k \times 0.010$, so $k = 400\,\text{N/m}$. Then
$E = 0.5 \times 400 \times 0.09 = 18\,\text{J}$. It agrees.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A spring stores 5.0 J. It is stretched to twice the extension. State the new
stored energy and explain your reasoning in one sentence.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Write down how $E$ depends on $x$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E \propto x^2$, so doubling $x$ multiplies $E$ by 4.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$20\,\text{J}$.

The stored energy is proportional to the **square** of the extension, so doubling the
extension multiplies the energy by $2^2 = 4$.

An answer of $10\,\text{J}$ comes from treating the relationship as proportional to
$x$ rather than to $x^2$. That is the mistake this question is designed to catch.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A force-extension graph for a spring is a straight line through the origin
passing through $(0.08\,\text{m},\ 4.0\,\text{N})$. Use the area under the graph to find
the energy stored at 0.08 m, then check your answer with $E = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The area is a triangle: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Area $= 0.5 \times 0.08 \times 4.0$. And $k = \frac{4.0}{0.08} = 50\,\text{N/m}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

By the area: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.08 \times 4.0 = 0.16\,\text{J}$.

By the formula: $k = \frac{4.0}{0.08} = 50\,\text{N/m}$, so
$E = 0.5 \times 50 \times 0.08^2 = 0.5 \times 50 \times 0.0064 = 0.16\,\text{J}$.

They agree. The triangle is where the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ comes from — the force rises
steadily from zero, so the average force is half the final one.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 5.2

**7.** Two springs of 30 N/m are joined in series. Find the spring constant of the
combination, then find the energy stored when it is stretched by 0.20 m.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Series first, then energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{30} + \frac{1}{30} = \frac{2}{30}$, so $k = 15\,\text{N/m}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$k = 15\,\text{N/m}$, and

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = 0.5 \times 15 \times 0.20^2 = 0.5 \times 15 \times 0.040 = 0.30\,\text{J}$$

A check on the series rule: one 30 N/m spring stretched by 0.20 m would store
$0.5 \times 30 \times 0.04 = 0.60\,\text{J}$. Two in series store **half** as much for
the same extension, because the combination is easier to stretch — which is why the
combined spring constant is half.

</details>
</details>
</details>
