# 5.2 The spring constant and combinations of springs — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What does a large spring constant tell you about a spring?
2. Calculate the force that stretches a 50 N/m spring by 0.30 m.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. It is stiff — it takes a large force to stretch it a little.
2. $F = kx = 50 \times 0.30 = 15\,\text{N}$.

A smaller spring constant means a softer spring. That is the idea that decides both
answers in this section.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** Two springs of spring constant 100 N/m and 100 N/m are joined in series. Find the
spring constant of the combination.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Series adds reciprocals: $\frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{100} + \frac{1}{100} = \frac{2}{100}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$\frac{1}{k} = \frac{2}{100}$, so $k = 50\,\text{N/m}$.

Two identical springs in series give half the spring constant of one of them, which
makes sense: two springs end to end stretch twice as far for the same force. And 50 is
smaller than 100, which the check requires.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Two springs of spring constant 100 N/m and 100 N/m are joined in parallel. Find
the spring constant of the combination.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Parallel adds the constants directly.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$k = 100 + 100$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$k = 200\,\text{N/m}$.

Two identical springs side by side give twice the spring constant of one, because the
load is shared and each spring stretches only half as far. And 200 is larger than 100,
which the check requires.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Springs of 40 N/m and 60 N/m are joined in series. Find the combined spring
constant.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A common denominator of 40 and 60 is 120.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\frac{1}{k} = \frac{3}{120} + \frac{2}{120} = \frac{5}{120}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$\frac{1}{k} = \frac{5}{120}$, so $k = \frac{120}{5} = 24\,\text{N/m}$.

The check: 24 is smaller than 40, the smaller of the two. Correct for a series
combination.

A quicker route when there are only two springs is $k = \dfrac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2} =
\dfrac{40 \times 60}{100} = 24\,\text{N/m}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Springs of 40 N/m and 60 N/m are joined in parallel. Find the combined spring
constant.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$k = 40 + 60$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$k = 100\,\text{N/m}$.

The check: 100 is larger than 60, the larger of the two. Correct for a parallel
combination.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A spring of spring constant 30 N/m is joined in series with one of 20 N/m, and
the combination is pulled with a force of 6.0 N. Find the extension of each spring and
the total extension.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

In series the same force acts on both. Use $x = \frac{F}{k}$ for each.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$x_1 = \frac{6.0}{30}$ and $x_2 = \frac{6.0}{20}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$x_1 = \frac{6.0}{30} = 0.20\,\text{m} \qquad x_2 = \frac{6.0}{20} = 0.30\,\text{m}$$

Total extension $= 0.20 + 0.30 = 0.50\,\text{m}$.

Now check it with the combined constant: $\frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{30} + \frac{1}{20} =
\frac{5}{60}$, so $k = 12\,\text{N/m}$, and $x = \frac{6.0}{12} = 0.50\,\text{m}$. It
agrees, which is a good way to check a series calculation.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A student joins two springs in parallel and calculates a combined spring constant
of 15 N/m. The springs are 40 N/m and 60 N/m. What has gone wrong, and what is the right
answer?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which rule gives a smaller answer than either spring, and which gives a larger one?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

15 N/m is smaller than both, so the student used the series rule.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The student used the series rule. Two springs side by side share the load and each
stretches less, so the combination is **stiffer** than either spring. Its spring
constant must be larger than both.

For parallel, add: $k = 40 + 60 = 100\,\text{N/m}$.

(The series answer would be $\frac{40 \times 60}{40 + 60} = 24\,\text{N/m}$, which also
is not 15 — so there is an arithmetic slip as well as the wrong rule.)

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 5.1

**7.** A spring of spring constant 80 N/m is stretched by 0.05 m. Calculate the force
and say whether the spring is stiff or soft compared with one of constant 20 N/m.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $F = kx$, then compare the two constants.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = 80 \times 0.05 = 4.0\,\text{N}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = kx = 80 \times 0.05 = 4.0\,\text{N}$$

The 80 N/m spring is the stiffer of the two: for the same 0.05 m extension the 20 N/m
spring would need only $20 \times 0.05 = 1.0\,\text{N}$.

This comparison is the whole idea of this section, applied to single springs. Joining
springs is a way of building a spring with the constant you want.

</details>
</details>
</details>
