# Chapter 13: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The drop that lands in the right place.** An inkjet print head gives each drop of
ink a charge of $-2.0 \times 10^{-12}$ C. Two plates $2.0$ mm apart, with a potential
difference of $1200$ V across them, steer the drop to the right spot on the page.

In these formulas $q$ is a charge, $n$ a number of electrons, $e$ the charge on one
electron, $F$ a force and $d$ a distance. Also $E$ is an electric field strength,
$V$ a potential difference, $W$ the work done, $k$ the Coulomb constant and $Q$ a
point charge.

**Step 1: the electrons on a drop.** The drop is negative, so it carries an excess of
electrons. The number follows from $q = ne$:

$$n = \frac{|q|}{e} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-12}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 1.25 \times 10^{7}$$

That is about $1.3 \times 10^{7}$ electrons on one drop.

**Step 2: the force between two drops.** Two drops $1.0$ mm apart each carry
$2.0 \times 10^{-12}$ C. Coulomb's law gives

$$F = \frac{kq_1q_2}{d^2} = \frac{9.0 \times 10^{9} \times (2.0 \times 10^{-12})^2}{(1.0 \times 10^{-3})^2} = 3.6 \times 10^{-8}\,\text{N}$$

Both charges are negative, so this force repels the drops. It is far too small to aim
them.

**Step 3: the field between the plates.** The plates make a uniform field, so

$$E = \frac{V}{d} = \frac{1200}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 6.0 \times 10^{5}\,\text{V/m}$$

**Step 4: the force that steers the drop.** The force on the drop is

$$F = qE = 2.0 \times 10^{-12} \times 6.0 \times 10^{5} = 1.2 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{N}$$

The drop is negative, so the force is opposite to the field. The field runs from the
positive plate to the negative plate, so the drop moves towards the **positive**
plate.

**Step 5: why the field wins.** Compare the two forces:

$$\frac{1.2 \times 10^{-6}}{3.6 \times 10^{-8}} \approx 33$$

The force from the plates is about 33 times larger than the force between two drops.
It is also straightforward to control: change the voltage on the plates and the field
changes at once. That is why the printer charges the drop and steers it with a field.

**Step 6: the work done on the drop.** The drop crosses the full potential difference,
so

$$W = qV = 2.0 \times 10^{-12} \times 1200 = 2.4 \times 10^{-9}\,\text{J}$$

**The answer.** One drop carries about $1.3 \times 10^{7}$ excess electrons. The force
between two drops is $3.6 \times 10^{-8}$ N, which repels them. The field between the
plates is $6.0 \times 10^{5}$ V/m, and the force that steers one drop is
$1.2 \times 10^{-6}$ N towards the positive plate. The work done on the drop as it
crosses is $2.4 \times 10^{-9}$ J.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies**
and say why. Two of them ask you to choose between the field of a point charge and the
uniform field between plates.

1. A body gains $1.5 \times 10^{14}$ electrons. Calculate its charge.
2. Two charges of $+2.0\ \mu\text{C}$ and $+4.0\ \mu\text{C}$ are $0.20$ m apart.
   Calculate the force between them and state its direction.
3. Two charges repel with a force of $0.60$ N at a separation of $0.20$ m. Calculate
   the force at a separation of $0.40$ m.
4. A charge of $4.0\ \mu\text{C}$ experiences a force of $0.16$ N at a point in a
   field. Calculate the field strength at that point.
5. Two parallel plates are $3.0$ mm apart with a potential difference of $600$ V.
   Calculate the field strength between them.
6. A point charge of $+3.0\ \mu\text{C}$ is isolated in air. Calculate the field
   strength $0.10$ m from it.
7. A charge of $2.0\ \mu\text{C}$ is moved between two points, and the work done is
   $5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ J. Calculate the potential difference between the points.
8. A point charge of $+3.0\ \mu\text{C}$ is isolated in air. Calculate the potential
   $0.10$ m from it.
9. You must find the force between two small charged spheres. Which relationship
   applies, and what must you check about the distance before you use it?
10. A charge sits in an electric field. Would you use $E = \dfrac{V}{d}$ or
    $E = \dfrac{kQ}{d^2}$ to find the field strength? Explain how you decide.
11. A question from Chapter 6. A 5.0 kg mass has a weight of $8.0$ N on a small moon.
    Calculate the gravitational field strength at its surface, and compare the method
    with $E = \dfrac{F}{q}$.

## What you can now do

- describe the two kinds of charge, and charge an object by friction or by induction;
- use $q = ne$ to find a charge or a number of electrons;
- tell a conductor from an insulator, and explain grounding;
- state Coulomb's law and use it to find a force, a charge or a distance;
- explain the inverse-square law and predict the effect of changing the distance or
  the charges;
- describe electric fields with field lines, and use $E = \dfrac{F}{q}$ and $F = qE$;
- find the field strength between parallel plates with $E = \dfrac{V}{d}$;
- find the field and the potential of a point charge;
- calculate the work done when a charge moves through a potential difference;
- explain the link between field strength and potential gradient.

## Thinking back

Look at the charge you chose at the start of the chapter — a balloon on a wall, a
shock from a car door, a crackle from a fleece, dust on a screen, a flash of lightning.

You can now say something quantitative about it. Estimate the charge, the distance
and the force, and use Coulomb's law to check that your numbers are consistent. For a
balloon, the charge might be a few nanocoulombs and the distance a fraction of a
millimetre.

If your estimate is rough, write that down too. Saying how rough an estimate is
belongs in the physics, and it is exactly what the Physics In Practice Investigation
asks you to do.
