# 13.1 Electric charge and charging — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the atom and its particles from earlier science; forces from
Chapter 3.

**Objectives:** describe the two kinds of electric charge; use $q = ne$; explain
charging by friction and by induction; tell a conductor from an insulator; describe
grounding.

By the end of this section, you can explain how an object becomes charged and work
out the charge it carries.

## Two kinds of charge

Rub a polythene rod with a cloth and hold it near a thin stream of water. The stream
bends towards the rod. Something acts across the gap, with nothing in it to push.

That something is **electric charge**. Charge comes in two kinds, and we call them
**positive** and **negative**.

- Two charges of the same kind **repel** each other. Two positive charges push apart,
  and so do two negative charges.
- Two charges of opposite kinds **attract** each other. A positive and a negative
  charge pull together.

Charge is measured in **coulombs** (C). The coulomb is a large unit for the charges
you meet in a laboratory. A rubbed rod may carry only a few nanocoulombs.

## Charge is carried by particles

Every atom has a small, dense nucleus. The nucleus holds **protons**, which are
positive, and **neutrons**, which have no charge. **Electrons** move around the
nucleus, and each electron is negative.

The sizes of the charges are equal. A proton carries $+e$ and an electron carries
$-e$, where

$$e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}.$$

That value is the **elementary charge**. Charge is **quantised**: every charge is a
whole-number multiple of $e$. There is no such thing as half an electron's charge.

$$q = ne$$

- $q$ is the total charge, in coulombs (C);
- $n$ is a whole number of electrons;
- $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C is the charge on one electron.

An atom with equal numbers of protons and electrons is **neutral**. It has no net
charge. An object becomes charged when it gains or loses electrons. The protons stay
in the nucleus, so only the electrons move.

**Example 1.** A polythene rod gains $2.0 \times 10^{13}$ electrons. Calculate its
charge.

$$
q = -ne
$$

$$
q = -2.0 \times 10^{13} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}
$$

$$
q = -3.2 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{C}
$$

That is $-3.2\ \mu\text{C}$. The rod is negative because it gained electrons.

**Example 2.** A small metal sphere carries a charge of $-4.8 \times 10^{-15}$ C.
Calculate the number of excess electrons on it.

$$
n = \frac{|q|}{e}
$$

$$
n = \frac{4.8 \times 10^{-15}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}
$$

$$
n = 3.0 \times 10^{4}
$$

The sphere has $3.0 \times 10^{4}$ more electrons than protons.

**Example 3.** Two identical metal spheres carry charges of $+3.0\ \mu\text{C}$ and
$-1.0\ \mu\text{C}$. They are touched together and then separated. Calculate the
charge on each sphere.

Charge is conserved, so the total charge is the same before and after. The total is

$$
+3.0\ \mu\text{C} + (-1.0\ \mu\text{C}) = +2.0\ \mu\text{C}
$$

The two spheres are identical, so they share the total equally. Each ends with

$$
\frac{+2.0\ \mu\text{C}}{2} = +1.0\ \mu\text{C}
$$

## Free electrons and bound electrons

Metals behave differently from rubber and glass. The difference is how tightly the
electrons are held.

- In a **conductor** such as copper, some electrons are **free**. They are not bound
  to one atom, and they can move through the material. Metals are conductors.
- In an **insulator** such as rubber, glass or plastic, the electrons are **bound**
  to their atoms. They cannot move through the material.

This one difference explains how charge behaves on each kind of material.

- On a conductor, charge spreads out over the surface. Like charges repel, so they
  move as far apart as they can. The charge collects where the surface curves most
  sharply, such as at a point.
- On an insulator, charge stays where it was placed. It cannot move to spread out.

## Charging by friction

Rub a polythene rod with a woollen cloth. Electrons are pulled from the wool onto
the polythene. The rod gains electrons and becomes negative. The wool loses the same
electrons and becomes positive.

The total charge does not change. The electrons only moved from one material to the
other. This is the **conservation of charge**.

**Which way the electrons go** depends on the two materials. Some materials hold
electrons more strongly than others. The material that holds them more strongly
gains them.

## Charging by induction

You can also charge a metal sphere without touching it with a charged object.

1. Bring a negatively charged rod near a neutral metal sphere. The free electrons in
   the sphere are pushed away from the rod, to the far side. The near side is left
   positive.
2. Connect the far side of the sphere to the Earth with a wire. This is
   **grounding**. The pushed-away electrons flow into the Earth.
3. Remove the wire, then remove the rod. The sphere is left with a positive charge.

The rod never touched the sphere. The charge was produced by the movement of free
electrons, which is why the method is called **charging by induction**.

**Grounding** a charged conductor connects it to the Earth. The Earth is enormous,
so it can accept or supply electrons without changing. The conductor shares its
charge with the Earth and is left neutral.

## A charged object attracts a neutral one

A charged rod attracts a small piece of paper, even though the paper has no net
charge. The rod pulls or pushes the electrons inside the paper, so the charges in the
paper separate a little. The side nearest the rod becomes opposite in charge to the
rod. The attraction to the near side is stronger than the repulsion from the far
side, so the paper moves towards the rod.

This is why a balloon sticks to a wall, and why dust clings to a screen.

> **Watch out.** Rubbing does not create charge. It moves electrons from one material
> to the other. If the rod gains electrons, the cloth loses the same number, so the
> two charges are equal in size and opposite in sign. A sentence such as "the rod
> creates negative charge" is wrong, because charge is conserved. Also check the sign
> of a charge before you use it: a body that gains electrons is negative, not
> positive.
