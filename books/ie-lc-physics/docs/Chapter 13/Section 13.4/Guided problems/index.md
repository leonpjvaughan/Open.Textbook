# 13.4 Electric field strength and potential — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write the equation for the work done by a force, and give the unit of work.
2. Define gravitational potential at a point, and give its unit.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. Work is force times distance along the direction of the force, $W = Fd$. The unit
   is the joule, J.
2. Gravitational potential at a point is the gravitational potential energy per unit
   mass at that point. Its unit is the joule per kilogram, J/kg.

Both ideas come back here. Electric potential is the work per unit **charge**, and
the unit is the volt, which is a joule per coulomb.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** Two parallel plates are $5.0$ mm apart with a potential difference of $200$ V
between them. Calculate the field strength between the plates.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

For a uniform field, $E = \dfrac{V}{d}$. Convert the separation to metres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = \dfrac{200}{5.0 \times 10^{-3}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = \frac{V}{d} = \frac{200}{5.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 4.0 \times 10^{4}\,\text{V/m}$$

The field is uniform between the plates, so this value is the same at every point in
the middle of the gap.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** The field strength between two parallel plates is $6.0 \times 10^{5}$ V/m, and
the plates are $3.0$ mm apart. Calculate the potential difference between them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $E = \dfrac{V}{d}$ for $V$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V = Ed = 6.0 \times 10^{5} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-3}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V = Ed = 6.0 \times 10^{5} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-3} = 1.8 \times 10^{3}\,\text{V}$$

That is $1800$ V. A small separation with a large field needs a large potential
difference.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A point charge of $+4.0\ \mu\text{C}$ is isolated in air. Calculate the field
strength $0.30$ m from it. Take $k = 9.0 \times 10^{9}$ N m$^{2}$ C$^{-2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The field of a point charge follows an inverse-square law.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = \dfrac{kQ}{d^2} = \dfrac{9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-6}}{(0.30)^2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = \frac{kQ}{d^2} = \frac{9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-6}}{(0.30)^2} = \frac{3.6 \times 10^{4}}{9.0 \times 10^{-2}} = 4.0 \times 10^{5}\,\text{N/C}$$

The field points away from the positive charge.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A point charge of $+4.0\ \mu\text{C}$ is isolated in air. Calculate the
electric potential $0.30$ m from it. Take $k = 9.0 \times 10^{9}$ N m$^{2}$
C$^{-2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The potential of a point charge is $V = \dfrac{kQ}{d}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V = \dfrac{9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.30}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V = \frac{kQ}{d} = \frac{9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.30} = 1.2 \times 10^{5}\,\text{V}$$

The potential is positive because the charge is positive. Notice that the field fell
as $1/d^2$ in question 3, but the potential falls only as $1/d$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A charge of $3.0\ \mu\text{C}$ is moved between two points, and the work done
is $6.0 \times 10^{-4}$ J. Calculate the potential difference between the points.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Potential difference is work done per unit charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V = \dfrac{W}{q} = \dfrac{6.0 \times 10^{-4}}{3.0 \times 10^{-6}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V = \frac{W}{q} = \frac{6.0 \times 10^{-4}}{3.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 2.0 \times 10^{2}\,\text{V}$$

That is $200$ V. A volt is a joule per coulomb, which is why dividing the work by the
charge gives volts.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A charge of $5.0\ \mu\text{C}$ moves through a potential difference of $80$ V.
Calculate the work done on the charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $V = \dfrac{W}{q}$ for the work.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = qV = 5.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 80$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = qV = 5.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 80 = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{J}$$

The work done is the charge multiplied by the potential difference it moves through.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** Explain the link between electric field strength and potential, and say why
$E = \dfrac{V}{d}$ can only be used between parallel plates.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about how quickly the potential changes with distance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The field is the negative of the potential gradient.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The electric field strength is the negative of the potential gradient, so it measures
how quickly the potential changes with distance.

Between parallel plates the field is uniform, so the potential falls steadily and the
gradient is constant. That is why $E = \dfrac{V}{d}$ gives the field strength there.
Around a point charge the field is not uniform, so the potential does not fall
steadily, and $E = \dfrac{V}{d}$ cannot be used.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 6

**8.** A 2.0 kg mass is raised $4.0$ m near the Earth's surface. Calculate the gain
in gravitational potential energy. Take $g = 9.8$ N/kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Near the surface, use $E_p = mgh$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E_p = 2.0 \times 9.8 \times 4.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E_p = mgh = 2.0 \times 9.8 \times 4.0 = 78.4\,\text{J}$$

To two significant figures that is $78$ J. Lifting the mass stores energy in the
gravitational field. Moving a charge against an electric field stores energy in the
same way, and that is what the potential difference measures.

</details>
</details>
</details>
