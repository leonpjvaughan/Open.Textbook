# 13.3 Electric fields and field lines — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Define gravitational field strength, and give its unit.
2. State what happens to the force between two charges if the distance between them
   is tripled.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. Gravitational field strength at a point is the gravitational force per unit mass
   at that point. Its unit is the newton per kilogram, N/kg.
2. The force falls to one ninth of its value, because the force follows an
   inverse-square law and $3^2 = 9$.

Both answers carry over to this section. An electric field strength is a force per
unit **charge**, and the field of a point charge is also inverse-square.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A charge of $+4.0\ \mu\text{C}$ placed at a point in an electric field
experiences a force of $0.20$ N. Calculate the field strength at that point.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Field strength is force per unit charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = \dfrac{F}{q} = \dfrac{0.20}{4.0 \times 10^{-6}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = \frac{F}{q} = \frac{0.20}{4.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 5.0 \times 10^{4}\,\text{N/C}$$

The field strength is the force per unit positive charge at the point.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A charge of $-5.0\ \mu\text{C}$ is placed in a uniform field of strength
$3.0 \times 10^{5}$ N/C. Calculate the size of the force on the charge, and state its
direction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $F = qE$, and remember the sign of the charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = 5.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 3.0 \times 10^{5}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = qE = 5.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 3.0 \times 10^{5} = 1.5\,\text{N}$$

The charge is negative, so the force is opposite to the direction of the field.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** An electron is placed in a uniform electric field of strength
$4.0 \times 10^{4}$ N/C. Calculate the force on the electron. Take
$e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The electron carries the elementary charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = eE = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.0 \times 10^{4}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = eE = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.0 \times 10^{4} = 6.4 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{N}$$

The force is tiny, but the electron is extremely light, so it accelerates strongly.
The force points opposite to the field.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Describe the electric field around a single positive point charge. Include the
direction of the field lines and where the field is strongest.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The field is radial, like the spokes of a wheel.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The lines point outwards, and they are closest together near the charge.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The field is radial. The lines point straight outwards, away from the positive
charge, in every direction.

Near the charge the lines are closest together, so the field is strongest there. At a
larger distance the lines are further apart, so the field is weaker. A small positive
test charge placed in the field would be pushed outwards along a line.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Describe the electric field between two oppositely charged parallel plates, and
explain why it is called a uniform field.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What do the field lines look like in the middle of the gap?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The lines are straight, parallel and equally spaced.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The field lines run from the positive plate to the negative plate, at right angles to
both. In the middle of the gap they are straight, parallel and equally spaced.

The field is called uniform because its strength and its direction are the same at
every point between the plates. Near the edges the lines curve outwards, so the field
is not uniform there.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain why two electric field lines never cross.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What would the direction of the force be at a crossing point?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A test charge at the crossing point would have two possible directions of force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

An electric field line shows the direction of the force on a positive test charge. If
two field lines crossed, a test charge placed at the crossing point would feel a
force in two different directions at once.

A field can only point one way at a point, so field lines never cross.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 6

**7.** A 2.0 kg mass has a weight of 3.2 N on the surface of a moon. Calculate the
gravitational field strength at the surface.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Field strength is force per unit mass.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$g = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{3.2}{2.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$g = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{3.2}{2.0} = 1.6\,\text{N/kg}$$

This is the same structure as $E = \dfrac{F}{q}$ in this section. Gravity gives the
force per kilogram; the electric field gives the force per coulomb.

</details>
</details>
</details>
