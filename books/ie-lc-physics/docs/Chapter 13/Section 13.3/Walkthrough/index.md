# 13.3 Electric fields and field lines — Learn

**Prerequisites:** electric charge and Coulomb's law from Sections 13.1 and 13.2;
gravitational fields from Chapter 6.

**Objectives:** explain what an electric field is; describe the direction of a field
line; use $E = \dfrac{F}{q}$ and $F = qE$; describe the field around point charges
and between parallel plates.

By the end of this section, you can describe an electric field with field lines and
work out the force a field puts on a charge.

## The field as a model

A charge affects the space around it. Place a second charge nearby, and it feels a
force at once, with nothing in between. How does the first charge tell the second
one that it is there?

Physicists answer that question with a **model**. They say that a charge fills the
space around it with an **electric field**. A second charge placed in the field feels
a force because it is in the field, not because it is touched.

A field is a model, not a substance. You cannot see it or weigh it. What you can do
is measure the force on a charge placed at a point, and draw what the field does.
The drawing uses **field lines**.

## What a field line shows

A field line shows the direction of the force on a small **positive** test charge
placed at that point.

- The lines point **away from a positive charge** and **towards a negative charge**.
- Where the lines are **closer together**, the field is stronger.
- Where the lines are **further apart**, the field is weaker.
- Field lines **never cross**. If they did, a charge at the crossing point would have
  two directions of force, and a field can only point one way at a point.

## Fields around point charges

A single point charge has a **radial** field.

- Around a **positive** charge, the lines point straight outwards, like the spokes of
  a wheel.
- Around a **negative** charge, the lines point straight inwards.

In both cases the lines are closest together near the charge. The field is strongest
there, which is why the force grows so quickly as two charges get closer.

Now place two charges near each other.

- **Two opposite charges.** The lines run from the positive charge to the negative
  one, curving between them. The field is strong in the gap.
- **Two like charges.** The lines leave both charges outwards and bend away from each
  other. Halfway between them the two fields cancel, so the field strength is zero at
  that point.

## The uniform field between parallel plates

Two flat metal plates, one positive and one negative, make the most useful field of
all. In the middle of the gap the field lines are straight, parallel and equally
spaced. They run from the positive plate to the negative plate.

A field whose strength and direction are the same at every point is a **uniform
field**. The field between the plates is uniform in the middle, away from the edges.
Near the edges the lines curve outwards, and the field is not uniform there.

## Electric field strength

To describe a field with numbers, you measure the force on a charge placed in it. The
**electric field strength** at a point is the force per unit positive charge at that
point.

$$E = \frac{F}{q}$$

- $E$ is the electric field strength, in newtons per coulomb (N/C);
- $F$ is the force on the charge, in newtons (N);
- $q$ is the charge placed at the point, in coulombs (C).

Rearranging gives the force on any charge placed in a known field:

$$F = qE$$

The field strength is a **vector**. Its direction is the direction of the force on a
positive charge.

- A **positive** charge feels a force **along** the field.
- A **negative** charge feels a force **opposite** to the field.

**Example 1.** A charge of $+3.0\ \mu\text{C}$ placed at a point in an electric field
experiences a force of $0.012$ N. Calculate the field strength at that point.

$$
E = \frac{F}{q}
$$

$$
E = \frac{0.012}{3.0 \times 10^{-6}}
$$

$$
E = 4.0 \times 10^{3}\,\text{N/C}
$$

**Example 2.** A charge of $-2.0\ \mu\text{C}$ is placed in a uniform field of
strength $5.0 \times 10^{4}$ N/C. Calculate the size of the force on it, and state
its direction.

$$
F = qE
$$

$$
F = 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 5.0 \times 10^{4}
$$

$$
F = 0.10\,\text{N}
$$

The charge is negative, so the force is opposite to the direction of the field.

**Example 3.** An electron is placed in a uniform field of strength
$2.0 \times 10^{5}$ N/C. Calculate the force on the electron.

$$
F = eE
$$

$$
F = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{5}
$$

$$
F = 3.2 \times 10^{-14}\,\text{N}
$$

The force points opposite to the field, because the electron is negative.

> **Watch out.** The field strength at a point belongs to the **field**, not to the
> test charge. Put a larger charge at the same point and the force grows, but $E$
> stays the same. When you use $E = F/q$, divide the force by the charge you placed
> there. A second trap is the direction: a negative charge feels a force **opposite**
> to the field lines, so do not draw the arrow the same way for an electron and a
> proton.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The steering plates are $2.0$ mm apart, and a drop
carries $-2.0 \times 10^{-12}$ C.

1. Sketch the field lines between the plates, marking the positive and negative
   plates.
2. State which plate the drop moves towards, and explain your answer using the
   direction of the force on a negative charge.

Keep your answers. Section 13.4 uses the potential difference across the plates to
find the field strength, and then the force on the drop.

## You should now be able to

- explain what an electric field is, and why it is a model for a force at a distance;
- state what the direction and the spacing of field lines show;
- describe the field around a positive charge, a negative charge, two opposite
  charges and two like charges;
- describe the uniform field between two parallel plates;
- define electric field strength and use $E = \dfrac{F}{q}$;
- use $F = qE$ and state the direction of the force on a positive or a negative
  charge.
