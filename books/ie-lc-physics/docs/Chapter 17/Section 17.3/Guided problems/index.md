# 17.3 The charge-to-mass ratio of the electron — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What provides the centripetal force on an object that moves in a circle?
2. State the size of the force on a charge $q$ that moves at speed $v$ at right angles
   to a magnetic field of flux density $B$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The centripetal force is the resultant force towards the centre of the circle. It is
   not a new kind of force; something such as tension, gravity or, here, a magnetic
   force must supply it.
2. $F = Bqv$. The force is at right angles to both the velocity and the field.

The second answer is the starting point of this section. Here the magnetic force is
the centripetal force on the electron.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** An electron moves at $1.5 \times 10^7$ m/s at right angles to a magnetic field
of flux density $4.0 \times 10^{-3}$ T. Calculate the magnetic force on it. Take
$e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $F = Bev$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = 4.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.5 \times 10^7$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = Bev = 4.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.5 \times 10^7$$

$$F = 9.6 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{N}$$

The force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it turns the electron without changing
its speed.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Calculate the radius of the circular path of the electron in question 1. Take
$m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Set the magnetic force equal to the centripetal force, $Bev = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$r = \dfrac{mv}{Be} = \dfrac{9.1 \times 10^{-31} \times 1.5 \times 10^7}{4.0 \times
10^{-3} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$r = \frac{mv}{Be} = \frac{9.1 \times 10^{-31} \times 1.5 \times 10^7}{4.0 \times
10^{-3} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}$$

$$r = \frac{1.4 \times 10^{-23}}{6.4 \times 10^{-22}} = 2.1 \times 10^{-2}\,\text{m}$$

So the radius is 2.1 cm. A stronger field would give a smaller radius, because the
magnetic force would be larger for the same speed.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** In a crossed-field experiment the electric field strength is
$3.52 \times 10^4$ V/m and the flux density is $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ T. The beam passes
through undeflected. Calculate the speed of the electrons.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The forces balance, so $eE = Bev$. Rearrange for $v$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \dfrac{E}{B} = \dfrac{3.52 \times 10^4}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \frac{E}{B} = \frac{3.52 \times 10^4}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 1.76 \times
10^7\,\text{m/s}$$

The charge cancels in $eE = Bev$, so the speed does not depend on the size of the
charge. That is why the method works for any charged particle.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Use the speed from question 3. The electrons now enter the magnetic field alone
and travel in a circle of radius 5.0 cm. Calculate the charge-to-mass ratio of the
electron.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $\dfrac{e}{m} = \dfrac{v}{Br}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{e}{m} = \dfrac{1.76 \times 10^7}{2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.05}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{e}{m} = \frac{v}{Br} = \frac{1.76 \times 10^7}{2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.05}$$

$$\frac{e}{m} = \frac{1.76 \times 10^7}{1.0 \times 10^{-4}} = 1.76 \times
10^{11}\,\text{C/kg}$$

This is the accepted value. The whole measurement needs only the two field strengths
and the radius.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain why the crossed-field method finds the speed of the electrons without
knowing their charge or mass.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look at the equation $eE = Bev$ and cancel.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The charge appears on both sides, so it cancels.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

When the beam is undeflected, the electric force and the magnetic force are equal in
size:

$$eE = Bev$$

The charge $e$ appears on both sides, so it cancels. Dividing by $B$ gives
$v = \dfrac{E}{B}$, which contains only the two field strengths.

The mass never appears in the balance at all. So the speed is found from the fields
alone, with no knowledge of the charge or the mass of the particle.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 7

**6.** An electron moves in a circle of radius 0.05 m at a constant speed of
$1.76 \times 10^7$ m/s. Calculate its centripetal acceleration, and compare it with the
acceleration the magnetic force gives it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Centripetal acceleration is $\dfrac{v^2}{r}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{(1.76 \times 10^7)^2}{0.05}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(1.76 \times 10^7)^2}{0.05} = \frac{3.1 \times 10^{14}}{0.05}
= 6.2 \times 10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The magnetic force on the electron is $F = Bev = 2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 1.6 \times
10^{-19} \times 1.76 \times 10^7 = 5.6 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{N}$. Dividing by the
mass gives

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{5.6 \times 10^{-15}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}} = 6.2 \times
10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2$$

The two ways agree. That is the point of the section: the magnetic force **is** the
centripetal force, so the two calculations describe the same motion.

</details>
</details>
</details>
