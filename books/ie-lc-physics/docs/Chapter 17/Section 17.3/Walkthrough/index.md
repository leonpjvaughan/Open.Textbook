# 17.3 The charge-to-mass ratio of the electron — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the electron gun from Section 17.2; the force on a moving charge in
a magnetic field from Chapter 15; circular motion from Chapter 7.

**Objectives:** describe the force on a moving charge in a magnetic field; explain how
crossed fields measure the speed of a beam; use $Bev = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ to find the
charge-to-mass ratio of the electron.

By the end of this section, you can find the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron from
a deflection measurement and explain how Thomson measured it.

## The force on a moving charge

A charge that moves across a magnetic field feels a force. When the velocity is at
right angles to the field, the size of the force is

$$F = Bqv$$

- $B$ is the magnetic flux density, in teslas (T);
- $q$ is the charge, in coulombs (C);
- $v$ is the speed, in metres per second (m/s).

For an electron, $q = e$, so the force is $F = Bev$.

The force is always perpendicular to the velocity. A force at right angles to the
motion changes the direction of the electron but not its speed, so it does no work on
the electron. The result is circular motion at a constant speed.

**Example 1.** An electron moves at $2.0 \times 10^7$ m/s at right angles to a
magnetic field of flux density $3.0 \times 10^{-3}$ T. Calculate the magnetic force on
it.

$$
F = Bev
$$

$$
F = 3.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^7
$$

$$
F = 9.6 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{N}
$$

## Circular motion in a magnetic field

The magnetic force is the centripetal force that keeps the electron on its circular
path. So the magnetic force equals $\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$:

$$Bev = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

Cancel one power of $v$ from each side and rearrange for the radius:

$$r = \frac{mv}{Be}$$

The radius grows with the speed and the mass, and shrinks as the field gets stronger.
That is exactly what a stiffer field does to a beam: it bends it into a tighter circle.

Rearranged for the charge-to-mass ratio, the same equation gives

$$\frac{e}{m} = \frac{v}{Br}$$

So if you know the speed $v$, the flux density $B$ and the radius $r$, you can find
$e/m$. That is the measurement.

## Crossed fields measure the speed

The radius equation needs the speed. Thomson found the speed with **crossed fields**:
an electric field and a magnetic field set at right angles to each other, and both at
right angles to the beam.

When the two fields are adjusted so that the beam passes through undeflected, the
electric and magnetic forces on each electron are equal and opposite:

$$eE = Bev$$

where $E$ is the electric field strength.

The charge cancels, leaving

$$v = \frac{E}{B}$$

This is the key step. The speed is found from the two field strengths alone. The
charge and the mass never appear, so the method works without knowing either of them.

**Example 2.** In a crossed-field experiment the electric field strength is
$3.0 \times 10^4$ V/m and the flux density is $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ T. The beam passes
through undeflected. Calculate the speed of the electrons.

$$
v = \frac{E}{B}
$$

$$
v = \frac{3.0 \times 10^4}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}}
$$

$$
v = 1.5 \times 10^7\,\text{m/s}
$$

## Finding the charge-to-mass ratio

Thomson then switched off the electric field and left the magnetic field on. The beam
curved into a circle, and he measured its radius. With $v$ from the crossed fields, he
could then calculate $e/m$ from $\dfrac{e}{m} = \dfrac{v}{Br}$.

**Example 3.** Electrons leave a gun at $2.7 \times 10^7$ m/s. They enter a magnetic
field of flux density $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ T at right angles and travel in an arc of
radius $7.5 \times 10^{-2}$ m. Calculate the charge-to-mass ratio.

$$
\frac{e}{m} = \frac{v}{Br}
$$

$$
\frac{e}{m} = \frac{2.7 \times 10^7}{2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 7.5 \times 10^{-2}}
$$

$$
\frac{e}{m} = \frac{2.7 \times 10^7}{1.5 \times 10^{-4}}
$$

$$
\frac{e}{m} = 1.8 \times 10^{11}\,\text{C/kg}
$$

The accepted value is $1.76 \times 10^{11}$ C/kg. The measurement is close, and it is
the same for every gas and every cathode.

**Example 4.** The speed can also be removed from the calculation. If the electrons
were accelerated through a voltage $V$, then $eV = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ as well. Squaring
$\dfrac{e}{m} = \dfrac{v}{Br}$ and substituting for $v^2$ gives

$$\frac{e}{m} = \frac{2V}{B^2r^2}$$

For $V = 2000$ V, $B = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ T and $r = 7.5 \times 10^{-2}$ m:

$$
\frac{e}{m} = \frac{2 \times 2000}{(2.0 \times 10^{-3})^2 (7.5 \times 10^{-2})^2}
$$

$$
\frac{e}{m} = \frac{4000}{2.25 \times 10^{-8}} = 1.8 \times 10^{11}\,\text{C/kg}
$$

The same answer, from the voltage, the field and the radius alone. This is the second
step of the chapter problem.

> **Watch out.** $F = Bqv$ applies only when the velocity is at **right angles** to the
> magnetic field. If the charge moves along the field, the force is zero and the path
> is a straight line. Also, the magnetic force does no work, so it changes the
> direction of the electron but never its speed.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The electrons leave the gun at $2.7 \times 10^7$ m/s, and
they curve in a field of $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ T with a radius of 7.5 cm.

Write down $Bev = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$, rearrange it for $\dfrac{e}{m}$, and substitute the
three values. State your answer in C/kg to two significant figures. Then write one
sentence comparing your value with the accepted value of $1.76 \times 10^{11}$ C/kg.

You have now answered the chapter problem. The Review collects the two steps together
and adds a route that does not need the speed at all.

## You should now be able to

- state the force on a charge moving at right angles to a magnetic field, and explain
  why the path is a circle;
- use $Bev = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ to find a radius or a speed;
- explain how crossed fields measure the speed of a beam without knowing the charge or
  the mass;
- calculate the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron from $v$, $B$ and $r$;
- explain why the value found is the same for every gas and every cathode.
