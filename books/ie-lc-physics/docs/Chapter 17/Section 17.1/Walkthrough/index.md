# 17.1 Evidence for the electron — Learn

**Prerequisites:** electric charge and electric fields from Chapter 13; the force on a
charge; magnetic fields from Chapter 15.

**Objectives:** describe a discharge tube and cathode rays; state the evidence that
cathode rays are negatively charged particles; explain what the Maltese cross shows;
state Thomson's conclusion.

By the end of this section, you can describe the experiments that showed cathode rays
are streams of electrons, and calculate the force and acceleration a field gives an
electron.

## The discharge tube

Take a glass tube and seal a metal electrode into each end. Pump most of the air out,
so the gas inside is at low pressure. Connect a high voltage across the electrodes.

The tube glows. The glow starts at the electrode connected to the negative terminal.
That electrode is the **cathode**, so the rays coming from it are called **cathode
rays**.

The glow is not the rays themselves. The rays are invisible. The glow is light given
out by the gas atoms where the rays strike them.

## What the experiments showed

A long series of experiments with discharge tubes gave cathode rays a clear set of
properties.

- They travel in straight lines from the cathode.
- They carry energy and momentum. A small paddle wheel placed in the beam can be made
  to turn.
- They are deflected by electric and magnetic fields, and the direction of the
  deflection is the one a **negative** charge would take.
- They behave in the same way whatever gas fills the tube, and whatever metal makes
  the cathode.

Each of these was a separate experiment. The last one is the most important, and it
comes back at the end of this section.

## The Maltese cross

Fix a metal cross in the path of the rays, and coat the far end of the tube with a
fluorescent screen. When the tube is switched on, a sharp shadow of the cross appears
on the screen.

The shadow has the same shape as the cross, with sharp edges. That is what you expect
if the rays travel in straight lines from the cathode and the cross blocks them. A
wave that spread out from a point would give a blurred shadow, with bright edges.

## Deflected by an electric field

Now place two metal plates inside the tube, one above the beam and one below. Connect
them to a supply so that one plate is positive and the other is negative.

An electric field runs from the positive plate to the negative plate. The force on a
charge is along the field for a positive charge, and opposite to the field for a
negative charge.

The beam bends towards the **positive** plate. So the charge carried by the rays is
negative. This is the deflection experiment that gives the sign of the charge.

A magnetic field bends the beam as well. The force on a moving charge in a magnetic
field is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field, so the beam curves. The
direction of the curve depends on the direction of the field, and it agrees with a
negative charge.

## Force and acceleration in a field

The force on a charge $q$ in a uniform electric field of strength $E$ is

$$F = qE$$

For an electron the charge is $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

**Example 1.** An electron sits in a uniform electric field of strength
$4.0 \times 10^4$ N/C. Calculate the electric force on it.

$$
F = eE
$$

$$
F = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.0 \times 10^4
$$

$$
F = 6.4 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{N}
$$

The force points opposite to the field, because the electron is negative.

**Example 2.** Calculate the acceleration of the same electron. Take its mass as
$m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.

$$
a = \frac{F}{m}
$$

$$
a = \frac{6.4 \times 10^{-15}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}}
$$

$$
a = 7.0 \times 10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2
$$

That is an enormous acceleration. It is one reason the beam responds so quickly when
the plates are charged.

**Example 3.** Compare the electric force from Example 1 with the weight of the
electron.

$$
W = mg = 9.1 \times 10^{-31} \times 9.8 = 8.9 \times 10^{-30}\,\text{N}
$$

Here $W$ is the weight and $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ is the gravitational field
strength.

The electric force is about $10^{15}$ times larger. This is why the weight of an
electron is ignored in every calculation in this chapter.

> **Watch out.** The glow in the tube is not the beam. The beam is invisible, and the
> glow is the gas giving out light where the beam hits it. A question that asks what
> you see wants the glow and the shadow, described in terms of the beam behind them.

## Thomson's conclusion

In 1897 J. J. Thomson measured the charge-to-mass ratio of the particles in cathode
rays. He found a value of about $1.76 \times 10^{11}$ C/kg.

Two things about that number mattered.

- It was the **same** for every gas and every cathode metal. So the particles are not
  pieces of the particular gas or metal. They are present in all matter.
- It was about 1800 times larger than the charge-to-mass ratio of a hydrogen ion. The
  charge on the particle is the same size as the charge on a hydrogen ion, so the mass
  must be far smaller. The particle is lighter than any atom.

Thomson concluded that cathode rays are streams of very light, negatively charged
particles. These particles are the **electrons**.
