# 17.1 Evidence for the electron — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the unit of electric charge?
2. A negative charge sits in a uniform electric field. State the direction of the force
   on it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The coulomb, C. One coulomb is the charge carried past a point by a current of one
   ampere in one second.
2. The force is opposite to the direction of the field. The field runs from positive to
   negative, so a negative charge is pushed towards the positive plate.

The second answer is the whole of the deflection experiment in Section 17.1. If you
wrote "along the field", that rule is for a positive charge.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A beam of cathode rays passes between two plates. The upper plate is positive.
State the direction in which the beam bends, and explain your answer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which way does the electric force push a negative charge?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The field runs from the positive plate to the negative plate. A negative charge is
pushed opposite to the field.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The beam bends **upwards**, towards the positive plate.

The field between the plates runs from the positive upper plate to the negative lower
plate. The force on a charge is along the field for a positive charge and opposite to
the field for a negative charge. The electrons are pushed upwards, so the rays carry a
negative charge.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** An electron sits in a uniform electric field of strength $2.0 \times 10^4$ N/C.
Calculate the electric force on it. Take $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $F = eE$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$F = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^4$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$F = eE = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^4 = 3.2 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{N}$$

The force is small in everyday terms, but the electron is extremely light, so the
effect is large.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Calculate the acceleration of the electron in question 2. Take its mass as
$9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Newton's second law, with the force from question 2.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{3.2 \times 10^{-15}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{3.2 \times 10^{-15}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}} = 3.5 \times 10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2$$

To two significant figures the answer is $3.5 \times 10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2$. This is
about $10^{14}$ times the acceleration of a falling object, which is why the beam in a
tube responds as soon as the plates are charged.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Calculate the weight of an electron and compare it with the electric force from
question 2. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Weight is $mg$. Then divide the electric force by the weight.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 9.1 \times 10^{-31} \times 9.8$, then compare with $3.2 \times 10^{-15}$ N.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mg = 9.1 \times 10^{-31} \times 9.8 = 8.9 \times 10^{-30}\,\text{N}$$

$$\frac{F}{W} = \frac{3.2 \times 10^{-15}}{8.9 \times 10^{-30}} = 3.6 \times 10^{14}$$

The electric force is about $10^{14}$ times the weight. Gravity is negligible here,
which is why every calculation in this chapter ignores it.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain how the Maltese cross experiment shows that cathode rays travel in
straight lines.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What would the shadow look like if the rays spread out from the cathode?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A sharp shadow means the rays were blocked along straight paths.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A metal cross is fixed in the tube, and the far end is coated with a fluorescent
screen. A sharp shadow of the cross appears on the screen.

A sharp shadow with the same shape as the cross can only form if the rays travel in
straight lines from the cathode. The cross blocks the rays along those lines. If the
rays spread out like a wave, the edges of the shadow would be blurred.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** The charge-to-mass ratio of cathode rays is measured with hydrogen, then with
air, then with carbon dioxide in the tube. The value is the same every time. Explain
why this matters.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Could the rays be pieces of the gas that happens to be in the tube?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The same value for different gases means the particles do not come from the gas.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

If the rays were pieces of the gas in the tube, the charge-to-mass ratio would change
when the gas changed. It does not change, so the particles are not pieces of the gas.

The same value is found for every cathode metal too. So the particles are present in
all matter. They are a common constituent of matter, which is why Thomson called them
electrons.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 13

**7.** Two parallel plates are 0.05 m apart, and the electric field strength between
them is $2.0 \times 10^4$ N/C. Calculate the potential difference between the plates.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

For a uniform field, field strength is potential difference divided by separation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V = Ed = 2.0 \times 10^4 \times 0.05$, with $V$ the potential difference, $E$ the
field strength and $d$ the separation of the plates.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V = Ed = 2.0 \times 10^4 \times 0.05 = 1000\,\text{V}$$

This is the link between this section and the next one. A pair of plates with a known
potential difference makes a uniform field, and an accelerating voltage is what gives
the electrons their speed in Section 17.2.

</details>
</details>
</details>
