# 17.2 Thermionic emission and the electron gun — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write down the equation for the kinetic energy of a moving object.
2. An electron is accelerated through a potential difference of 100 V. What energy does
   it gain, in electronvolts?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, where $m$ is the mass in kilograms and $v$ is the speed in
   metres per second.
2. 100 eV. One electronvolt is the energy gained by an electron accelerated through one
   volt, so the number of electronvolts equals the voltage in volts.

The second answer is the definition that makes the electronvolt useful. To turn it
into joules, multiply by $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** An electron is accelerated from rest through 250 V. Calculate the kinetic energy
it gains, in joules and in electronvolts. Take $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The energy gained is $eV$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 250$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = eV = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 250 = 4.0 \times 10^{-17}\,\text{J}$$

In electronvolts the answer is 250 eV, because the energy in electronvolts equals the
accelerating voltage in volts.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** An electron is accelerated from rest through 800 V. Calculate its speed. Take
$e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C and $m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Set $eV = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ and rearrange for $v$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2eV}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 800}{9.1
\times 10^{-31}}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 800}{9.1
\times 10^{-31}}}$$

$$v = \sqrt{2.8 \times 10^{14}} = 1.7 \times 10^7\,\text{m/s}$$

To two significant figures the speed is $1.7 \times 10^7\,\text{m/s}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** An electron has a kinetic energy of $8.0 \times 10^{-17}$ J. Calculate its
speed. Take $m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start from $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ and rearrange for $v$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E_k}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 8.0 \times 10^{-17}}{9.1 \times
10^{-31}}}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2E_k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 8.0 \times 10^{-17}}{9.1 \times
10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{14}}$$

$$v = 1.3 \times 10^7\,\text{m/s}$$

The energy $8.0 \times 10^{-17}$ J is the same as 500 eV, so this speed matches the
500 V gun in the section.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Explain why the electrons in an electron gun must travel through a vacuum.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What is in the tube if it is not a vacuum?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Air molecules would be in the way.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

If the tube contained air, the electrons would collide with the air molecules. Each
collision transfers energy and changes the direction of the electron, so the electrons
would slow down and scatter.

In a vacuum there are no air molecules to collide with. The electrons keep the energy
the field gives them and travel in a narrow beam.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain what thermionic emission is, and why the filament must be heated.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What holds an electron inside a metal?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

An electron needs at least the work function to escape.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Thermionic emission is the release of electrons from the surface of a hot metal.

An electron inside a metal is held in place, and it needs at least the work function,
the minimum escape energy, to leave the surface. Heating the filament gives the
electrons thermal energy. Once the metal is hot enough, some electrons have more
energy than the work function and escape from the surface.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 4

**6.** A force of $2.0 \times 10^{-15}$ N pushes an electron through a distance of
0.05 m. Calculate the work done. Then work out the potential difference that would
give the electron the same energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work is force times distance. Then use $E = eV$ to find the voltage.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = Fd = 2.0 \times 10^{-15} \times 0.05$, then $V = \dfrac{W}{e}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = Fd = 2.0 \times 10^{-15} \times 0.05 = 1.0 \times 10^{-16}\,\text{J}$$

Then $W = eV$, so

$$V = \frac{W}{e} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-16}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 625\,\text{V}$$

So a 625 V accelerating voltage gives the electron the same energy as this constant
force. This is the connection between the work-energy idea from Chapter 4 and the
accelerating voltage in an electron gun.

</details>
</details>
</details>
