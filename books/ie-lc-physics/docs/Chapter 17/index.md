# Chapter 17: The electron

A sealed glass tube, a heated wire and a high voltage. In this chapter you find out how
a beam of electrons is made, how fast the electrons travel, and how the curved path of
the beam in a magnetic field gives the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron.

## The chapter problem

**How fast, and how heavy?** A sealed glass tube holds a heated filament. A potential
difference of 2000 V accelerates the electrons into a narrow beam. The beam then enters
a magnetic field of flux density $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ T at right angles to its
direction, and the electrons curve along an arc of radius 7.5 cm.

By the end of the chapter you can work out the speed of the electrons from the
accelerating voltage, find the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron from the radius of
the arc, and say what each result tells you about this particle.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- describe the evidence that cathode rays are streams of negatively charged particles,
  not waves;
- explain what thermionic emission is and how an electron gun uses it;
- use $eV = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ to find the speed of an electron from an accelerating
  voltage;
- explain how crossed electric and magnetic fields measure the speed of a beam;
- use $Bev = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ to find the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron.

## Where it is used

Old television tubes and oscilloscopes steer an electron beam with fields. Electron
guns drive electron microscopes and X-ray tubes. The same charge-to-mass ratio lets you
follow a particle through a mass spectrometer.

## Your turn to connect it

Where have you met a beam of particles that is steered without being touched? A
television picture tube, an old computer monitor, a hospital X-ray set, an electron
microscope, a particle accelerator.

Choose one. Write down what makes the beam move, and what you would change to steer it
somewhere else. You will be able to answer that with fields by the end of the chapter.
