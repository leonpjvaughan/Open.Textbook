# 14.2 Ohm's law and resistors — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write down the equation that defines resistance, and give the unit of resistance.
2. A 6.0 Ω resistor has a potential difference of 3.0 V across it. Calculate the current
   through it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$. Resistance is measured in ohms, Ω, and one ohm is one volt per
   ampere.
2. $I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{3.0}{6.0} = 0.50\,\text{A}$. This is the equation you
   rearrange in this section, so keep it close.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A 20 Ω resistor has a potential difference of 4.0 V across it. Calculate the
current through it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use Ohm's law in the form $I = \dfrac{V}{R}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I = \dfrac{4.0}{20}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{4.0}{20} = 0.20\,\text{A}$$

The resistor is ohmic, so doubling the potential difference to 8.0 V would double the
current to 0.40 A.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A current of 0.30 A flows through a 50 Ω resistor. Calculate the potential
difference across it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Now you know the current and the resistance, so use $V = IR$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$V = 0.30 \times 50$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$V = IR = 0.30 \times 50 = 15\,\text{V}$$

The unit works out as amperes multiplied by ohms, which gives volts.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A student plots potential difference on the vertical axis against current on the
horizontal axis for a resistor. The points are $(0.10\,\text{A}, 2.0\,\text{V})$,
$(0.20\,\text{A}, 4.0\,\text{V})$ and $(0.30\,\text{A}, 6.0\,\text{V})$. Calculate the
gradient of the line and state what it represents.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Gradient is $\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}$, and the vertical axis is potential
difference.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Gradient $= \dfrac{6.0 - 2.0}{0.30 - 0.10}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{gradient} = \frac{6.0 - 2.0}{0.30 - 0.10} = \frac{4.0}{0.20} = 20\,\Omega$$

Because $V = IR$ is $V = R \times I$, the gradient of a graph of $V$ against $I$ is the
resistance. The line passes through the origin, which is what an ohmic conductor gives.
If the axes were swapped, the gradient would be $\dfrac{1}{R}$ instead.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A filament lamp has a resistance of 8.0 Ω when it is cold and 40 Ω when it is hot
and lit. It is connected to a 12 V supply and reaches its working temperature. Calculate
the current through it, and explain why the current is smaller than it would be if the
resistance stayed at 8.0 Ω.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the resistance at the working temperature, because that is the resistance the lamp
has while it is lit.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I = \dfrac{12}{40}$. Compare it with $\dfrac{12}{8.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{12}{40} = 0.30\,\text{A}$$

If the resistance stayed at 8.0 Ω, the current would be $\dfrac{12}{8.0} = 1.5$ A, five
times larger. The current heats the filament, the temperature rises, and the resistance
of the metal rises with it. A larger resistance means a smaller current, so the lamp
settles at 0.30 A. This is why a filament lamp is non-ohmic: its resistance is not
constant.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Describe the graph of current against potential difference for a diode in forward
bias and in reverse bias, and say why a diode does not obey Ohm's law.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the direction the diode allows charge to flow, and about the potential
difference needed before it conducts at all.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Forward bias: almost no current until about 0.6 V, then a steep rise. Reverse bias:
almost no current.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

In forward bias the diode carries almost no current until the potential difference
reaches about 0.6 V. After that the current rises steeply. In reverse bias it carries
almost no current whatever the potential difference. The current is not proportional to
the potential difference in either direction, so the graph is not a straight line
through the origin and the diode does not obey Ohm's law.

A diode is useful because of this one-way behaviour: it lets current through in one
direction and blocks it in the other.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A thermistor has a resistance of 400 Ω at 25 °C and 150 Ω at 70 °C. The potential
difference across it is 3.0 V at both temperatures. Calculate the current through it at
each temperature.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Ohm's law applies at each temperature separately, with the resistance the thermistor has
at that temperature.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I = \dfrac{3.0}{400}$ at 25 °C, and $I = \dfrac{3.0}{150}$ at 70 °C.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

At 25 °C:

$$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{3.0}{400} = 0.0075\,\text{A} = 7.5\,\text{mA}$$

At 70 °C:

$$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{3.0}{150} = 0.020\,\text{A} = 20\,\text{mA}$$

The current is larger when the thermistor is hot, because its resistance is smaller. A
thermistor is used as a temperature sensor for exactly this reason.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 14.1

**7.** A current of 0.25 A flows through a resistor for 40 s. Calculate the charge that
passes through it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the definition of current, $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$Q = It = 0.25 \times 40$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$Q = It = 0.25 \times 40 = 10\,\text{C}$$

This matters here because Ohm's law links a current to a potential difference. The
current itself is still a rate of flow of charge, and the two ideas are used together in
every circuit calculation.

</details>
</details>
</details>
