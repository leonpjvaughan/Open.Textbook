# 14.1 Current, potential difference and resistance — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. What is the unit of charge, and what is the charge on a single electron?
2. A force of 12 N pushes a box 3.0 m along a floor. Calculate the work done.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The coulomb, C. The charge on an electron is $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C, and it is
   negative. You met this in Chapter 13.
2. $W = Fd = 12 \times 3.0 = 36\,\text{J}$. Work is energy transferred, and energy is
   measured in joules. That idea returns in this section: a potential difference is an
   energy per unit charge, so it links joules to coulombs.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A current of 0.60 A flows through a wire for 20 s. Calculate the charge that
passes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You are given a current and a time, and asked for a charge. Which form of
$I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$ do you need?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Multiply: $Q = It = 0.60 \times 20$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$Q = It = 0.60 \times 20 = 12\,\text{C}$$

The unit checks out: amperes multiplied by seconds give coulombs.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A charge of 150 C passes a point in a wire in 2.5 minutes. Calculate the current.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The time must be in seconds before you use $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$2.5 \times 60 = 150$ s, so $I = \dfrac{150}{150}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{Q}{t} = \frac{150}{150} = 1.0\,\text{A}$$

If you wrote 60 A, you divided by 2.5 instead of by 150. The ampere is a coulomb per
second, so the time must always be in seconds.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A potential difference of 9.0 V transfers 45 J to a component. Calculate the
charge that passed through it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $V = \dfrac{W}{Q}$ for $Q$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$Q = \dfrac{W}{V} = \dfrac{45}{9.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$Q = \frac{W}{V} = \frac{45}{9.0} = 5.0\,\text{C}$$

The volt is a joule per coulomb, so dividing joules by volts gives coulombs.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A potential difference of 6.0 V drives a current of 0.20 A through a resistor.
Calculate its resistance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Resistance is the potential difference divided by the current.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$R = \dfrac{V}{I} = \dfrac{6.0}{0.20}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{6.0}{0.20} = 30\,\Omega$$

Check the sense of it: a small current of 0.20 A from a fairly large 6.0 V means a large
resistance, and 30 Ω is large compared with the resistors in Section 14.2.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A resistor of resistance 40 Ω is connected to a 12 V supply. Calculate the current
through it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Now you want the current, so rearrange $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$ for $I$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{12}{40}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{12}{40} = 0.30\,\text{A}$$

A larger resistance would give a smaller current for the same 12 V, and 40 Ω is larger
than the resistor in question 4, so the current is smaller.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A current of 0.40 A flows through a heater for 90 s. The potential difference
across the heater is 4.5 V. Calculate the charge that passes through it and the energy
transferred.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Two steps. First find the charge from the current and the time, then use the charge with
the potential difference.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$Q = It = 0.40 \times 90 = 36$ C. Then $W = VQ = 4.5 \times 36$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$Q = It = 0.40 \times 90 = 36\,\text{C}$$

$$W = VQ = 4.5 \times 36 = 162\,\text{J}$$

The energy is $1.6 \times 10^{2}$ J to two significant figures. Notice that you did not
need the resistance of the heater at all: the potential difference and the charge are
enough.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 4

**7.** A crane lifts a 250 kg load by 8.0 m. Calculate the energy transferred. Take
$g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The energy transferred is the work done against gravity, $W = mgh$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$W = 250 \times 9.8 \times 8.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$W = mgh = 250 \times 9.8 \times 8.0 = 2.0 \times 10^{4}\,\text{J}$$

This matters here because a potential difference is an energy per unit charge. A 12 V
battery transfers 12 J to each coulomb, and the crane transfers $2.0 \times 10^{4}$ J to
the load. Both are energies, measured in joules.

</details>
</details>
</details>
