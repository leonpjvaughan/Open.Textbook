# 14.1 Current, potential difference and resistance — Learn

**Prerequisites:** electric charge from Chapter 13; energy and work from Chapter 4.

**Objectives:** define electric current and use $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$; define potential
difference and use $V = \dfrac{W}{Q}$; define resistance and use $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$;
describe how an ammeter and a voltmeter are connected.

By the end of this section, you can use the three defining equations in either
direction and say what each quantity means.

## Charge and current

Electric charge comes in two kinds, positive and negative. Like charges repel and
opposite charges attract. Charge is measured in **coulombs** (C).

A battery pushes charge around a circuit. The rate at which charge flows past a point
is the **electric current**:

$$I = \frac{Q}{t}$$

- $I$ is the current, in amperes (A);
- $Q$ is the charge that passes, in coulombs (C);
- $t$ is the time, in seconds (s).

One ampere is one coulomb per second. That definition is worth remembering, because
it turns a question about charge into a question about current and time, and the other
way round.

In a metal wire the charge is carried by electrons, which are negative. The electrons
drift one way, and the conventional current is drawn the other way: from the positive
terminal of the cell, around the circuit, back to the negative terminal. Questions and
circuit diagrams always use conventional current unless they say otherwise.

**Example 1.** A current of 0.40 A flows through a lamp for 25 s. Calculate the charge
that passes through it.

$$
Q = It
$$

$$
Q = 0.40 \times 25
$$

$$
Q = 10\,\text{C}
$$

**Example 2.** A charge of 90 C passes a point in a wire in 1.5 minutes. Calculate the
current.

The time must be in seconds: $1.5 \times 60 = 90$ s.

$$
I = \frac{Q}{t}
$$

$$
I = \frac{90}{90}
$$

$$
I = 1.0\,\text{A}
$$

## Potential difference

Current does not flow on its own. Something has to push the charge, and the push comes
from a source of energy: a cell, a battery or a power supply.

The **potential difference** between two points is the energy transferred to the charge
as it moves between them, divided by the charge:

$$V = \frac{W}{Q}$$

- $V$ is the potential difference, in volts (V);
- $W$ is the energy transferred, in joules (J);
- $Q$ is the charge that passes, in coulombs (C).

One volt is one joule per coulomb. A potential difference of 6.0 V transfers 6.0 J to
each coulomb that passes. The word *across* goes with potential difference: a potential
difference is always between two points. The word *through* goes with current.

**Example 3.** A charge of 3.0 C passes through a component, and 18 J is transferred
to it. Calculate the potential difference across the component.

$$
V = \frac{W}{Q}
$$

$$
V = \frac{18}{3.0}
$$

$$
V = 6.0\,\text{V}
$$

## Resistance

Some components let charge through with little opposition; others hold it back. The
**resistance** of a
component is the potential difference across it divided by the current through it:

$$R = \frac{V}{I}$$

- $R$ is the resistance, in ohms (Ω);
- $V$ is the potential difference across the component, in volts (V);
- $I$ is the current through it, in amperes (A).

One ohm is one volt per ampere. A component with a large resistance needs a large
potential difference to drive a small current through it. When charge is pushed through
a resistor, energy is transferred to the resistor and it gets warm. That heating effect
is useful in a heater and a nuisance in a long cable.

**Example 4.** A potential difference of 4.5 V drives a current of 0.30 A through a
resistor. Calculate its resistance.

$$
R = \frac{V}{I}
$$

$$
R = \frac{4.5}{0.30}
$$

$$
R = 15\,\Omega
$$

## Measuring current and potential difference

Two instruments are used in almost every circuit experiment, and each is connected in
a different way.

- An **ammeter** measures current. It is connected **in series** with the component, so
  the current passes through it.
- A **voltmeter** measures potential difference. It is connected **in parallel** with
  the component, so it measures the difference between the two ends.

An ideal ammeter has zero resistance, so it does not reduce the current it is measuring.
An ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance, so it takes no current and does not change
the circuit. Real instruments are close to these ideals.

> **Watch out.** The three equations look similar, so it is worth checking which
> quantity is which. $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$ is not
> $R = \dfrac{I}{V}$, and a current is not a charge. If a question gives you a charge
> and a time, divide to get the current; if it gives you a current and a time, multiply
> to get the charge. Also remember that charge is not used up as it goes round a
> circuit: in a single loop the current is the same at every point.
