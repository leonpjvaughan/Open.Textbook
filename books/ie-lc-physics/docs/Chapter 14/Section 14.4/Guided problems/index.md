# 14.4 Electrical power and energy — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A 4.0 Ω resistor has a potential difference of 8.0 V across it. Calculate the current
   through it.
2. A force of 20 N moves an object 5.0 m in the direction of the force. Calculate the
   work done.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{8.0}{4.0} = 2.0\,\text{A}$. Ohm's law is used inside every
   power calculation, because the three power equations are rearrangements of $P = VI$
   with $V = IR$.
2. $W = Fd = 20 \times 5.0 = 100\,\text{J}$. Work is energy transferred, and power is
   the rate at which that energy is transferred. The two ideas are linked by
   $P = \dfrac{W}{t}$.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A 230 V supply drives a current of 0.20 A through an appliance. Calculate its
power.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You know the potential difference and the current, so use $P = VI$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$P = 230 \times 0.20$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$P = VI = 230 \times 0.20 = 46\,\text{W}$$

The appliance transfers 46 J every second.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A current of 0.50 A flows through a 40 Ω resistor. Calculate the power transferred
to it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You know the current and the resistance, so use $P = I^2R$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$P = 0.50^2 \times 40$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$P = I^2R = 0.50^2 \times 40 = 0.25 \times 40 = 10\,\text{W}$$

If you had used $P = VI$, you would have needed the potential difference first:
$V = IR = 0.50 \times 40 = 20$ V, and then $P = 20 \times 0.50 = 10$ W. Same answer.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A potential difference of 12 V is across an 8.0 Ω resistor. Calculate the power
transferred to it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You know the potential difference and the resistance, so use $P = \dfrac{V^2}{R}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$P = \dfrac{12^2}{8.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$P = \frac{V^2}{R} = \frac{144}{8.0} = 18\,\text{W}$$

This form is the quick one when the potential difference is known. In a parallel circuit
every branch has the same potential difference, so it is often the form to choose.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A 1.2 kW heater runs for 5.0 hours. Calculate the energy transferred, in
kilowatt-hours and in joules.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

In kilowatt-hours, use the power in kilowatts and the time in hours. In joules, convert
the time to seconds first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 1.2 \times 5.0$ kWh. For joules, $5.0 \times 3600 = 1.8 \times 10^{4}$ s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = Pt = 1.2 \times 5.0 = 6.0\,\text{kWh}$$

$$E = Pt = 1200 \times 1.8 \times 10^{4} = 2.16 \times 10^{7}\,\text{J}$$

The two answers describe the same energy. The kilowatt-hour is easier to read and is the
unit on an electricity bill.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** An appliance of power 0.80 kW runs for 6.0 hours. Electricity costs €0.30 per
kilowatt-hour. Calculate the cost.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the energy in kilowatt-hours first, then multiply by the price of one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 0.80 \times 6.0 = 4.8$ kWh, then multiply by €0.30.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = Pt = 0.80 \times 6.0 = 4.8\,\text{kWh}$$

$$\text{cost} = 4.8 \times 0.30 = 1.44$$

The cost is €1.44.

The price is per kilowatt-hour, so the energy must be in kilowatt-hours before you
multiply.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A 2.5 kW kettle is connected to a 230 V supply. Calculate the current it draws,
and choose a fuse from the ratings 3 A, 5 A and 13 A.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange $P = VI$ for the current, then pick the smallest rating above it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I = \dfrac{2500}{230}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{2500}{230} = 10.9\,\text{A}$$

The smallest standard rating above 10.9 A is 13 A, so a 13 A fuse is the right choice. A
5 A fuse would melt in normal use.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 8

**7.** A 2.0 kg block of aluminium is heated from 20 °C to 70 °C. The specific heat
capacity of aluminium is $900\,\text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$. Calculate the energy
transferred to the block.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E = mc\Delta\theta$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The temperature rise is $70 - 20 = 50$ K, so $E = 2.0 \times 900 \times 50$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = mc\Delta\theta = 2.0 \times 900 \times 50 = 9.0 \times 10^{4}\,\text{J}$$

where $m$ is the mass, $c$ the specific heat capacity and $\Delta\theta$ the
temperature rise.

This matters here because an electric heater transfers energy at a rate you can now
calculate. A 1.5 kW heater would supply $9.0 \times 10^{4}$ J in
$\dfrac{9.0 \times 10^{4}}{1500} = 60$ s, if all the energy reached the block.

</details>
</details>
</details>
