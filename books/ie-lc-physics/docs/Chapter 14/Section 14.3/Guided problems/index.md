# 14.3 Series and parallel circuits — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. State Ohm's law and write down the equation that goes with it.
2. A 12 Ω resistor has a potential difference of 6.0 V across it. Calculate the current
   through it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The current through a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential
   difference across it, provided its temperature stays constant. The equation is
   $V = IR$.
2. $I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{6.0}{12} = 0.50\,\text{A}$. Every branch of a parallel
   circuit in this section is worked out with this same equation.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** An 8.0 Ω resistor and a 4.0 Ω resistor are connected in series. Calculate the
combined resistance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

In series the resistances add directly.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$R = 8.0 + 4.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$R = R_1 + R_2 = 8.0 + 4.0 = 12\,\Omega$$

The combined resistance is larger than either resistor, because the charge has to get
through both of them on the single path.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Two 8.0 Ω resistors are connected in parallel. Calculate the combined resistance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{8.0} + \dfrac{1}{8.0} = \dfrac{2}{8.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{8.0} + \frac{1}{8.0} = \frac{2}{8.0} = \frac{1}{4.0}$$

$$R = 4.0\,\Omega$$

Two identical resistors in parallel give half the resistance of one of them. The answer
is smaller than either resistor, as it must be.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A 12 Ω resistor and a 4.0 Ω resistor are connected in parallel. Calculate the
combined resistance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Put both fractions over a common denominator of 12.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{12} + \dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{4}{12}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{4.0} = \frac{1}{12} + \frac{3}{12} = \frac{4}{12} = \frac{1}{3.0}$$

$$R = 3.0\,\Omega$$

Check it against the smallest resistor, 4.0 Ω: the combined resistance is smaller, which
is the quick test for a parallel answer.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A 6.0 V supply is connected to a 2.0 Ω resistor and a 4.0 Ω resistor in series.
Calculate the current and the potential difference across each resistor.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the combined resistance first, then use Ohm's law to find the current. The current
is the same through both resistors.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$R = 2.0 + 4.0 = 6.0\,\Omega$, so $I = \dfrac{6.0}{6.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{6.0}{6.0} = 1.0\,\text{A}$$

$$V_1 = IR_1 = 1.0 \times 2.0 = 2.0\,\text{V}$$

$$V_2 = IR_2 = 1.0 \times 4.0 = 4.0\,\text{V}$$

The two potential differences add to 6.0 V, which is the supply. The larger resistor
takes the larger share, and the current is the same through both.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A 6.0 V supply is connected to a 3.0 Ω resistor and a 6.0 Ω resistor in parallel.
Calculate the current in each branch and the total current from the supply.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The potential difference across each branch is the full 6.0 V.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$I_1 = \dfrac{6.0}{3.0}$ and $I_2 = \dfrac{6.0}{6.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$I_1 = \frac{V}{R_1} = \frac{6.0}{3.0} = 2.0\,\text{A}$$

$$I_2 = \frac{V}{R_2} = \frac{6.0}{6.0} = 1.0\,\text{A}$$

The total current is $2.0 + 1.0 = 3.0$ A. Check it with the combined resistance: the
3.0 Ω and 6.0 Ω in parallel give 2.0 Ω, and $\dfrac{6.0}{2.0} = 3.0$ A.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain why the combined resistance of two resistors in parallel is smaller than
either resistor on its own.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the number of routes the charge can take.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Two branches give the charge two paths instead of one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

In parallel, each resistor is a separate path between the same two points. Two paths
carry more current than one path for the same potential difference, and more current for
the same potential difference means less resistance. So the combined resistance is
smaller than either resistor alone.

That is also why a parallel branch added to a circuit increases the total current from
the supply, which is what the fuse has to carry.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 14.2

**7.** A heater draws a current of 3.0 A when the potential difference across it is
12 V. Calculate its resistance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use Ohm's law, $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$R = \dfrac{12}{3.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{12}{3.0} = 4.0\,\Omega$$

This is the same heater that appears in the chapter problem. Once you know its
resistance, you can treat it as one branch of a parallel circuit, which is what the next
questions in the chapter do.

</details>
</details>
</details>
