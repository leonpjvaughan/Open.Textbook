# Chapter 14: Electric circuits

A circuit is a path for charge. Push charge around that path and it can light a lamp,
heat a room or run a motor. In this chapter you find the rules that connect the push,
the flow and the opposition — and you use them to explain why a lamp dims when
something else switches on.

## The chapter problem

**The battery that is not 12 V.** A caravan has a 12 V battery. Three appliances are
connected to it at the same time: a lamp of resistance 12 Ω, a second lamp of
resistance 6.0 Ω, and a heater rated at 36 W when the potential difference across it
is 12 V. The three are connected in parallel.

The battery is old. It has its own internal resistance, and that is the reason the
lamps are dimmer than the label on the battery promises.

These are the facts to use all the way through the chapter.

- the battery is labelled **12 V**;
- the first lamp has a resistance of **12 Ω**;
- the second lamp has a resistance of **6.0 Ω**;
- the heater is rated at **36 W** when the potential difference across it is **12 V**.

<!-- tier:h -->
The internal resistance of the battery is **0.50 Ω**.
<!-- /tier:h -->

By the end of the chapter you can find the current in each appliance, the combined
resistance of the three in parallel, the total current from the battery, the power of
each appliance and the energy used in a three-hour evening. You can also explain why
the lamps dim when the heater switches on.

<!-- tier:h -->
You can also work out how much the terminal potential difference falls.
<!-- /tier:h -->

## By the end of this chapter you can

- define electric current, potential difference and resistance, and use $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$,
  $V = \dfrac{W}{Q}$ and $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$;
- state Ohm's law and use $V = IR$ to find a current, a potential difference or a
  resistance;
- describe the current-potential difference graphs of an ohmic conductor, a filament
  lamp and a diode, and explain what makes a conductor non-ohmic;
- use the rules for current, potential difference and resistance in series and in
  parallel circuits;
- use $P = VI$, $P = I^2R$ and $P = \dfrac{V^2}{R}$ to find a power, and $E = Pt$ to
  find an energy;
- calculate the cost of electrical energy in kilowatt-hours, and choose the right fuse
  for an appliance;
<!-- tier:h -->
- define electromotive force and internal resistance, and use $V = E - Ir$ to find the
  terminal potential difference.
<!-- /tier:h -->

## Where it is used

Every home is wired in parallel, and every circuit in it is protected by a fuse or a
circuit breaker. Car electrics, phone chargers and power tools all follow the same
rules. The national grid moves energy at a high potential difference for the same
reason that a battery has internal resistance: to keep the energy that is wasted as
small as possible. Chapter 15 takes the next step, and shows what a current does to a
magnetic field.
