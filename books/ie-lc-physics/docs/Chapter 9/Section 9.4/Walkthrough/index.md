# 9.4 The principle of superposition — Learn

**Prerequisites:** what a wave is, from Section 9.1; transverse and longitudinal waves,
from Section 9.3.

**Objectives:** state the principle of superposition; calculate the resultant
displacement where two waves meet; explain constructive and destructive superposition;
describe what happens to two pulses after they have passed through each other.

By the end of this section, you can work out what two waves do when they meet.

## Two waves in the same place

Send two pulses along a rope from opposite ends and watch what happens where they meet.
Each pulse is a wave in its own right, and at the meeting point the rope has only one
displacement. The two displacements combine.

> **The principle of superposition.** Where two or more waves meet, the resultant
> displacement at a point is the sum of the displacements of the individual waves.

The rule is an addition, and the displacements can be positive or negative. Add them
with their signs.

**Example 1.** Two pulses meet on a rope. One has a displacement of $+3.0\,\text{cm}$
and the other has a displacement of $+5.0\,\text{cm}$. Calculate the resultant
displacement where they meet.

Both displacements point the same way, so they add:

$$3.0 + 5.0 = 8.0\,\text{cm}$$

**Example 2.** Two pulses meet on a rope. One has a displacement of
$+6.0\,\text{cm}$ and the other has a displacement of $-2.0\,\text{cm}$. Calculate the
resultant displacement.

The displacements point in opposite directions, so the smaller one takes away from the
larger:

$$6.0 + (-2.0) = 4.0\,\text{cm}$$

**Example 3.** Two pulses meet on a rope. One has a displacement of
$+4.0\,\text{cm}$ and the other has a displacement of $-4.0\,\text{cm}$. Calculate the
resultant displacement.

$$4.0 + (-4.0) = 0\,\text{cm}$$

At that instant the rope is straight, even though both pulses are still travelling along
it.

## Constructive and destructive superposition

Two waves can meet in step or out of step, and the names describe the result.

- **Constructive superposition** happens when the two displacements point the same way.
  They add, and the resultant has a larger amplitude than either wave on its own.
- **Destructive superposition** happens when the two displacements point opposite ways.
  They take away from each other, and the resultant has a smaller amplitude. If the two
  are equal and opposite, the resultant is zero at that instant.

Sound waves superpose in the same way. Two loudspeakers playing the same note are louder
at the points where the two sound waves arrive in step. Noise-cancelling headphones
detect the noise around you and play a sound wave that is out of step with it, so the two
cancel where they reach your ear.

## After the waves have crossed

When two pulses have passed through each other, each one continues with the same shape,
the same speed and the same direction as before. The meeting changes the rope only while
the pulses overlap. After that, each pulse behaves as if the other had never been there.

This is worth checking for yourself. Send two pulses towards each other along a rope and
watch them come out the other side.

> **Watch out.** Waves pass through each other; they do not bounce off each other and
> they do not stop. Another trap is to add only the sizes and to forget the signs. A
> displacement of $+6.0\,\text{cm}$ and one of $-2.0\,\text{cm}$ give
> $+4.0\,\text{cm}$, not $+8.0\,\text{cm}$.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. Two pulses travel towards each other along the guitar
string. One pulse has a displacement of $+2.0\,\text{mm}$ and the other has a
displacement of $-2.0\,\text{mm}$.

1. Calculate the resultant displacement where they meet.
2. Describe what the string looks like at that instant.
3. Describe what each pulse does after they have passed through each other.

Keep your answers. Section 9.5 shows how two waves travelling along the string in
opposite directions can produce a stationary wave, which is how the string sounds a note.

## You should now be able to

- state the principle of superposition;
- calculate the resultant displacement where two waves meet, keeping the signs;
- explain the difference between constructive and destructive superposition;
- describe what happens to two pulses after they have passed through each other;
- give one everyday example of superposition.
