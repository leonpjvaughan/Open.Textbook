# 9.4 The principle of superposition — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A wave has an amplitude of 0.040 m. State the distance from a crest to the next
   trough.
2. A wave travels at 12 m/s and has a wavelength of 3.0 m. Calculate its frequency.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. The crest is 0.040 m above the rest position and the trough is 0.040 m below it, so
   the distance is $2 \times 0.040 = 0.080\,\text{m}$.
2. $f = \dfrac{v}{\lambda} = \dfrac{12}{3.0} = 4.0\,\text{Hz}$.

The first answer is the displacement that will be added to another displacement in this
section. The second one keeps the wave equation fresh, because a wave that superposes
still has a frequency and a wavelength.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Two pulses meet on a rope. One has a displacement of $+2.0\,\text{cm}$ and the
other has a displacement of $+6.0\,\text{cm}$. Calculate the resultant displacement
where they meet.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Both displacements point the same way, so add them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$2.0 + 6.0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$2.0 + 6.0 = 8.0\,\text{cm}$$

The two displacements add because they point the same way. This is constructive
superposition, and the resultant is larger than either pulse on its own.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Two pulses meet on a rope. One has a displacement of $+5.0\,\text{cm}$ and the
other has a displacement of $-3.0\,\text{cm}$. Calculate the resultant displacement.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Keep the signs when you add.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$5.0 + (-3.0)$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$5.0 + (-3.0) = 2.0\,\text{cm}$$

The two displacements point in opposite directions, so the smaller one takes away from
the larger. The result is positive, so the resultant displacement points the same way as
the larger pulse.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Two pulses meet on a rope. One has a displacement of $+4.0\,\text{cm}$ and the
other has a displacement of $-4.0\,\text{cm}$. Calculate the resultant displacement.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The two displacements are equal and opposite.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$4.0 + (-4.0)$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$4.0 + (-4.0) = 0\,\text{cm}$$

At that instant the rope is straight. This is destructive superposition. Both pulses are
still travelling along the rope, and they reappear after they have passed through each
other.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** State the principle of superposition.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The word *superposition* means placing one thing on top of another.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

What happens to the two displacements at the point where the waves meet?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Where two or more waves meet, the resultant displacement at a point is the sum of the
displacements of the individual waves. The sum keeps the sign of each displacement, so a
positive displacement and a negative one partly cancel.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Explain the difference between constructive superposition and destructive
superposition.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One adds the displacements and one takes them away.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Describe the amplitude of the resultant in each case.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

In constructive superposition the two displacements point the same way. They add, and the
resultant has a larger amplitude than either wave on its own. In destructive
superposition the two displacements point in opposite directions. They take away from
each other, and the resultant has a smaller amplitude. If the two are equal and opposite,
the resultant is zero at that instant.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Two pulses travel towards each other along a rope and pass through each other.
Describe what happens to each pulse after they have crossed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Does a pulse change shape when it meets another pulse?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Think about the shape, the speed and the direction of each pulse.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

After passing through each other, each pulse continues with the same shape, the same
speed and the same direction as before. The meeting changes the rope only while the
pulses overlap. After that, each pulse behaves as if the other pulse had never been
there. The pulses do not bounce off each other and they do not change shape permanently.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** Two loudspeakers play the same note. At one point in the room the two sound waves
arrive in step, and at another point they arrive exactly out of step. Describe what a
listener hears at each point, and name the effect.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

In step means the two displacements point the same way.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Use the two names for superposition.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Where the waves arrive in step, their displacements add and the sound is loud. This is
constructive superposition. Where they arrive exactly out of step, their displacements
cancel and the sound is quiet. This is destructive superposition. A listener walking
across the room hears the loudness rise and fall, which is called an interference
pattern.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 2

**8.** A cyclist travels 60 m in 5.0 s. Calculate her average speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Average speed is distance divided by time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$v = \dfrac{60}{5.0}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$v = \frac{s}{t} = \frac{60}{5.0} = 12\,\text{m/s}$$

A wave speed is the same kind of quantity, and $v = f\lambda$ is another way to find it.
The cyclist and the wave both travel at a steady speed, and both speeds are measured in
metres per second.

</details>
</details>
</details>
