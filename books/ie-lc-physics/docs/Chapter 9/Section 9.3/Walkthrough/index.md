# 9.3 Transverse and longitudinal waves — Learn

**Prerequisites:** what a wave is, from Section 9.1; the wave equation, from
Section 9.2.

**Objectives:** describe a transverse wave and a longitudinal wave; give an example of
each; explain what the particles of the medium do in each; explain why only a transverse
wave can be polarised.

By the end of this section, you can classify any wave by the direction in which its
particles vibrate.

## Two directions of vibration

Every wave carries energy in one direction. The particles of the medium vibrate as the
energy passes, and there are only two ways the vibration can point.

- In a **transverse wave**, the particles vibrate at right angles to the direction the
  wave travels.
- In a **longitudinal wave**, the particles vibrate along the direction the wave travels.

The direction of the vibration is what separates the two families. The wave direction is
the same in both.

## Transverse waves

Ripple a rope up and down and the bump travels along the rope while the rope moves up
and down. The rope moves at right angles to the direction the bump travels, so this is a
transverse wave.

A transverse wave has crests and troughs. Examples include:

- a wave on a string or a rope;
- a wave on the surface of water, which is mostly transverse;
- every electromagnetic wave, including light, radio waves and X-rays.

Because the vibration has a direction at right angles to the wave, a transverse wave can
be **polarised**. A polarising filter selects one direction of vibration and blocks the
other. The next chapter uses polarisation, so remember that it belongs to transverse
waves.

## Longitudinal waves

Push and pull the end of a stretched spring and the coils bunch together and spread
apart. Each coil vibrates back and forth along the spring, in the same direction as the
disturbance travels. That is a longitudinal wave.

A longitudinal wave has compressions and rarefactions instead of crests and troughs.

- A **compression** is a region where the particles are closer together than usual.
- A **rarefaction** is a region where the particles are farther apart than usual.

Sound is a longitudinal wave. Ultrasound is a longitudinal wave. A sound wave travels
through the air as a compression followed by a rarefaction, then another compression.

## Try it: the spring

**Predict.** Stretch a spring or a Slinky along a table with a partner holding the far
end. You will push your end quickly forwards and then pull it back, so that a single
pulse travels along the spring. Before you start, write down which way you expect the
coils to move: along the spring, or across it?

**Test.** Make the pulse and watch the coils as it passes. Then send a pulse by moving
your end sideways instead, and watch the coils again.

**Explain.** Write two or three sentences. Say which pulse was longitudinal and which
was transverse, and describe the direction in which the coils moved in each case.

## Comparing the two

| | Transverse wave | Longitudinal wave |
|---|---|---|
| Direction of vibration | at right angles to the wave | along the wave |
| Features | crests and troughs | compressions and rarefactions |
| Examples | light, a wave on a string, a water wave | sound, ultrasound |
| Can be polarised? | yes | no |

Only a transverse wave can be polarised, because its vibration has a direction at right
angles to the wave that a filter can select. A longitudinal wave vibrates along the wave,
so there is no such direction to select.

> **Watch out.** Sound is a longitudinal wave. It is tempting to draw a sound wave as a
> wavy line with crests and troughs, because that is how a wave is usually drawn. A wavy
> line is a picture of a transverse wave. For sound, the correct picture shows bands of
> compressed air and spread-out air travelling away from the source.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The guitar string sounds a note that travels through the air
to your ear.

1. State whether the wave on the string is transverse or longitudinal.
2. State whether the sound wave in the air is transverse or longitudinal.
3. Describe the direction in which the particles vibrate in each of the two waves.

Keep your answers. Section 9.4 uses the wave on the string to show what happens when two
waves meet.

## You should now be able to

- describe a transverse wave and a longitudinal wave, and say how they differ;
- give two examples of each kind;
- describe what the particles of the medium do in each kind;
- state that sound and ultrasound are longitudinal, and that light is transverse;
- explain why only a transverse wave can be polarised;
- name a compression and a rarefaction, and say which kind of wave they belong to.
