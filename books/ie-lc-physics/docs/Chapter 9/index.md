# Chapter 9: Waves

Pluck a guitar string and something travels to your ear, even though the string stays
where it is. A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from one place to another
without carrying the material with it. The same idea describes sound, light, water waves
and the radio signal reaching your phone.

## The chapter problem

**The guitar string.** A guitar's G string is fixed at both ends and is 0.650 m long.
When it is plucked it sounds a note of frequency 196 Hz. Waves travel along the string
at 255 m/s, and the speed of sound in air is 340 m/s.

By the end of the chapter you can say what travels along the string and what stays in
place, find the wavelength and the speed of the wave on the string, work out the
wavelength of the sound in the air, and explain why the string sounds 196 Hz and where
its nodes and antinodes are. You can also work out what happens when two pulses meet on
the string, and why the string also sounds at 392 Hz.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- explain how a wave carries energy from one place to another without carrying matter
  with it;
- name the rest position, displacement, crest, trough and amplitude of a wave;
- say how the energy carried by a wave depends on its amplitude;
- use $v = f\lambda$ to find a wave speed, a frequency or a wavelength;
- tell a transverse wave from a longitudinal wave, and say what the particles of the
  medium do in each;
- use the principle of superposition to find the resultant displacement where two waves
  meet;
- explain how a stationary wave forms on a stretched string, and name its nodes,
  antinodes, harmonics and fundamental frequency;
- explain resonance, and use the fundamental-frequency relationship for a stretched
  string.

## Where it is used

Every musical instrument, and every voice. Ultrasound scans, sonar and seismic surveys.
Radio, television, wi-fi and mobile phone signals. Noise-cancelling headphones, which
use superposition to cancel sound. The Physics In Practice Investigation, whenever you
measure a wave.
