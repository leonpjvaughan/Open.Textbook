# 9.5 Stationary waves and resonance — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the wave equation from Section 9.2; the principle of superposition
from Section 9.4.

**Objectives:** describe how a stationary wave forms on a stretched string; define a
node, an antinode, the fundamental frequency and a harmonic; relate the length of a
string to the wavelength of the fundamental; use
$f = \dfrac{1}{2l}\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$; explain resonance.

By the end of this section, you can explain why a guitar string sounds a particular note
and where its nodes and antinodes are.

## Two waves on one string

A guitar string is fixed at both ends. Pluck it and a wave travels along the string.
When the wave reaches a fixed end, it reflects and travels back. So there are now two
waves on the string, with the same frequency, travelling in opposite directions.

By the principle of superposition, the two waves add everywhere they meet. Along most of
the string the two displacements are constantly changing, but at certain points the two
waves always cancel. At those points the string never moves. The pattern that results is
a **stationary wave**, also called a standing wave.

A stationary wave is not travelling anywhere. Its crests and troughs stay in the same
places, and only the size of the displacement changes.

## Nodes and antinodes

- A **node** is a point that stays at the rest position. The displacement there is always
  zero.
- An **antinode** is a point where the displacement is largest.

The ends of the string are fixed, so they cannot move. Each end is a node. Between them
the string can move, and the simplest pattern has a single antinode in the middle. That
pattern is the **fundamental** vibration.

## The fundamental and the harmonics

Half a wavelength fits between two neighbouring nodes. For the fundamental, the two nodes
are the ends of the string, so the length of the string is half a wavelength:

$$L = \frac{\lambda}{2} \qquad \lambda = 2L$$

A **harmonic** is a vibration whose frequency is a whole-number multiple of the
fundamental frequency $f_1$. Higher harmonics fit more half-wavelengths on the same
string:

$$L = n\frac{\lambda}{2} \qquad \lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n} \qquad f_n = n f_1$$

where $n = 1$ gives the fundamental, $n = 2$ the second harmonic, and so on. The
**first overtone** is the second harmonic.

**Example 1.** A guitar string is 0.650 m long and fixed at both ends. Calculate the
wavelength of its fundamental note.

$$\lambda = 2L = 2 \times 0.650 = 1.30\,\text{m}$$

**Example 2.** The fundamental frequency of the string is 196 Hz. Calculate the frequency
of the second harmonic.

$$f_2 = 2f_1 = 2 \times 196 = 392\,\text{Hz}$$

The second harmonic has twice the frequency and half the wavelength of the fundamental.

## The frequency of a stretched string

The speed of a wave on a string depends on how tight the string is and how heavy it is
for its length. That gives the frequency of the fundamental:

> **Fundamental frequency of a stretched string.**
> $$f = \frac{1}{2l}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$$
> - $f$ is the fundamental frequency, in hertz (Hz);
> - $l$ is the length of the string, in metres (m);
> - $T$ is the tension in the string, in newtons (N);
> - $\mu$ is the mass per unit length of the string, in kilograms per metre (kg/m).

A tighter string has a higher note, because $f$ increases with $T$. A longer or heavier
string has a lower note, because $f$ decreases as $l$ or $\mu$ increases. A guitarist
tunes by turning the tuning peg, which changes the tension.

**Example 3.** A stretched string is 0.50 m long. Its tension is 500 N and its mass per
unit length is 0.0080 kg/m. Calculate its fundamental frequency.

$$\frac{T}{\mu} = \frac{500}{0.0080} = 62500$$

$$\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = \sqrt{62500} = 250\,\text{m/s}$$

That is the speed of the wave on the string. Then

$$f = \frac{250}{2 \times 0.50} = 250\,\text{Hz}$$

## Resonance

Every object has natural frequencies at which it vibrates most strongly. A stretched string has
the harmonics $f_1$, $2f_1$, $3f_1$, and so on.

> **Resonance.** Resonance is the large amplitude of vibration that happens when the
> driving frequency equals a natural frequency of the object.

Pluck the guitar string and it vibrates at its own natural frequencies. Drive the string
with a loudspeaker at 196 Hz and it vibrates strongly, because that is its fundamental
frequency. Drive it at 200 Hz and it hardly responds. A swing behaves in the same way:
push it at its natural rate and it goes higher, and push it at any other rate and it
fights you.

## Try it: loops on a rope

**Predict.** Tie a long rope to a door handle. Hold the free end and shake it gently
sideways until one large loop forms along the rope. Now predict what you will see when
you shake it twice as fast. How many loops will there be?

**Test.** Double the rate at which you shake the rope and count the loops. Try three
times the rate as well.

**Explain.** Write two or three sentences. Say how the number of loops changed when you
changed the rate, and say where the rope stayed still. Those still points are nodes, and
the pattern is a stationary wave.

> **Watch out.** The speed of the wave on the string is set by the tension and the mass
> per unit length, not by the frequency. When you change the frequency, the wavelength
> changes so that $v = f\lambda$ still holds. A second trap is to think a node is a point
> where the wave has stopped. The wave is still travelling in both directions through the
> node; the two displacements cancel there at every instant.

- define the fundamental frequency and a harmonic, and use $f_n = n f_1$;
- use $\lambda = \dfrac{2L}{n}$ to find the wavelength of a harmonic;
- use $f = \dfrac{1}{2l}\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$ to find the fundamental frequency of a
