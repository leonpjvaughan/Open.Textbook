# 9.5 Stationary waves and resonance — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. State the principle of superposition.
2. A wave on a string has a frequency of 196 Hz and a wavelength of 1.30 m. Calculate its
   speed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. Where two or more waves meet, the resultant displacement at a point is the sum of the
   displacements of the individual waves.
2. $v = f\lambda = 196 \times 1.30 = 255\,\text{m/s}$.

The first answer is the rule that makes a stationary wave possible: two waves travelling
in opposite directions add where they meet. The second answer is the speed of the wave on
the guitar string.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A string is fixed at both ends and is 0.80 m long. Calculate the wavelength of its
fundamental note.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Half a wavelength fits between the two fixed ends.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\lambda = 2L = 2 \times 0.80$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = 2L = 2 \times 0.80 = 1.6\,\text{m}$$

The fundamental has a node at each end and one antinode in the middle, so the string
holds half a wavelength. The wavelength is therefore twice the length of the string.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** The fundamental frequency of a string is 150 Hz. Calculate the frequency of the
third harmonic.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Each harmonic is a whole-number multiple of the fundamental.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$f_3 = 3f_1 = 3 \times 150$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$f_3 = 3f_1 = 3 \times 150 = 450\,\text{Hz}$$

The third harmonic has three times the frequency of the fundamental, and its wavelength
is a third of the fundamental wavelength.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A string of length 0.60 m sounds its second harmonic. Calculate the wavelength of
this standing wave.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

For the second harmonic, $n = 2$. Use $\lambda = \dfrac{2L}{n}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\lambda = \dfrac{2 \times 0.60}{2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = \frac{2L}{n} = \frac{2 \times 0.60}{2} = 0.60\,\text{m}$$

The second harmonic fits two half-wavelengths on the string, so the wavelength equals the
length of the string. It has a node at each end and one more node in the middle.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A string is 0.50 m long. Its tension is 800 N and its mass per unit length is
0.0050 kg/m. Calculate the fundamental frequency of the string.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the wave speed from $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$ first, then use
$f = \dfrac{v}{2l}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{T}{\mu} = \dfrac{800}{0.0050} = 160000$, so $v = 400\,\text{m/s}$. Then
$f = \dfrac{400}{2 \times 0.50}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{800}{0.0050}} = \sqrt{160000} = 400\,\text{m/s}$$

$$f = \frac{v}{2l} = \frac{400}{2 \times 0.50} = 400\,\text{Hz}$$

The unit check: $\sqrt{\dfrac{\text{N}}{\text{kg/m}}} =
\sqrt{\dfrac{\text{kg m/s}^{2}}{\text{kg/m}}} = \sqrt{\text{m}^{2}/\text{s}^{2}} =
\text{m/s}$, which is a speed. Correct.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A string is 0.40 m long and its fundamental frequency is 300 Hz. Calculate the
speed of the wave on the string.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The fundamental wavelength is twice the length of the string.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\lambda = 2 \times 0.40 = 0.80\,\text{m}$, then $v = f\lambda$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\lambda = 2L = 2 \times 0.40 = 0.80\,\text{m}$$

$$v = f\lambda = 300 \times 0.80 = 240\,\text{m/s}$$

The wave travels 240 m every second along the string, even though the string itself does
not travel anywhere.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Explain why the note from a guitar string rises in pitch when the string is
tightened.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $f = \dfrac{1}{2l}\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$ and decide which quantity changes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Tightening the string increases the tension $T$. The length $l$ and the mass per unit
length $\mu$ do not change.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The fundamental frequency of the string is
$f = \dfrac{1}{2l}\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$. Tightening the string increases the tension
$T$, and the length $l$ and the mass per unit length $\mu$ stay the same. A larger $T$
makes $\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$ larger, so $f$ increases. A higher frequency is heard as a
higher pitch.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** Explain what resonance is, and give one example of it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about a swing, and about the rate at which you push it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Name the two frequencies that must match.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Resonance is the large amplitude of vibration that happens when the driving frequency
equals a natural frequency of the object. A guitar string sounds loudly when it is
plucked at its natural frequency, which is 196 Hz for the G string. A swing goes higher
when it is pushed at its natural frequency, and it fights you when you push at any other
rate.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 5

**8.** A spring has a spring constant of 80 N/m and is stretched by 0.10 m. Calculate the
energy stored in the spring.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

$E = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$. Square the extension first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$E = 0.5 \times 80 \times 0.10^2 = 0.5 \times 80 \times 0.010$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = 0.5 \times 80 \times 0.010 = 0.40\,\text{J}$$

A stretched string stores energy in the same way as a spring, because a stretched string
is under tension. That stored energy is what the string gives to the air when it is
plucked.

</details>
</details>
</details>
