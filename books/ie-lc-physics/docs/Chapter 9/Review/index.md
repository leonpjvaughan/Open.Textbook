# Chapter 9: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The guitar string.** A guitar's G string is fixed at both ends and is 0.650 m long. It
sounds a note of 196 Hz. Waves travel along the string at 255 m/s, and the speed of sound
in air is 340 m/s.

In these formulas $L$ is the length of the string, $\lambda$ a wavelength, $v$ a wave
speed and $f$ a frequency. $f_1$ is the fundamental frequency and $f_2$ the second
harmonic.

**Step 1: what travels, and what stays.** Energy travels from the string, through the air,
to your ear. The string and the air stay in place. Their particles vibrate about fixed
positions and pass the disturbance on to their neighbours.

**Step 2: the wave on the string.** The string is fixed at both ends and sounds its
fundamental note, so the two ends are nodes and half a wavelength fits on the string:

$$L = \frac{\lambda}{2} \qquad \lambda = 2L = 2 \times 0.650 = 1.30\,\text{m}$$

The speed of the wave follows from the wave equation:

$$v = f\lambda = 196 \times 1.30 = 254.8\,\text{m/s}$$

To three significant figures that is $255\,\text{m/s}$, which is the value the problem
gives.

**Step 3: the two kinds of wave.** The wave on the string is transverse. The particles of
the string vibrate at right angles to the direction the wave travels. The sound wave in
the air is longitudinal. The air particles vibrate back and forth along the direction the
sound travels.

**Step 4: the sound in the air.** The frequency is set by the source, so the sound wave
also has a frequency of 196 Hz. The speed of sound in air is different, so the wavelength
is different:

$$\lambda_{\text{air}} = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{340}{196} = 1.7\,\text{m}$$

The two waves have the same frequency and different wavelengths. That is exactly what
$v = f\lambda$ requires when the speed changes and the frequency does not.

**Step 5: the nodes and the antinodes.** The fundamental standing wave has a node at each
fixed end and one antinode at the middle of the string. The distance between the two
nodes is half a wavelength, which is $1.30 \div 2 = 0.650\,\text{m}$. That is the length
of the string, so the pattern fits.

**Step 6: the second harmonic.** The harmonics are whole-number multiples of the
fundamental:

$$f_2 = 2f_1 = 2 \times 196 = 392\,\text{Hz}$$

Its wavelength is half the fundamental wavelength,
$\lambda_2 = 2L \div 2 = 0.650\,\text{m}$, so two half-wavelengths fit on the string.

**Step 7: resonance.** The string vibrates strongly when it is driven at 196 Hz, because
that is its fundamental frequency. It also responds at 392 Hz and at 588 Hz, which are
its second and third harmonics. Drive it at 200 Hz and it hardly responds, because
200 Hz is not one of its natural frequencies. A large amplitude at a driving frequency
that matches a natural frequency is resonance.

**The answer.** The wave on the string is transverse, with a wavelength of 1.30 m and a
speed of 255 m/s. The sound in the air is longitudinal, with the same frequency of 196 Hz
and a wavelength of 1.7 m. The fundamental standing wave has a node at each end and one
antinode in the middle, and the string also sounds at 392 Hz and 588 Hz. It vibrates
strongly at those frequencies because of resonance.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which relationship applies** and
say why. Two of them ask you to choose between the wave equation and the string
relationships, and two come from earlier chapters.

1. A wave has a frequency of 250 Hz and a wavelength of 1.2 m. Calculate its speed.
2. A wave on a rope has an amplitude of 0.15 m. State the distance from a crest to the
   next trough.
3. A sound wave has a frequency of 500 Hz and travels through air at 340 m/s. Calculate
   its wavelength.
4. A string fixed at both ends is 0.90 m long. Calculate the wavelength of its
   fundamental note.
5. The fundamental frequency of a string is 220 Hz. Calculate the frequency of the third
   harmonic.
6. Two pulses meet on a rope. One has a displacement of $+3.0\,\text{cm}$ and the other
   has a displacement of $-5.0\,\text{cm}$. Calculate the resultant displacement.
7. A string is 0.75 m long. Its tension is 300 N and its mass per unit length is
   0.012 kg/m. Calculate its fundamental frequency. Which relationship did you use first,
   and why?
8. A sound wave passes from air into water. Explain why its frequency does not change and
   why its wavelength does.
9. Explain the difference between a transverse wave and a longitudinal wave, and give one
   example of each.
10. A car starts from rest and accelerates at 2.5 m/s$^2$ for 6.0 s. Calculate its final
    speed and the distance it travels. Which chapter does this method come from?

## What you can now do

- explain how a wave carries energy without carrying matter;
- name the rest position, displacement, crest, trough and amplitude of a wave, and relate
  the amplitude to the energy carried;
- use $v = f\lambda$ and $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$ in any direction;
- explain why the frequency of a wave stays the same when it enters a new medium, while
  the speed and the wavelength change;
- tell a transverse wave from a longitudinal wave, and describe the vibration in each;
- use the principle of superposition, keeping the signs, to find a resultant displacement;
- explain constructive and destructive superposition, and give an example of each;
- describe how a stationary wave forms on a stretched string, and find its nodes and
  antinodes;
- use $\lambda = \dfrac{2L}{n}$ and $f_n = n f_1$ for the harmonics of a string;
- use $f = \dfrac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$ for the fundamental frequency of a
  stretched string;
- explain resonance, and say why a string responds strongly at its natural frequencies.

## Thinking back

Return to the wave you chose at the start of the chapter — the water wave, the rope, the
whip, the stadium wave, the curtain or the sound from a speaker.

You can now say exactly what travelled and what stayed in place. The energy travelled, and
the material did not. Write down the amplitude you can see, or estimate it. Then write
down the frequency, or estimate it, and use $v = f\lambda$ to work out the wave speed.
Compare your value with a speed you can check: the speed of sound in air is 340 m/s, and
the speed of a water wave in shallow water is a few metres per second.

If your numbers are rough estimates, write that down as well. Saying how rough an estimate
is belongs in the physics, and it is what the Physics In Practice Investigation asks you
to do.
