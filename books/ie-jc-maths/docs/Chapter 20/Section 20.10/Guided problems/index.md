# 20.10 Constructing a proof — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Work out $180-120$.
2. In the isosceles theorem, which sides are equal, and which angles does that
   make equal?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $60$. Subtracting from 180 is the last step of many proofs.
2. The two legs are equal, and the angles opposite them, the base angles, are
   equal. The converse works the other way round.

Both facts return here. A proof often ends with a subtraction from 180, and the
isosceles theorem is a common bridge from sides to angles.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A question says: "In the triangle $ABC$, $|AB|=|AC|$. Prove that
$|\angle B|=|\angle C|$." Write down the given and what is to be proved.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The given is what you are told. The goal is what you must show.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The given is $|AB|=|AC|$. The goal is $|\angle B|=|\angle C|$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

**Given:** triangle $ABC$ with $|AB|=|AC|$. **To prove:** $|\angle B|=|\angle C|$.
The given is the hypothesis, and the goal is the conclusion. Separating them is
the first step of every proof.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A proof reaches the equation $48+73+x=180$. Work out $x$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the two known angles, then subtract from 180.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$48+73=121$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$48+73=121$, and $180-121=59$. So $x=59°$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A proof contains the line: "therefore $|\angle ABC|=|\angle DAB|$." Which
theorem is the reason?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The two angles are alternate angles between two parallel lines.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Name the theorem about alternate angles.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The reason is the **alternate angles theorem**. The line through $A$ is parallel
to $BC$, and $AB$ is a transversal of the two parallel lines, so the alternate
angles are equal.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Write a proof that the base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare triangle $ABC$ with triangle $ACB$ and use SAS.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$|AB|=|AC|$, $|AC|=|AB|$, and the angle at $A$ is common.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Let $ABC$ have $|AB|=|AC|$. Then triangle $ABC$ is congruent to triangle $ACB$ by
SAS: $|AB|=|AC|$, $|AC|=|AB|$, and the angle at $A$ is the same angle in both.
Matching angles give $|\angle B|=|\angle C|$. The base angles are equal.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Prove that each exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two
interior opposite angles.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the straight line at the corner and the triangle angle sum.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Subtract the shared interior angle from both 180° sums.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Let $\alpha$ be the exterior angle at $A$. Then $|\alpha|+|\angle A|=180°$, because
the two angles are on a straight line. The triangle angle sum gives
$|\angle B|+|\angle C|+|\angle A|=180°$. Subtracting $|\angle A|$ from both sums
leaves $|\alpha|=|\angle B|+|\angle C|$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Put the proof of the triangle angle sum in order: (a) the three angles at
$A$ lie on a straight line, so they add to 180°; (b) draw the line through $A$
parallel to $BC$; (c) the alternate angles give the angle at $B$ equal to one
angle at $A$; (d) the alternate angles give the angle at $C$ equal to the other
angle at $A$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start by drawing the line, then move the angles, then use the straight line.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The order is b, then c and d, then a.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The order is **b, c, d, a**. Draw the parallel line through $A$. Use the alternate
angles with $AB$ and with $AC$ to move the angles at $B$ and $C$ up to $A$. The
three angles now at $A$ lie on a straight line, so they add to 180°. Therefore the
three angles of the triangle add to 180°.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** The statement "if a shape is a square, then it is a rectangle" is true.
Write the converse and say whether it is true.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Swap the hypothesis and the conclusion.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Look for a rectangle that is not a square.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The converse is: **if a shape is a rectangle, then it is a square.** It is false. A
rectangle 4 cm by 2 cm has four right angles but its sides are not all equal, so
it is not a square. One counterexample is enough.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** You must prove that the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect one another.
Write the first step of a proof plan and name the congruence test you expect to
use.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Draw a diagonal so that two triangles share it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Mark the alternate angles made by the parallel sides.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

First draw one diagonal, so the two triangles share it, and mark the alternate
angles made by the parallel sides. The two triangles have two pairs of equal
angles and the equal opposite sides of the parallelogram, so **ASA** is the test.
The matching sides then give the two halves of each diagonal as equal.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**9.** Explain why a proof must give a reason for each step, not only the steps.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A step follows from something already accepted.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Naming the reason is what makes the argument valid.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Steps on their own are not a proof. Each step must follow from an axiom, a
definition, or a theorem already proved. Naming that reason is what makes the
argument valid. It also lets a reader check the proof, because they can see which
result each step uses.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from an earlier section

**10.** A triangle has sides of 9 cm, 12 cm and 15 cm. Decide whether it is
right-angled.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the converse of Pythagoras.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Compare $9^2+12^2$ with $15^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$9^2+12^2=81+144=225$, and $15^2=225$. The values match, so the triangle is
right-angled.

The converse of Pythagoras from Section 20.8 is one of the bridges a proof can
use.

</details>
</details>
</details>
