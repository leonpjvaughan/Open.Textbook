# 20.11 Evaluating someone else's proof — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write the converse of "if a shape is a square, then it has four right angles."
2. Work out $15\div6$ and say what the remainder is.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. "If a shape has four right angles, then it is a square." The converse is a new
   statement, and it is false: a rectangle 4 cm by 2 cm is a counterexample.
2. $15\div6=2$ with a remainder of 3. A remainder that is not zero disproves a
   claim of divisibility.

Both skills return here. Judging a proof means checking a converse, and testing a
general claim means looking for a counterexample.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A proof of the triangle angle sum begins: "the angles add to 180° because
the angles in a triangle add to 180°." Name the fault and explain it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Does the proof use the result it is meant to prove?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

That is circular reasoning.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The fault is **circular reasoning**. The proof assumes the very result it is meant
to show, so it proves nothing. A proof must start from the axioms and from results
already proved.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A proof says: "from the diagram you can see the two angles are equal."
Explain why this is not a proof.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

How many cases does one diagram show?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A proof must hold for every case, not one picture.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A diagram shows one case, drawn one way. A proof must hold for every triangle, so
"you can see it" is not a reason. The equality has to follow from an axiom or from
a theorem already proved.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A proof writes $55+68+x=180$. Work out $x$, and say what the proof has not
yet shown.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Do the arithmetic, then ask why the sum is 180.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$55+68=123$, and $180-123=57$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$55+68=123$, and $180-123=57$. So $x=57°$. The arithmetic is right, but the proof
has not shown why the three angles add to 180°. It has used the triangle angle sum
without proving it.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A proof of the isosceles theorem says: "triangle $ABC$ is congruent to
triangle $ACB$ by SAS." What else must the proof state before this step is
complete?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

SAS needs two sides and the included angle.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

State the two pairs of equal sides and the common angle.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

It must state that $|AB|=|AC|$ and $|AC|=|AB|$, the two pairs of equal sides, and
that the angle at $A$ is the included angle, common to both triangles.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A student claims: "if a number is divisible by 4, then it is divisible by
8." Test the number 12 to decide whether the claim is true.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Check both divisions.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$12\div4=3$, but $12\div8$ leaves a remainder.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$12\div4=3$ exactly, so 12 is divisible by 4. But $12\div8=1$ with a remainder of
4, so 12 is not divisible by 8. The claim is false, and 12 is a counterexample.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A proof uses the converse of a theorem as if it were the theorem itself.
Explain why this can be wrong, and how a proof should use a converse.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A converse is a separate statement.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

It may be false, so it needs its own proof.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A converse is a separate statement. It can be false even when the original theorem
is true, so using it without proof leaves a gap. A converse may be used only when
it has itself been stated and proved, as the converses of the isosceles theorem
and of Pythagoras have been.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** A proof of the triangle angle sum draws the parallel line, uses the
alternate angles, and concludes that the three angles add to 180°. Which step is
missing?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The angles have been moved to $A$. What makes their sum 180°?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

They lie on a straight line.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The missing step is the straight angle. After moving the two angles up to $A$, the
proof must state that the three angles at $A$ lie on a straight line, so they add
to 180°.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** A proof contains: "the line through $A$ is parallel to $BC$, therefore
$|\angle ABC|=|\angle DAB|$." Is the step justified? Say why.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look for the transversal and the alternate angles.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$AB$ is a transversal of the two parallel lines.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

**Yes.** The two lines are parallel and $AB$ is a transversal, so the alternate
angles are equal by the alternate angles theorem. The step uses a real reason
correctly.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**9.** Here is a proof: "A quadrilateral has four angles. Each triangle has 180°,
so a quadrilateral has 180°." Find the fault.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

How many triangles make a quadrilateral?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Two triangles, not one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The fault is the count. A quadrilateral splits into **two** triangles, not one.
Each triangle has 180°, so the four angles of the quadrilateral add to
$2\times180=360°$, not 180°. The reason is right and the arithmetic is wrong.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from an earlier section

**10.** A proof reaches the equation $62+71+x=180$. Work out $x$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the two known angles, then subtract from 180.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$62+71=133$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$62+71=133$, and $180-133=47$. So $x=47°$.

The triangle angle sum from Section 20.3 is used inside many proofs, and checking
the arithmetic is part of judging a proof.

</details>
</details>
</details>
