# 20.1 Axioms, theorems, corollaries and converses — Learn

**Prerequisites:** measuring an angle in degrees, and knowing that a triangle has
three sides and three angles.

**Objectives:** say what an axiom, a theorem, a corollary and a converse are; tell
them apart; write the converse of a statement; explain what "implies" means.

By the end of this section you can read a geometrical statement and say what kind
of statement it is, and what would be needed to prove it.

## Geometry is built in order

You have used many geometrical facts for years. A triangle has three angles. The
angles on a straight line add to 180°. Two lines that never meet are parallel.

In this chapter you find out where those facts come from. Geometry is built like a
tower. At the bottom are a few statements we agree to accept. Each level above is
built only from what is already below it. Nothing is allowed in without a reason.

That order is the whole point. It is why a proof is possible at all.

## Axioms

An **axiom** is a statement we accept as true without proof. Every proof has to
start somewhere, so a course in geometry begins by naming its axioms.

This course uses five.

- **Axiom 1 (two points).** There is exactly one line through any two given points.
- **Axiom 2 (ruler).** A distance is never negative, the distance from $A$ to $B$
  equals the distance from $B$ to $A$, and any distance can be marked off along a
  ray.
- **Axiom 3 (protractor).** A straight angle has 180°, and angles can be added when
  they sit side by side.
- **Axiom 4 (congruent triangles).** Two triangles are congruent if SSS, SAS or ASA
  holds.
- **Axiom 5 (parallels).** Through a point not on a line, there is exactly one line
  parallel to it.

You do not prove an axiom. You use it.

A **definition** is different again. A definition gives a name to something: a right
angle is an angle of exactly 90°. A definition is not true or false. It is a naming
decision, and the rest of the course uses that name.

## Theorems and proofs

A **theorem** is a statement deduced from the axioms by logical argument. It is
proved, not assumed.

The statement "the angles in a triangle add to 180°" is a theorem. You have used it
since primary school. In Section 20.3 you will prove it from Axiom 5 and the
theorems that come before it.

A **proof** is the chain of reasons that carries you from what is given to what is
claimed. Every step names a reason: an axiom, a definition, or a theorem already
proved. A list of steps with no reasons is not a proof.

**Example 1.** Is "a straight angle has 180°" an axiom or a theorem?

It is an **axiom**. It is part of Axiom 3, and it is accepted without proof. There
is nothing earlier in the course from which to prove it.

**Example 2.** Is "the angles in a triangle add to 180°" an axiom or a theorem?

It is a **theorem**. It is true, but it is not accepted for free. It can be deduced
from Axiom 5 and the alternate angles theorem, and Section 20.3 does exactly that.

## Corollaries

A **corollary** is a result that follows quickly from a theorem already proved. It
is still proved, but the proof is short because the hard work is done.

For example, once you know that a diagonal divides a parallelogram into two
congruent triangles, it follows at once that the diagonal cuts the parallelogram
into two equal areas. That second statement is a corollary of the first.

## Converses

Most statements in geometry have the form "if $P$, then $Q$". The part after "if"
is the **hypothesis**, and the part after "then" is the **conclusion**.

The **converse** of a statement swaps them: "if $Q$, then $P$".

A converse is a new statement, and it may be true or false.

- "If a shape is a square, then it is a rectangle" is true. Its converse, "if a
  shape is a rectangle, then it is a square", is false: a rectangle 4 cm by 2 cm is
  a counterexample.
- "If a triangle is isosceles, then the angles opposite the equal sides are equal"
  is true. Its converse is also true: if two angles are equal, the triangle is
  isosceles.

So a converse must be checked. You cannot assume it.

**Example 3.** Write the converse of "if a number is divisible by 6, then it is
divisible by 3", and decide whether it is true.

The converse is "if a number is divisible by 3, then it is divisible by 6".

It is false. The number 9 is divisible by 3, because $9\div3=3$. But 9 is not
divisible by 6, because $9\div6$ leaves a remainder of 3. One counterexample is
enough.

## "Implies"

When we write "$P$ implies $Q$", we mean that whenever $P$ is true, $Q$ is true as
well. The first statement is the reason for the second.

"Equal alternate angles implies parallel lines" means that if a transversal makes
equal alternate angles, then the two lines are parallel. The equality is the
reason; the parallelism is the result.

> **Watch out.** A theorem and its converse are two different statements. The
> theorem "if a shape is a rhombus, then its diagonals are perpendicular" is true.
> Its converse, "if the diagonals of a quadrilateral are perpendicular, then it is
> a rhombus", is false. Writing the converse is the simpler part; deciding whether it
> is true needs a proof or a counterexample.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. You are going to prove that the angles of a triangle
add to 180°. The proof will use four statements:

1. There is exactly one line through a given point parallel to a given line.
2. When two parallel lines are cut by a transversal, the alternate angles are
   equal.
3. A straight angle is 180°.
4. The angles in a triangle add to 180°.

Which of these is the axiom, which is a theorem, and which is the result being
proved? Write your answers down, with one sentence for each. Keep them: Section
20.10 asks you to put the proof together.

## You should now be able to

- say what an axiom, a definition, a theorem, a proof and a corollary are;
- tell an axiom from a theorem, and say what would be needed to prove a theorem;
- find the hypothesis and the conclusion of a statement, and write its converse;
- test a converse with a counterexample;
- explain what "implies" means between two statements.
