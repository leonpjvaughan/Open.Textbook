# 20.1 Axioms, theorems, corollaries and converses — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Work out $180-65$.
2. Is 21 divisible by 3? Give a reason.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $115$. The angles on a straight line add to 180°, so this subtraction is how you
   find the missing angle.
2. Yes. The digit sum is $2+1=3$, and 3 is a multiple of 3, so 21 is divisible by 3.

Both skills come back here. You subtract from 180 to find an angle, and you test a
claim with an example. The second one is what a counterexample is made of.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A statement reads: "if a shape is a square, then it has four right angles."
Write down the hypothesis and the conclusion.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The hypothesis is what comes after "if". The conclusion is what comes after "then".

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Hypothesis: a shape is a square. Conclusion: it has four right angles.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The **hypothesis** is "a shape is a square". The **conclusion** is "it has four
right angles".

The statement says that being a square is enough to guarantee four right angles.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Write the converse of "if a shape is a square, then it has four right
angles", and say whether it is true.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Swap the hypothesis and the conclusion. Then look for a shape with four right
angles that is not a square.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The converse is "if a shape has four right angles, then it is a square".

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The converse is false. A rectangle 4 cm by 2 cm has four right angles, but its
sides are not all equal, so it is not a square. The rectangle is a counterexample.

A true statement can have a false converse.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Is "there is exactly one line through any two given points" an axiom or a
theorem? Give a reason.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

An axiom is accepted without proof. Is there anything earlier to prove this from?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

There is nothing earlier. It is the starting point.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

It is an **axiom**. It is accepted as true without proof, and it is the starting
point for other results. This is Axiom 1 of the course.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Is "the angles in a triangle add to 180°" an axiom or a theorem? Give a
reason.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Can it be deduced from something already accepted?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Yes: it follows from the parallel axiom and the alternate angles theorem.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

It is a **theorem**. It is true, but it is not accepted for free. It is deduced
from Axiom 5 and the alternate angles theorem. Section 20.3 writes the proof out.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A corollary is a result that follows quickly from a theorem. Which of these
is a corollary: (a) a straight angle has 180°, or (b) a diagonal divides a
parallelogram into two congruent triangles? Give a reason.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which one is accepted, and which one follows from a theorem about parallelograms?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

(a) is part of Axiom 3. (b) follows from the theorem about opposite sides.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

**(b)** is the corollary. Once Theorem 9 gives the opposite sides of a parallelogram
as equal, a diagonal makes two congruent triangles by ASA. No new argument is
needed, so the result follows quickly.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Write the converse of the isosceles theorem: "in an isosceles triangle, the
angles opposite the equal sides are equal."

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Swap what is given with what is claimed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Start with the equal angles, and conclude the equal sides.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The converse is: **if two angles of a triangle are equal, then the triangle is
isosceles.** It is true, and Section 20.5 proves it.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** Test the converse "if a number is divisible by 3, then it is divisible by 6"
with the number 9.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Check both divisions. One of them leaves a remainder.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$9\div3=3$, and $9\div6$ leaves a remainder of 3.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

9 is divisible by 3, because $9\div3=3$ exactly. But 9 is not divisible by 6,
because $9\div6$ leaves a remainder of 3. The converse is false, and 9 is the
counterexample.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** Explain what "equal alternate angles implies parallel lines" means.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

"Implies" connects a reason to a result.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

If the first statement is true, the second must be true.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

It means that whenever a transversal makes equal alternate angles with two lines,
the two lines are parallel. The equality of the angles is the reason, and the
parallelism is the result.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**9.** The chapter problem asks you to prove that the angles of a triangle add to
180°. One step of the proof is "a straight angle is 180°". Is that step an axiom
or something proved earlier?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look at the list of five axioms.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

It is part of Axiom 3, the protractor axiom.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

It is an **axiom**, part of Axiom 3. The proof of the triangle angle sum is allowed
to use it without proving it. The proof is not allowed to use the triangle angle
sum itself, because that is the result being proved.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from an earlier section

**10.** Round 4.68 to one decimal place.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look at the second decimal digit.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The digit after the first decimal is 8, which is 5 or more.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$4.7$. The hundredths digit is 8, so the tenths digit rounds up from 6 to 7.

Rounding returns whenever an angle is not a whole number.

</details>
