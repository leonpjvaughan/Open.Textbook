# Chapter 13: Formulae

A formula is a rule that connects quantities, written with letters. You already know
many of them: the area of a rectangle, the speed of a car, the cost of some tickets.
In this chapter you learn to put numbers into a formula, and to turn a formula around
so that a different letter stands on its own.

## The chapter problem

**The planter box.** The school garden club is building a wooden planter box. It is a
cuboid with no lid, so it is open at the top. It must hold **0.6 m³** of soil and fit
a space that is **1.5 m long** and **0.4 m tall**.

Two formulae describe the box.

- The volume of soil it holds is $V=lwh$.
- The area of board for its four sides and its base is $A=2h(l+w)+lw$.

Before you calculate anything, make a prediction and write it down. Do you think the
box will be wider or narrower than 1 m? Which formula do you think you will need
first?

By the end of the chapter you can find the width the box needs, and the area of board
it needs. You will return to this box in every section.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- put numbers into a formula and work out the value, following the order of operations;
- change the subject of a formula by doing the inverse operation to both sides;
- change the subject of a formula that contains a bracket or a fraction;
- substitute into a formula with a bracket or a fraction and check your answer.

## Where it is used

Science, engineering, building and business all work with formulae. A phone bill, a
temperature conversion and the amount of paint for a wall are all formulae. Changing
the subject is how you answer the question you actually have, rather than the one the
formula was first written for.

## Your turn to connect it

Where in your own week do you use a rule that connects two amounts? Think of a phone
bill, a bus fare, a recipe, or the number of minutes in some hours. Write down one
rule, name the letters you would use, and say which letter you would like to find.
