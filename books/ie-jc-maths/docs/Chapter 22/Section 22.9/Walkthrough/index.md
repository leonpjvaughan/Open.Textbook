# 22.9 Misleading graphs and how to spot them — Learn

**Prerequisites:** bar charts and histograms from Section 22.4, and interpreting
data from Section 22.8.

**Objectives:** spot the common ways a graph misleads; explain what makes a graph
honest; redraw a graph so that it shows the data fairly.

By the end of this section you can look at a graph and say whether it tells the
whole story.

## A true graph can still mislead

A graph can use correct numbers and still give a false impression. The person who
drew it chooses the axis, the scale and the pictures. Those choices change what
you see.

## Truncated axes

A **truncated axis** starts above zero. This makes small differences look large,
because the bars are drawn from a high base.

**Example 1.** A bar chart compares 12 points with 14 points. If the axis starts
at 0, the bars look almost the same height. If the axis starts at 10, the bar for
14 is twice as tall as the bar for 12.

The numbers did not change. Only the axis changed.

The honest fix is to start the axis at zero, or to mark the break with a clear
zigzag so the reader knows the axis is cut.

## Wrong-sized pictures

A pictogram uses a picture for a number. If the picture is made taller and wider
when the number doubles, its area grows four times. The picture then exaggerates
the change.

**Example 2.** A symbol stands for 10 cars, and another symbol stands for 20 cars.
If the second symbol is twice as tall and twice as wide, it looks four times as
big. Only the height, or the number of symbols, should change.

## Other traps

- A **broken axis** jumps over a range of numbers. A small jump can hide a big
  gap.
- **Different intervals** on the axis make one part of the graph look steeper.
- **A 3D effect** changes the height of bars through perspective.
- **A short time range** hides the full trend. A graph of one good month can hide
  a bad year.
- **Different group sizes** make totals unfair. Compare percentages when the
  groups are not the same size.

## How to check a graph

Ask these questions.

1. Does the vertical axis start at zero? If not, how big is the difference really?
2. Is the scale even, with equal steps?
3. Are the pictures the same size, or do they grow in two directions?
4. What time range is shown, and what is left out?
5. Are the groups the same size, so the comparison is fair?

## The chapter problem

The survey found a mean sleep of 7.4 hours. The school target is 8 hours.

A poster shows two bars. The bar for 7.4 and the bar for 8 look very different in
height. The vertical axis starts at 7.0, not at 0.

The difference is $8-7.4=0.6$ hours. On an axis from 7.0 to 8.0, the bar for 8
reaches the top and the bar for 7.4 reaches four-fifths of the way up. The gap
looks huge.

On an honest axis from 0 to 8, both bars are tall and close together. The gap is
real, but it is small. The poster used a truncated axis to make the gap look
large.

## Watch out

> **Watch out.** A truncated axis is not always a lie. Some data, such as
> temperature, needs a narrow range to show the changes. The problem is a
> truncated axis with no warning.
>
> The common error is to believe a graph because the numbers on it are correct.
> The numbers can be correct and the picture can still mislead.
>
> **Re-check:** read the bottom of the vertical axis. If it does not start at
> zero, work out the real difference before you judge the size.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem. A poster compares the mean sleep of 7.4 hours with
the target of 8 hours.

1. The vertical axis starts at 7.0. How does this change the picture?
2. Work out the real difference between the target and the mean.
3. How would the graph look with an axis from 0 to 8?
4. Write one sentence explaining why the poster is misleading.

Keep your answers. The Review brings the whole chapter together.

## You should now be able to

- explain what a truncated axis is and how it misleads;
- spot a pictogram whose pictures grow in two directions;
- name other traps, such as a broken axis or a short time range;
- ask the five questions that check a graph;
- redraw a graph so that it shows the data fairly.
