# 22.9 Misleading graphs and how to spot them — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Round 47 to the nearest 10.
2. A graph shows the values 12 and 14. Work out the difference between them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. 47 rounds to 50.
2. $14-12=2$.

Reading a graph starts with the axis, and judging a difference means subtracting
the two values. A misleading graph changes how the difference looks, not what it
is.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A bar chart compares 30 with 35. The vertical axis starts at 0. Are the
bars almost the same height, or very different?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The difference is 5, and the axis covers 0 to 35.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A difference of 5 is a small part of 35.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The bars look almost the same height. The difference of 5 is a small part of the
full height, because the axis starts at 0.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** The same two values, 30 and 35, are drawn on a bar chart whose axis starts
at 25. Do the bars look closer or further apart than before?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The bar for 30 rises 5 units above 25, and the bar for 35 rises 10 units.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The bar for 35 is twice as tall as the bar for 30.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The bars look much further apart. With the axis starting at 25, the bar for 35 is
twice as tall as the bar for 30, even though the real difference is only 5.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A pictogram uses one symbol for 20 visitors. A second symbol stands for 40
visitors, and it is twice as tall and twice as wide. How many times larger does the
second symbol look?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Area grows with height and width together.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$2\times2=4$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The second symbol looks 4 times larger, because it is twice as tall and twice as
wide. The value only doubled, so the picture exaggerates the change.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A graph shows only the last three months of sales. Sales were high in those
months, but low all year. Why is the graph misleading?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What does the graph leave out?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

It leaves out the other nine months.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The graph is misleading because it shows only a short time range. The three good
months hide the low sales in the rest of the year. A longer range would show the
full trend.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A company compares its sales with a rival. The company sold 200 items and
the rival sold 100. The rival has 10 shops and the company has 100 shops. Why is
the comparison unfair?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare sales per shop.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The company sold $200\div100=2$ items per shop. The rival sold $100\div10=10$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The totals are unfair because the groups are different sizes. Per shop, the rival
sells 10 items and the company sells 2. Comparing totals hides that the rival is
doing better per shop. Compare percentages or per-shop values instead.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A bar chart has a zigzag on the vertical axis between 0 and 100. What does
the zigzag tell you?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What does the axis skip over?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The zigzag marks a break in the axis.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The zigzag shows that the axis is broken and skips over a range of values. You
must not read the bar heights as if the axis ran straight from 0, because the
break changes the scale.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** The chapter problem. A poster shows the mean sleep of 7.4 hours next to the
target of 8 hours. The vertical axis starts at 7.0. How does this change the
picture?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The axis covers only 1 hour, from 7.0 to 8.0.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The difference of 0.6 hours fills most of the axis.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Starting the axis at 7.0 makes the difference of 0.6 hours look large, because the
whole axis covers only 1 hour. The two bars look far apart, although the real
difference is small.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** The chapter problem. How would the same graph look with an axis from 0 to 8?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Both bars are close to the top of a tall axis.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The bar for 8 reaches the top, and the bar for 7.4 is just below it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

With an axis from 0 to 8, both bars are tall and close together. The bar for 7.4
reaches most of the way up, and the bar for 8 reaches the top. The gap is real, but
it looks small, which is the honest picture.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from earlier work

**9.** A bar chart has bar heights 4, 6 and 8. Work out the mean height.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the heights, then divide by 3.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$4+6+8=18$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{18}{3}=6$$

The mean height is 6. A graph shows the data, and a measure of centre summarises
it; both must be read with care.

</details>
</details>
