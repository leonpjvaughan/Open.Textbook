# 22.1 Populations, samples and surveys — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A class has 30 students. 18 walk to school, and the rest take the bus. How many
   take the bus?
2. Write 18 out of 30 as a fraction in its simplest form.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $12$ students, because $30-18=12$.
2. $\frac{18}{30}=\frac{3}{5}$. Divide the top and the bottom by 6.

A sample is a part of a whole group, so the parts must add back to the whole. That
is the same idea as the class splitting into walkers and bus users.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A hospital wants to know how long patients wait for an appointment. It
asks 40 patients. Name the population and the sample.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The population is the whole group the hospital wants to know about.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The whole group is every patient. The part that was asked is the 40 patients.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The population is every patient of the hospital. The sample is the 40 patients
who were asked. The hospital uses those 40 answers to describe all the patients.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Why does the hospital ask 40 patients instead of every patient?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about time, cost and reach.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Asking every patient would take a long time and cost a lot. Some patients are
also hard to reach.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Asking every patient is a census. It takes a long time, costs a lot and may not
be possible. A sample of 40 is quicker and cheaper, and it gives a good estimate
if the sample is chosen well.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Is a census or a sample quicker? Give a reason.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A census asks everyone.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Fewer people means fewer answers to collect and count.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A sample is quicker, because it asks fewer people. A census is slower, because it
asks every member of the population.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** You count the number of cars passing the school gate each morning. Is this
primary data or secondary data?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Who collected the data?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

You counted the cars yourself, so you collected the data.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

It is primary data, because you collected it yourself. Data that someone else
collected is secondary data.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A report in a newspaper gives the average rent in your town. Is this
primary data or secondary data?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Did you collect the rents, or did the newspaper?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The newspaper collected the rents, and you read them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

It is secondary data, because the newspaper collected it and you are using it.
Before you trust it, ask who collected it and how.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A question asks: "Do you agree that the new canteen is much better and
cheaper?" Give one reason why this is a poor survey question.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Count the ideas in the question. Which words push the reader towards one answer?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The question asks about quality and price at the same time. The words "much
better" also lead the reader.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The question asks two things at once, so one answer cannot cover both. It also
leads the reader, because "much better" suggests the canteen has improved. Split
it into two clear questions with no leading words.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** The chapter problem. Your year group wants to know whether students get
enough sleep. What is the population?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Who does the year group want to describe?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The claim is about the whole school, not only one class.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The population is every student in the school. The sample of 20 students is the
part that will be asked. The 20 answers will be used to describe all 600 students.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** Write a survey question about screen time that asks about one thing only.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Ask for a number, and ask about one thing.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A good question names a time, so everyone counts the same period.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A good question is: "About how many hours did you spend on a screen after school
yesterday?" It asks about one thing, it names a clear time, and it asks for a
number.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from earlier work

**9.** A two-way table shows that 24 students play a sport and 16 do not. How many
students are in the class?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The two groups make up the whole class.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Add the two numbers.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$24+16=40$$

There are 40 students in the class. The two groups together are the population,
which is the whole class.

</details>
</details>
</details>
