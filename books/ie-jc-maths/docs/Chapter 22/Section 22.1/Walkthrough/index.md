# 22.1 Populations, samples and surveys — Learn

**Prerequisites:** reading a table, and counting a group of items.

**Objectives:** say what a population and a sample are; explain why a sample is
used; name the population in a context; write a survey question that gives a
useful answer.

By the end of this section you can look at a survey and say who was asked, and
whether that was the right group to ask.

## A question about a group

Statistics starts with a question about a group of people or things. You want to
know how many hours the students in a school sleep, or how many cars pass a school
gate each morning. The group you want to know about has a name.

The **population** is the whole group you want to find out about. It is not only
people. A population can be every car made in a factory, every apple in an
orchard, or every student in a school.

The **sample** is the part of the population that you actually collect data from.
You ask the sample, and you use their answers to describe the population.

**Example 1.** A shop wants to know what teenagers in a town think of a new drink.
The shop asks 60 teenagers in the town centre.

- The population is every teenager in the town.
- The sample is the 60 teenagers who were asked.

The shop did not ask every teenager. It asked a part of the group, and it will use
those 60 answers to describe all of them.

## Why we use a sample

You might ask why the shop did not ask every teenager in the town. There are two
reasons.

- Asking everyone takes a long time and costs a lot of money.
- Some members of the population are hard to reach, so asking everyone is not
  possible.

Asking every member of the population is called a **census**. A census gives the
full picture, but it is slow and expensive. A **sample survey** asks a part of the
population. It is quicker and cheaper, but it gives only an estimate.

The whole job of statistics is to make that estimate as good as it can be.

## Primary and secondary data

Data that you collect yourself is **primary data**. Data that someone else
collected, and you use, is **secondary data**.

| Kind of data | Who collected it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | You | You ask your class how many hours they sleep |
| Secondary | Someone else | You read the sleep figures in a health report |

Primary data is fresher, because you know how it was collected. Secondary data is
quicker to find, but you must ask who collected it and why.

## Writing a survey question

A **survey** collects data by asking people questions. The questions decide how
useful the answers are. A good survey question has three qualities.

- It asks about one thing only.
- It is clear, so every person reads it the same way.
- It does not lead the person towards one answer.

**Example 2.** A student writes this question for the sleep survey.

> How many hours do you sleep, and do you use your phone at night?

The question asks two things at once. A person who sleeps well but uses a phone
cannot answer both in one number. Split it into two questions.

> How many hours of sleep did you get on the last school night?
>
> About how many hours did you spend on a screen after school yesterday?

Each question now asks about one thing, and each answer is a number.

## Watch out

> **Watch out.** A sample describes the population only if the sample is like the
> population. The common error is to think that any sample will do.
>
> A shop asks only the teenagers standing outside a sports shop. Those teenagers
> may like sport more than other teenagers do. Their answers are not a good guide
> to the whole town.
>
> **Re-check:** ask two questions about a sample. Who was left out? Is the group
> that was asked like the whole population?

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem. Your year group wants to know whether the students
in the school get enough sleep.

1. What is the population?
2. You will survey 20 students. What is the sample?
3. Write one survey question about sleep that asks about one thing only.
4. Why is a sample used here, rather than asking all 600 students?

Keep your answers. In Section 22.2 you will choose the 20 students in a way that
avoids bias.

## You should now be able to

- say what a population is and what a sample is;
- name the population and the sample in a survey;
- explain why a sample is used instead of a census;
- tell primary data from secondary data;
- write a survey question that asks about one thing and does not lead.
