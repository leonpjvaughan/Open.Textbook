# 22.8 Interpreting data and questioning a claim — Learn

**Prerequisites:** samples from Section 22.1 and 22.2, and correlation from
Section 22.7.

**Objectives:** ask the four questions that test a claim; say what a set of data
supports and what it does not; use the size of a sample when judging an estimate.

By the end of this section you can read a claim and say how much it is worth.

## A claim is not a proof

A newspaper, an advert or a report often makes a claim from data. The claim may be
true, partly true, or too strong. Your job is to ask questions before you believe
it.

Four questions test almost every claim.

1. **Who was asked?** A biased sample cannot describe the whole population.
2. **How many were asked?** A small sample gives a rough estimate, not a fact.
3. **What was asked?** A leading question pushes the answer one way.
4. **What does the number mean?** A mean and a median can tell different stories.

## Who was asked?

If the sample is biased, the claim does not describe the population. A survey
about school meals that asks only students who bring a packed lunch will not
describe the whole school.

**Example 1.** An advert says: "Nine out of ten dentists recommend this
toothpaste." Ask who was asked.

If the survey asked dentists who were paid by the company, the sample is biased.
The claim does not describe all dentists.

## How many were asked?

A sample of 20 gives an estimate. A sample of 2000 gives a much better estimate.
A claim from a very small sample should be treated with care.

**Example 2.** A report says: "Most students walk to school." The survey asked 10
students, and 6 said they walk.

The sample is small. Six out of 10 is 60%, but with only 10 students the result
could change a lot if another 10 were asked. The claim is too strong for the
evidence.

## What does the number mean?

A single number can hide the spread. Two classes can have the same mean and very
different results. Always ask for the spread as well.

A percentage also needs its base. "Half of students" means one thing in a class of
10 and another in a school of 600. Always ask: half of how many?

## What the data supports

The data supports a statement about the sample. It may support an estimate about
the population, if the sample is fair. It does not support a claim about cause.

**Example 3.** The sleep survey found a mean of 7.4 hours in a fair sample of 20
students.

- Supported: "In this sample, the mean sleep was 7.4 hours."
- Supported with care: "The mean sleep of students in the school is probably near
  7.4 hours." The word *probably* matters, because 20 is a small sample.
- Not supported: "Screens cause students to sleep less." The survey shows a link,
  not a cause.

## The chapter problem

The survey gives these results.

- The mean sleep is 7.4 hours and the median is 7.5 hours.
- The range is 5 hours and the interquartile range is 2.5 hours.
- 10 of the 20 students slept at least 8 hours.
- Screen time and sleep are negatively correlated.

What does the survey support?

- In this sample, students slept less than the 8-hour target on average.
- The middle half slept between 6 and 8.5 hours.
- Students with more screen time tended to sleep less.
- Half the sample met the 8-hour target.

What does the survey not support?

- It does not prove that screens cause less sleep.
- It does not give an exact figure for the whole school, because the sample has
  only 20 students.
- It does not describe students who were absent on the day of the survey.

## Watch out

> **Watch out.** The word *prove* is too strong for a sample survey. The common
> error is to turn "the data shows" into "the data proves".
>
> A sample survey gives evidence. A fair, large sample gives strong evidence. It
> still does not prove a cause.
>
> **Re-check:** ask whether the claim is about the sample or about the whole
> population. Then ask whether the sample was fair and large enough.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem. The survey found a mean sleep of 7.4 hours in a
sample of 20 students.

1. Write one statement that the survey supports.
2. Write one statement that the survey does not support.
3. The school wants to make a claim about all 600 students. What should you say
   about the sample of 20?
4. A headline says: "Screens are stealing students' sleep." Is the survey strong
   enough for this headline? Explain.

Keep your answers. In Section 22.9 you will see how a graph can make a weak claim
look strong.

## You should now be able to

- ask the four questions that test a claim;
- say what a set of data supports and what it does not;
- use the sample size when judging an estimate;
- ask for the spread as well as the mean;
- avoid turning a link into a proof of cause.
