# 22.8 Interpreting data and questioning a claim — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Work out 25% of 40.
2. Work out 50% of 30.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $25\%$ of 40 is $40\div4=10$.
2. $50\%$ of 30 is $30\div2=15$.

Claims often use percentages, and a percentage needs its base. Always ask: a
percentage of how many?

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A headline says: "Most students want a longer lunch break." The survey
asked 8 students. Give one reason to question the claim.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

How many students were asked?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Only 8 students were asked.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The sample is very small. Eight students are not enough to describe a whole
school. If another 8 students were asked, the result could be different, so the
claim is too strong for the evidence.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A survey about the new timetable is given only to students in the music
room. Why should you question the result?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Who was left out?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Students who never go to the music room had no chance of being asked.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The sample is biased. It includes only students who use the music room, so it
does not represent the whole school. The result describes that group, not the
population.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** An advert says: "80% of people prefer our drink." The survey asked 10
people. How many preferred the drink?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find 80% of 10.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$80\% \text{ of } 10 = 8$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Eight of the 10 people preferred the drink. A sample of 10 is very small, so the
claim is weak even though the percentage looks large.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A report says the mean rent in a town is €1400, but the median is €1100.
Which number describes a typical rent better? Explain.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A few very expensive homes pull the mean up.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The median is the middle value and ignores the extreme values.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The median of €1100 describes a typical rent better. A few very expensive homes
pull the mean above the middle, so the mean is higher than most rents.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A survey finds that students who play sport score higher in tests. Can it
conclude that sport raises test scores? Explain.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A link is not a cause.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A third factor, such as a healthy routine, could affect both.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

It cannot conclude that sport raises test scores. The survey shows a link. A
third factor, such as a regular routine or good health, could affect both sport
and test scores, so a correlation alone does not prove cause.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A claim says: "Half of all students cycle to school." The survey asked 4
students, and 2 cycle. What is wrong with the claim?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Half of how many?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Half of 4 is 2, but that says nothing about all students.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The claim generalises from a sample of 4 students. Half of 4 is 2, but such a
small sample cannot describe all students. The claim needs a much larger and
fairer sample.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** The chapter problem. The survey found a mean sleep of 7.4 hours in a
sample of 20 students. Write one statement that the survey supports.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Keep the statement about the sample, or use the word "probably" for the school.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

"In this sample, the mean sleep was 7.4 hours."

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A supported statement is: "In this sample of 20 students, the mean sleep was 7.4
hours." It is also fair to say the mean for the school is probably near 7.4 hours,
because 20 is a small sample.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** The chapter problem. A headline says: "Screens are stealing students'
sleep." Is the survey strong enough for this headline? Explain.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The survey shows a link. Does it show cause?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The survey cannot prove that screens cause less sleep.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The survey is not strong enough for the headline. It shows a negative correlation
between screen time and sleep in one sample of 20 students. It does not prove that
screens cause less sleep, and a third factor could explain the link.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from earlier work

**9.** A survey of 50 students finds that 30 walk to school. Estimate how many of
600 students walk to school.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Scale the sample result up to the population.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$\frac{30}{50}\times600$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{30}{50}\times600=360$$

About 360 students walk to school. This is an estimate from the sample, not a
count.

</details>
</details>
