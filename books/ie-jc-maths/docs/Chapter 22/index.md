# Chapter 22: Statistics

Statistics is how we learn from data. We cannot ask every student in a school, or
every person in a country, what they think. So we ask some of them. From those
answers we describe the whole group, and we say how much we can trust the
description.

This chapter takes you from a question to a conclusion. You will choose a sample,
record the answers in a table, draw a graph, and work out numbers that summarise
the data. You will also learn to look hard at a graph or a claim, because a number
can be true and still mislead.

## The chapter problem

**Is the school getting enough sleep?** The school nurse says that students should
sleep for at least 8 hours on a school night. Your year group wants to find out
whether the students in the school really do.

The school roll has about 600 students. You cannot ask all of them, so you will
survey a sample of 20 students, chosen so that the sample represents the school.
Each student answers two questions:

- How many hours of sleep did you get on the last school night?
- About how many hours did you spend on a screen after school yesterday?

Here are the answers from the 20 students.

| Student | Screen time (hours) | Sleep (hours) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 9 |
| 2 | 1 | 10 |
| 3 | 2 | 9 |
| 4 | 2 | 10 |
| 5 | 2 | 8 |
| 6 | 3 | 8 |
| 7 | 3 | 8 |
| 8 | 3 | 9 |
| 9 | 4 | 7 |
| 10 | 4 | 8 |
| 11 | 4 | 8 |
| 12 | 5 | 7 |
| 13 | 5 | 6 |
| 14 | 5 | 7 |
| 15 | 6 | 6 |
| 16 | 6 | 7 |
| 17 | 7 | 5 |
| 18 | 7 | 6 |
| 19 | 8 | 5 |
| 20 | 8 | 5 |

Before you calculate anything, look at the table and write down a prediction. Do
you think the average sleep will be above or below 8 hours? Do you think students
who spend more time on screens sleep less?

By the end of the chapter you can build the frequency table, draw the graph, work
out the mean, the median and the mode, measure the spread, describe the link
between screen time and sleep, and say what the survey does and does not support.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- say what a population and a sample are, and why we use a sample;
- choose a sample in a way that avoids bias;
- build a frequency table from raw data;
- draw and read a bar chart, a line plot and a histogram;
- work out the mean, the median and the mode of a set of data;
- work out the range and the interquartile range;
- describe a scatter graph and the correlation it shows;
- question a claim by asking how the data was collected;
- spot a misleading graph and say how it misleads.

## Where it is used

Reports, news stories, health advice and advertisements all use statistics. A
headline says that a new breakfast makes students concentrate better, or that a
town has the fastest buses. Behind the headline is a sample, a table and a graph,
and the quality of those decides how much the claim is worth.

The same skills help you in school and at work. A teacher reads a class result to
plan the next lesson. A shop reads its sales to decide what to stock. A coach reads
a player's scores to decide who starts. In each case the question is the same: what
does the data show, and what does it not show?
