# 22.2 Choosing a sample and avoiding bias — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A bag holds 5 red counters and 15 blue counters. What fraction of the counters
   are red?
2. If you pick one counter without looking, is red or blue more likely?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. There are 20 counters, so the fraction is $\frac{5}{20}=\frac{1}{4}$.
2. Blue is more likely, because there are more blue counters.

A fair choice gives every item the same chance. A biased sample gives some
members of the population a bigger chance than others, just as a heavy blue bag
favours blue.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** What does it mean for a sample to be biased?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about which members of the population are more likely to be chosen.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

A biased sample favours one part of the population.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A biased sample is one in which some members of the population are more likely to
be chosen than others. The answers then describe that favoured part, not the whole
population.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A student asks the first 20 people who come out of the library about
reading habits. Explain why this sample is biased.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Who never goes to the library?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

People who are in the library are likely to read more than other students.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The sample is a convenience sample. The people in the library already read more
than most students, so they are not a fair copy of the school. Students who never
use the library have no chance of being asked.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Describe how to choose a simple random sample of 30 students from a school
of 600.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Number the population first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Give each student a number from 1 to 600, then pick 30 numbers at random.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Number every student from 1 to 600. Use a random number generator, or draw numbers
from a hat, to pick 30 different numbers. Ask the 30 students whose numbers come
up. Every student then has the same chance of being chosen.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A school has 360 students. You want a systematic sample of 45 students. How
many students do you take between each chosen name?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the population size by the sample size.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$360 \div 45$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$360 \div 45 = 8$$

Take every 8th name on the list, starting from a random point. This gives 45
students.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A school has 500 students and wants a sample of 25. How many students do
you take between each chosen name in a systematic sample?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out $500 \div 25$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$500 \div 25 = 20$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Take every 20th name. Starting at a random point keeps the sample fair.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A radio station asks listeners to phone in about a new road plan. Explain
why this sample is biased.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Who chooses to phone in?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

People with a strong opinion are the most likely to reply.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

This is a voluntary response sample. The people who phone in choose to take part,
and they usually feel strongly about the road plan. Quiet listeners are left out,
so the answers are not a fair copy of all listeners.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** The chapter problem. Describe a fair way to choose the 20 students for the
sleep survey.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Every student needs the same chance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Number the roll, then pick 20 numbers at random. Or take every 30th name.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Number every student on the roll from 1 to 600. Use a random number generator to
pick 20 different numbers, and ask those students. Every student then has the same
chance, so the sample is not biased towards one year group or one class.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** A student says that asking 200 students in the computer room is better than
asking 20, because the sample is bigger. Is the student right? Explain.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A bigger sample reduces the random wobble. Does it change who was chosen?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The 200 students are still all in the computer room.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The student is not right. A bigger sample gives a steadier estimate, but it does
not remove a bias. All 200 students are in the computer room, so the sample still
misses students who never go there.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from earlier work

**9.** A fair six-sided die is rolled. What is the probability of rolling a 3?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

There are six equally likely outcomes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

One outcome is a 3.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$P(3)=\frac{1}{6}$$

A fair die gives every outcome the same chance. A fair sample gives every member
of the population the same chance in the same way.

</details>
</details>
</details>
