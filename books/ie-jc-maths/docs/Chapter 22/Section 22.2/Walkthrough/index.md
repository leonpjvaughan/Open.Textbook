# 22.2 Choosing a sample and avoiding bias — Learn

**Prerequisites:** population and sample from Section 22.1.

**Objectives:** say what a random sample is; describe how to choose one; spot a
biased sample; explain why a larger sample does not fix bias.

By the end of this section you can choose a sample that gives a fair picture of
the population.

## Bias

A sample should be like a small copy of the population. If one part of the
population is more likely to be chosen than another part, the sample is **biased**.

A **biased sample** favours one part of the population. Its answers are not a fair
guide to the whole group.

**Example 1.** A school wants to know how students travel to school. A student
asks the first 20 people in the bicycle shed.

The sample is biased. Every student in the bicycle shed cycles to school, so the
answers will make cycling look far more common than it is. Students who walk, take
the bus or come by car have almost no chance of being asked.

## Random sampling

A **random sample** gives every member of the population the same chance of being
chosen. This is the fairest way to choose a sample.

Here is one simple method.

1. Number every member of the population.
2. Use a random number generator, or draw numbers from a hat.
3. Take the members whose numbers come up.

A second method is a **systematic sample**. Put the population in a list, choose a
starting point at random, then take every 10th member, or every 8th member.

**Example 2.** A school has 480 students. You want a sample of 60.

- For a systematic sample, work out $480 \div 60 = 8$. Take every 8th name on the
  roll, starting from a random point.
- For a simple random sample, give each student a number from 1 to 480 and use a
  random number generator to pick 60 numbers.

Both methods give every student a fair chance.

## Samples that go wrong

Some sampling methods are quick but biased. Two common ones are worth knowing.

- A **convenience sample** asks the people who are easiest to reach. Asking
  students in the library misses everyone who never goes there.
- A **voluntary response sample** lets people choose to answer. People with a
  strong opinion are the most likely to reply, so the answers are not typical.

A large sample does not fix bias. Asking 500 people outside a sports shop still
gives a sample of sports fans. Size reduces the random wobble; it does not remove a
bias in who was chosen.

## Watch out

> **Watch out.** A random sample is not a careless sample. Choosing "20 students
> at random" means using a fair method, not asking the first 20 people you meet.
>
> A sample chosen by walking into a corridor is a convenience sample. It looks
> random, because the chooser did not plan who to pick, but the people in that
> corridor are not a fair copy of the school.
>
> **Re-check:** ask whether every member of the population had the same chance of
> being chosen. If some had no chance, the sample is biased.

## Progress check

roll of about 600.

1. Describe a method that gives every student the same chance of being chosen.
2. For a systematic sample, how many students would you take between each chosen
   name?
3. A student suggests asking the 20 people in the computer room. Explain the bias.
