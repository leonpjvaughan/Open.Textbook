# 22.7 Scatter graphs and correlation — Learn

**Prerequisites:** plotting coordinates from Chapter 17, and the mean from
Section 22.5.

**Objectives:** plot a scatter graph from paired data; describe the correlation
as positive, negative or none; describe its strength; explain why correlation
does not prove cause.

By the end of this section you can look at two sets of data and say whether they
are linked.

## Two values for each person

Sometimes we collect two pieces of data from each person. Each student gives a
sleep time and a screen time. Data with two values for each item is called
**bivariate data**.

A **scatter graph** shows bivariate data. Each pair becomes one point. The first
value goes along the horizontal axis and the second value goes up the vertical
axis.

## Correlation

The pattern of the points is called **correlation**. It describes how the two
variables are related.

- **Positive correlation**: as one value increases, the other tends to increase.
  The points rise from left to right.
- **Negative correlation**: as one value increases, the other tends to decrease.
  The points fall from left to right.
- **No correlation**: there is no pattern. The points are scattered.

The correlation can also be **strong** or **weak**. A strong correlation means the
points lie close to a straight line. A weak correlation means they are spread out
around it.

## The line of best fit

A **line of best fit** is a straight line drawn through the middle of the points.
It shows the general trend. Draw it so that roughly the same number of points sit
above the line as below it.

The line of best fit lets you estimate a value that was not measured. Read across
from the horizontal axis to the line, then read down to the vertical axis.

## The chapter problem

The survey recorded screen time and sleep for 20 students. Here are the pairs.

| Student | Screen time (hours) | Sleep (hours) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 9 |
| 2 | 1 | 10 |
| 3 | 2 | 9 |
| 4 | 2 | 10 |
| 5 | 2 | 8 |
| 6 | 3 | 8 |
| 7 | 3 | 8 |
| 8 | 3 | 9 |
| 9 | 4 | 7 |
| 10 | 4 | 8 |
| 11 | 4 | 8 |
| 12 | 5 | 7 |
| 13 | 5 | 6 |
| 14 | 5 | 7 |
| 15 | 6 | 6 |
| 16 | 6 | 7 |
| 17 | 7 | 5 |
| 18 | 7 | 6 |
| 19 | 8 | 5 |
| 20 | 8 | 5 |

**Example 1.** Describe the correlation.

As screen time increases, sleep time tends to decrease. The points fall from left
to right, so the correlation is **negative**. The points lie fairly close to a
straight line, so the correlation is **strong**.

**Example 2.** Use the trend to estimate the sleep of a student with 6 hours of
screen time.

The students with 6 hours of screen time slept 6 and 7 hours. The trend suggests
about 6.5 hours.

## Correlation is not cause

A strong correlation does not prove that one variable causes the other. Both
variables might be affected by a third one.

Screen time and sleep are negatively correlated. That does not prove that screens
cause less sleep. Students who use screens late may also go to bed later for other
reasons, or students who are busy may have both less screen time and more sleep.

The correct statement is: the data shows a link. It does not show why the link
exists.

## Watch out

> **Watch out.** A correlation can be caused by a third variable. The common error
> is to read a scatter graph as a proof of cause.
>
> Ice cream sales and sunburn rise together. Ice cream does not cause sunburn. The
> hot weather causes both.
>
> **Re-check:** when you see a link, ask what else could explain it. A link is not
> a proof.
