# 22.3 Tallying and frequency tables — Learn

**Prerequisites:** counting, and reading a table.

**Objectives:** build a frequency table from a list of data; use tally marks; add
the frequencies to check the total; build a grouped frequency table.

By the end of this section you can turn a pile of answers into a table that shows
the pattern at a glance.

## Raw data

The answers you collect are called **raw data**. Raw data is only a list, and a
long list is hard to read.

Here are the sleep answers from the chapter problem, in the order the students
gave them.

$$9, 10, 9, 10, 8, 8, 8, 9, 7, 8, 8, 7, 6, 7, 6, 7, 5, 6, 5, 5$$

The list does not show a pattern. You have to count how many times each value
appears.

## Frequency tables

The **frequency** of a value is the number of times it appears. A **frequency
table** lists each value and its frequency.

To count, use a **tally**. Make a mark for each value. Every fifth mark is drawn
across the group of four, which makes the marks quick to count.

| Sleep (hours) | Frequency |
|---|---|
| 5 | 3 |
| 6 | 3 |
| 7 | 4 |
| 8 | 5 |
| 9 | 3 |
| 10 | 2 |
| **Total** | **20** |

The total is the sample size. Always add the frequencies at the end, because the
total must equal the number of answers you collected.

## Reading a frequency table

Once you have the table, you can answer questions quickly.

**Example 1.** Use the sleep table.

1. How many students slept 8 hours?
2. How many students slept fewer than 7 hours?
3. Which value is the most common?

1. The frequency of 8 is 5, so 5 students slept 8 hours.
2. Fewer than 7 means 5 or 6. The frequencies are 3 and 3, so $3+3=6$ students
   slept fewer than 7 hours.
3. The largest frequency is 5, which belongs to 8 hours. So 8 hours is the most
   common value.

## Grouped frequency tables

When the data can take many values, a table with one row per value is too long.
Group the values into **class intervals** instead.

Here are the minutes 30 students spent on homework.

| Minutes | Frequency |
|---|---|
| 0 to 19 | 4 |
| 20 to 39 | 9 |
| 40 to 59 | 8 |
| 60 to 79 | 6 |
| 80 to 99 | 3 |
| **Total** | **30** |

Each class has the same width, so the table is clear to read. The total is still
the number of students.

## Watch out

> **Watch out.** The value and the frequency are different numbers. The common
> error is to read the frequency as the value.
>
> In the sleep table, the row for 8 has frequency 5. That means 5 students each
> slept 8 hours. It does not mean anyone slept 5 hours.
>
> **Re-check:** add the frequencies. They must equal the number of answers you
> collected. In the sleep table the frequencies add to 20, which is the sample
> size.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem. Use the 20 sleep answers.

1. Copy and complete the frequency table for the values 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10.
2. What is the total of the frequencies? Why must it be 20?
3. How many students slept at least 8 hours?
4. How many students slept fewer than 7 hours?

Keep your answers. In Section 22.4 you will turn the frequency table into a bar
chart.

## You should now be able to

- explain what raw data is;
- make tally marks and count them;
- build a frequency table from a list of values;
- check that the frequencies add to the sample size;
- group data into class intervals of equal width.
