# Chapter 4: Accuracy and standard form

A measurement is never exact. A ruler stops at the nearest millimetre, a scale
jumps in steps, and a calculator prints ten digits whatever you type in. In this
chapter you learn how many of those digits to keep, how to check an answer with a
rough calculation, and how to write numbers that are very large or very small.

## The chapter problem

**The dripping tap.** A tap in the school cloakroom drips once every 4 seconds.
Each drip is about 0.25 ml of water. Nobody turns it off properly, so it drips all
day, every day, for a year.

Before you calculate anything, write down a prediction. Do you expect the tap to
waste nearer 20 litres, 200 litres or 2000 litres in a year? A prediction costs
nothing, and it gives you something to check your final answer against.

By the end of the chapter you can work out the volume for a whole year. You can
round it to a sensible degree of accuracy, check it with an estimate, and write it
in standard form. You will return to this tap in every section.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- round a number to the nearest 10, 100 or 1000, to a number of decimal places,
  and to a given number of significant figures;
- say how many significant figures a number has, and explain why the leading
  zeros in 0.0042 do not count;
- estimate the answer to a calculation by rounding each number to one significant
  figure, and use the estimate to check that an answer is plausible;
- write very large and very small numbers in standard form, and change standard
  form back to an ordinary number;
- compare two numbers written in standard form, and multiply or divide them.

## Where it is used

Science, engineering, medicine and money all work with measurements. A reading
from an instrument is only good to a certain number of figures, and a calculator
answer can carry more digits than the measurement deserves. Standard form is how
science writes the very large and the very small: the distance to the Sun, the
width of a cell, and the mass of a grain of sand.
