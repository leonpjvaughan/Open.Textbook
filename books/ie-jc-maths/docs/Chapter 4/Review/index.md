# Chapter 4: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**A tap drips once every 4 seconds. Each drip is about 0.25 ml. How much water
does the tap waste in one year? Give your answer in litres and in standard form,
to a sensible degree of accuracy.**

Start with one minute. There are 60 seconds in a minute, and a drip every 4
seconds gives 15 drips.

$$60\div4=15$$

Multiply by the volume of one drip.

$$15\times0.25=3.75\ \text{ml per minute}$$

Now build up to a year: 60 minutes, 24 hours and 365 days.

$$3.75\times60\times24\times365=1971000\ \text{ml}$$

There are 1000 ml in a litre, so divide by 1000.

$$1971000\div1000=1971\ \text{litres}$$

In standard form that is $1.971\times10^{3}$ litres, or $1.971\times10^{6}$ ml.

The tap drips all year, and no measurement here is exact, so the last few digits
are not worth keeping. To 2 significant figures the volume is 2000 litres, written
$2.0\times10^{3}$ litres.

**The tap wastes about 2000 litres of water in a year.**

Two checks, and both are free. First, the estimate: round 3.75 ml to 4 ml, then
$4\times60\times24\times365=2102400$ ml. The estimate is about 2,100,000 ml, which
is close to the exact 1,971,000 ml, so the answer is the right size. Second, the
size makes sense. Two thousand litres is about twenty full baths, which is a lot of
water from one dripping tap.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which idea applies** and
say why in a few words. Then calculate. Choosing between rounding, estimating and
standard form is the skill; the arithmetic follows from it.

1. Round 4.276 to 2 decimal places.
2. Round 0.007349 to 2 significant figures.
3. Round 27450 to 3 significant figures. Write the answer with enough zeros to
   hold its size.
4. Estimate $5.8\times41.7$. Say whether your estimate is too big or too small,
   and why.
5. A calculator shows $41.7\times5.8=241.86$. Is this plausible? Use an estimate.
6. Estimate $728\div19.4$.
7. Write 5200000 in standard form.
8. Write $3.4\times10^{-2}$ as an ordinary number.
9. Which is larger, $6.2\times10^{3}$ or $1.5\times10^{4}$? Give a reason.
10. Work out $(5\times10^{3})\times(4\times10^{2})$, and give your answer in
    standard form.
11. A number is written as $0.6\times10^{4}$. Is it in standard form? Give a
    reason, and write it correctly.
12. Find the highest common factor of 24 and 36.
13. Write 360 as a product of its prime factors.
14. Work out $2^{3}\times2^{4}$.
15. Write 0.875 as a fraction in its simplest form.

## What you can now do

- round a number to the nearest 10, 100 or 1000, to a whole number, and to a given
  number of decimal places;
- find the significant figures of a number, round to a given number of them, and
  explain why leading zeros do not count;
- estimate the answer to a calculation by rounding each number to one significant
  figure, and use the estimate to check that an answer is plausible;
- spot a wrong decimal point in a calculator answer;
- write very large and very small numbers in standard form, and change standard
  form back to an ordinary number;
- compare two numbers in standard form, and multiply or divide them.

## Thinking back

Look back at what you wrote at the start of the chapter, where you described a very
large or a very small number that you had seen somewhere.

Now find that number again. Write it in standard form, and say how many significant
figures the version you found carries. A phone's memory, a distance in space and a
population are all written in different ways, and the number of figures tells you
how precisely each one is known. If the number you found carries more figures than
the measurement deserves, that is worth writing down.
