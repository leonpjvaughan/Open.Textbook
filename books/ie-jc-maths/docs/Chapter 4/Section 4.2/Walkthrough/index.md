# 4.2 Estimating and checking a result — Learn

**Prerequisites:** rounding a number to one significant figure, from Section 4.1,
and multiplying and dividing whole numbers.

**Objectives:** estimate the answer to a calculation by rounding each number to one
significant figure; decide whether an exact answer is plausible; use an estimate to
spot a wrong decimal point; say whether rounding made an estimate too big or too
small.

By the end of this section, you can make a rough answer in your head and use it to
check a calculation.

## Estimating with one significant figure

An **estimate** is a rough answer that you can work out quickly. To make one, round
every number in the calculation to one significant figure. Then do the arithmetic.

Rounding to one significant figure keeps the size of each number and throws away
the detail. The estimate tells you roughly what the answer should be.

**Example 1.** Estimate $4.9\times21.3$.

Round 4.9 to 5, and 21.3 to 20.

$$5\times20=100$$

The exact answer is 104.37, so the estimate of 100 is close.

**Example 2.** Estimate $813\div18.6$.

Round 813 to 800, and 18.6 to 20.

$$800\div20=40$$

The exact answer is about 43.7.

**Example 3.** Estimate $0.42\times5.9$.

Round 0.42 to 0.4, and 5.9 to 6.

$$0.4\times6=2.4$$

The exact answer is 2.478.

## Checking an answer with an estimate

An estimate has one job: it tells you whether an answer is the right size. If the
estimate and the exact answer are close, the answer is **plausible**. If they are
far apart, something is wrong.

**Example 4.** A calculator gives $4.9\times21.3=1043.7$. Is this plausible?

The estimate is $5\times20=100$. The digits 10437 are the ones you expect, but
1043.7 is about ten times too big. The decimal point is in the wrong place. The
correct answer is 104.37.

This is the most common use of an estimate: checking the decimal point and the
number of digits.

## When the estimate is too big or too small

Rounding changes every number, so an estimate is rarely exact. The errors can push
it up or down.

- If you round both numbers up, the estimate is too big.
- If you round both numbers down, the estimate is too small.
- If you round one up and one down, the two errors partly cancel, and the estimate
  is often very close.

**Example 5.** Estimate $3.7\times4.2$.

Round both to one significant figure: $4\times4=16$.

Both numbers were rounded up, so 16 is too big. The exact answer is 15.54.

> **Watch out.** An estimate is not the answer. Rounding every number to one
> significant figure throws away the detail on purpose. A question that says
> **estimate** wants the rough answer; a question that says **work out** wants the
> exact one. Always write the exact answer when a question asks for it, and keep
> the estimate for checking that the exact answer is the right size.

## Progress check

Back to the dripping tap. In Section 4.1 you found that the tap loses 3.75 ml each
minute.

1. Round 3.75 ml to 1 significant figure.
2. Use that rounded value to estimate the volume lost in one year. Multiply by 60
   minutes, then 24 hours, then 365 days.
3. The exact volume for the year is 1,971,000 ml. Is your estimate close? Say
   whether the exact answer is plausible.

## You should now be able to

- round each number in a calculation to one significant figure and work out an
  estimate;
- compare an estimate with an exact answer and say whether the exact answer is
  plausible;
- spot a wrong decimal point in a calculator answer;
- say whether rounding up or rounding down made an estimate too big or too small.
