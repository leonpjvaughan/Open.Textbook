# Chapter 10: Expressions and substitution

Algebra begins with one idea: a letter can stand for a number. Once a letter can
stand for a number, you can write one rule that works for every case, instead of
a separate sum for each one. In this chapter you learn to write those rules, put
numbers into them, and read what an equals sign really says.

## The chapter problem

**The sponsored walk.** Your class is doing a sponsored walk for a charity. Each
sponsor agrees to pay €5 to take part, plus €2 for every kilometre the walker
completes.

Two questions come up.

- What does one sponsor pay when a walker completes 8 km?
- How far must a walker go for one sponsor to pay €25?

Before you calculate anything, write down a prediction. For the second question,
do you think the walker needs to go more or less than 10 km?

By the end of the chapter you can write the rule as an expression, put a number
into it, and use an equation to find the distance that gives €25. You will return
to this walk in every section.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- say what a letter stands for in an expression, and explain what each part of
  the expression means;
- write an expression from a rule given in words;
- put a number into an expression and work out its value;
- say what the equals sign means, and tell an expression from an equation;
- test whether a value makes an equation true, and find the value that does.

## Where it is used

Every rule that connects two amounts can be written as an expression. A phone
bill depends on the minutes you use, a bus fare depends on the number of stops,
and a recipe depends on how many people you cook for. Writing the rule with a
letter is what lets you answer the question you have, for any number, rather than
working out one case at a time.

## Your turn to connect it

Where in your own week does one amount depend on another? Think of a phone bill,
a taxi fare, a recipe, or the cost of some tickets. Write down one example, and
say which amount you would like to find.
