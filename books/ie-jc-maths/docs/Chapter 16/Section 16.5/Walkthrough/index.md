# 16.5 Volume of prisms, cylinders and spheres — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the area of a rectangle and a triangle from Section 16.1, and the
area of a circle from Section 16.2.

**Objectives:** find the volume of a rectangular solid; find the volume of a prism;
find the volume of a cylinder; find the volume of a sphere; write a volume in cubic
units, and change cubic metres to litres.

By the end of this section, you can find the space inside a solid.

## Volume and its units

The **volume** of a solid is the amount of space it fills. Volume is measured in cubes,
so its units are cubic centimetres (cm³), cubic metres (m³) and cubic millimetres
(mm³).

A cube of side 1 m holds 1000 litres of water. This links volume to capacity.

$$1\ \text{m}^3 = 1000\ \text{L} \qquad 1\ \text{cm}^3 = 1\ \text{mL}$$

## Volume of a prism

A **prism** has the same cross-section all the way along. To find its volume, find the
area of the cross-section and multiply by the length.

$$V = \text{area of cross-section} \times \text{length}$$

A rectangular solid is a prism with a rectangle as its cross-section. Its volume is
length times width times height.

$$V = l \times w \times h$$

**Example 1.** A rectangular solid is 5 cm long, 3 cm wide and 4 cm tall. Find its
volume.

$$V = 5 \times 3 \times 4 = 60$$

The volume is 60 cm³.

**Example 2.** A triangular prism is 10 cm long. Its cross-section is a triangle with
base 6 cm and height 4 cm. Find its volume.

Find the area of the triangular cross-section first.

$$\tfrac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 4 = 12$$

Multiply by the length.

$$V = 12 \times 10 = 120$$

The volume is 120 cm³.

## Volume of a cylinder

A cylinder is a prism with a circle as its cross-section. The area of the circle is
$\pi r^2$, and the length of the prism is the height $h$.

$$V = \pi r^2 h$$

**Example 3.** A cylinder has a radius of 3 cm and a height of 10 cm. Find its volume
in terms of $\pi$.

Square the radius first.

$$r^2 = 3^2 = 9$$

Multiply by the height and by $\pi$.

$$V = \pi \times 9 \times 10 = 90\pi$$

The volume is $90\pi$ cm³, which is about 282.6 cm³.

## Volume of a sphere

A sphere is a ball. Every point on its surface is the same distance from the centre,
and that distance is the radius.

$$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$$

The radius is cubed, so the radius is used three times.

**Example 4.** A sphere has a radius of 6 cm. Find its volume in terms of $\pi$.

Cube the radius first.

$$r^3 = 6^3 = 216$$

Then multiply by $\tfrac{4}{3}$ and by $\pi$.

$$V = \tfrac{4}{3} \times 216\pi = 288\pi$$

The volume is $288\pi$ cm³, which is about 904.32 cm³.

## The water tank

The chapter tank has a cylindrical part of radius 0.5 m and height 1 m. Find the
volume of the cylindrical part.

$$V = \pi r^2 h = \pi \times 0.5^2 \times 1 = 0.25\pi$$

The volume is $0.25\pi$ m³, which is about 0.785 m³.

Change it to litres. One cubic metre is 1000 L.

$$0.785 \times 1000 = 785$$

The cylindrical part holds about 785 L. In Section 16.7 you will add the
half-sphere on top.

## Watch out

Volume and surface area are different, and the formulas are not interchangeable.

> **Watch out.** A cube has sides of 4 cm. The common error is to write the volume as
> $6\times4^2=96$, which is the surface area.
>
> Volume fills the inside: $V=4\times4\times4=64$ cm³.
>
> Surface area covers the outside: $6\times4^2=96$ cm².
>
> **Re-check:** a volume is in cm³ and a surface area is in cm². The unit tells you
> which one you have found.

A second trap is the diameter. The formulas for a cylinder and a sphere use the
radius. Halve the diameter before you start, or the answer will be too large.

## Investigate a cuboid

**Predict, then test.** The figure draws a cuboid with its hidden edges dashed. Before
you move anything, write down what happens to the **volume** if you double the length,
and what happens to the **surface area** if you double the length. Are they the same?

Now double the length and check. Then set all three dimensions to the same number, so
the box is a cube, and see what the volume and the surface area are.

**Explain.** Write two or three sentences. Say what doubling one dimension does to the
volume and what it does to the surface area, and why the two answers are different.
Use the words "layers" and "faces" in your explanation.

## You should now be able to

- explain that volume is measured in cubic units;
- find the volume of a rectangular solid using $V=l\times w\times h$;
- find the volume of a prism using cross-section area times length;
- find the volume of a cylinder using $V=\pi r^2 h$;
- find the volume of a sphere using $V=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$;
- change cubic metres to litres using $1\ \text{m}^3=1000\ \text{L}$.
