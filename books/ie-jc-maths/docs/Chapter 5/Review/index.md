# Chapter 5: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The fruit punch for the school fair.** One jug holds 1.5 litres, and the recipe
uses $\frac{3}{4}$ of a litre of orange juice, $\frac{1}{2}$ of a litre of apple
juice and $\frac{1}{4}$ of a litre of sparkling water. The stall needs 12 litres.
Orange juice comes in 2-litre bottles that cost €2.50 each.

**Step 1. The volume of one jug.** Write every fraction with the denominator 4 and
add the numerators.

$$\frac{3}{4}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}=\frac{3}{4}+\frac{2}{4}+\frac{1}{4}=\frac{6}{4}=\frac{3}{2}$$

One jug holds $\frac{3}{2}$ litres, which is 1.5 litres.

**Step 2. The number of jugs.** Divide the total by the volume of one jug.

$$12\div\frac{3}{2}=12\times\frac{2}{3}=8$$

**The stall needs 8 jugs.**

**Step 3. The orange juice.** Each jug uses $\frac{3}{4}$ of a litre.

$$8\times\frac{3}{4}=\frac{24}{4}=6$$

**Six litres of orange juice are needed.**

The other two amounts work the same way. Eight times $\frac{1}{2}$ gives 4 litres of
apple juice, and eight times $\frac{1}{4}$ gives 2 litres of sparkling water.

**Step 4. Check the volume.** Add the three amounts back together.

$$6+4+2=12$$

That is the total the stall needs, so the three amounts are right.

**Step 5. The share of orange juice.** Divide the orange juice in one jug by the
whole jug.

$$\frac{3}{4}\div\frac{3}{2}=\frac{3}{4}\times\frac{2}{3}=\frac{1}{2}=0.5=50\%$$

**Half of every jug is orange juice.**

**Step 6. The bottles.** Each bottle holds 2 litres, and 6 litres are needed.

$$6\div2=3$$

**The class needs 3 bottles of orange juice.**

**Step 7. The cost.** The shop raises the price of a bottle by $10\%$, and then the
committee uses a voucher for $20\%$ off.

$$2.50\times1.1=2.75 \qquad 2.75\times0.8=2.20$$

One bottle costs €2.20 after the voucher, so three bottles cost

$$3\times2.20=6.60$$

**The orange juice costs €6.60.**

Every step used one of the five sections: ordering fractions, adding them, dividing
them, changing to a percentage, and changing a price by a percentage.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **which idea applies** and say
why in a few words. The idea might be equivalent fractions, a common denominator,
multiplying or dividing fractions, a conversion, or a percentage change.

1. Write $\frac{18}{24}$ in its simplest form.
2. Work out $\frac{2}{5}+\frac{1}{2}$.
3. Work out $\frac{3}{4}\times\frac{8}{9}$.
4. Work out $5\div\frac{1}{2}$.
5. Work out $\frac{7}{8}-\frac{1}{2}$.
6. Write $0.45$ as a fraction in its simplest form.
7. Write $\frac{7}{8}$ as a percentage.
8. Write $36\%$ as a fraction in its simplest form.
9. Write $\frac{3}{5}$, $0.55$ and $58\%$ in order, smallest first.
10. Increase 60 by $15\%$.
11. Decrease 150 by $30\%$.
12. A price falls from €80 to €68. Work out the percentage decrease.
13. A recipe for 4 people uses $\frac{3}{4}$ of a cup of rice. How much rice is
    needed for 6 people?
14. Find the highest common factor of 24 and 36, and use it to write
    $\frac{24}{36}$ in its simplest form.
15. Work out $4+3\times5$, and say which operation you did first.

## What you can now do

- make an equivalent fraction, and write any fraction in its simplest form using the
  highest common factor;
- compare and order fractions, decimals and percentages by writing them in the same
  form;
- add and subtract fractions with different denominators;
- multiply and divide fractions, and find a fraction of a quantity;
- change between fractions, decimals and percentages;
- increase and decrease an amount by a percentage, and work out a percentage change.

## Thinking back

At the start of the chapter you wrote down something in your own week that is a part
of a whole — a recipe, a battery level, a sale, or a score in a match.

Look back at what you wrote. Write the amount in all three forms now: as a fraction,
as a decimal and as a percentage. Then change it by a percentage. If a phone battery
is at $40\%$ and it charges by $25\%$ of that amount, what percentage is it at now?
Work it out and check that the answer is larger than $40\%$.

Finally, look at your prediction about the number of jugs. If you predicted 8, you
can now see why. If you predicted 4 or 16, work out where the estimate went wrong.
That comparison is worth more than the right answer on its own.
