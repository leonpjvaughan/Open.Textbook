# Chapter 15: Units of measure and time

Every measurement needs a unit. A length of 5 means nothing until you know whether it
is 5 mm or 5 km. A mass of 2 could be 2 g or 2 tonnes. The unit tells you what the
number measures, and how big one of them is.

Time has its own units, and they do not use tens. Sixty seconds make a minute, sixty
minutes make an hour, and twenty-four hours make a day. That is why time needs care
when you add and subtract it.

## The chapter problem

**The school tour to Paris.** The third-year class is flying from Dublin to Paris. The
plan has four numbers in it.

- The coach from the school to Dublin Airport is **12 km** and takes **25 minutes**.
- The class must be at the airport **2 hours before departure**.
- The flight leaves Dublin at **07:35** and takes **1 hour 55 minutes**.
- Paris is **1 hour ahead** of Dublin.

Before you calculate anything, make a prediction and write it down. Do you think the
coach leaves the school before 5 am or after? Write down your guess.

By the end of the chapter you can work out the time the coach must leave, the local
time in Paris when the class lands, and whether each bag is inside the baggage limit.
You will return to this tour in every section.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- name the metric units of length, mass and capacity, and write their symbols;
- read a length, a mass or a capacity from a scale;
- convert between units by multiplying or dividing by 10, 100 or 1000;
- convert between units of area and of volume, where the factor is squared or cubed;
- change a time between the 12-hour clock and the 24-hour clock;
- find a time interval from a timetable;
- change a time from one time zone to another;
- choose a sensible unit for a measurement, and estimate it;
- round a measurement to a sensible accuracy.

## Where it is used

Units are everywhere. A recipe gives grams and millilitres. A road sign gives
kilometres. A suitcase is weighed in kilograms, and a medicine spoon is measured in
millilitres. A bus timetable, a flight time and a match that runs into extra time all
need time arithmetic.

Getting the unit right can matter a great deal. A nurse who reads 5 mL as 5 L, or a
builder who reads 5 m as 5 cm, makes a serious mistake. Naming the unit is part of the
answer, not an extra detail.

## Your turn to connect it

Think of one measurement you met today. It might be the capacity of a bottle, the mass
of a bag, or the length of a journey. Write down the number, the unit, and what it
measures.

Then write one sentence saying why a different unit would be a poor fit. For example,
the mass of a bag is 5 kg, and 5000 g would be awkward to read.
