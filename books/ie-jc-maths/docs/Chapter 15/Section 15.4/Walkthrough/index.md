# 15.4 Choosing sensible units and estimating — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the units from Section 15.1, converting between units from
Section 15.2, and rounding from Section 4.1.

**Objectives:** choose a unit that fits a measurement; estimate a length, a mass or a
capacity from a known reference; round an estimate to a sensible accuracy.

By the end of this section, you can pick the unit that fits a measurement and give a
value that is close to the truth.

## Choosing the unit

A good unit keeps the number readable. It usually gives a number between 1 and
1000.

| What you measure | Sensible unit | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A pin head | millimetres | about 1 mm |
| A pencil | centimetres | about 18 cm |
| A room | metres | about 6 m |
| A journey | kilometres | about 12 km |
| A letter | grams | about 20 g |
| A person | kilograms | about 60 kg |
| A lorry | tonnes | about 12 t |
| A spoonful | millilitres | about 5 mL |
| A bottle | millilitres or litres | about 500 mL |

A unit that gives 0.003 or 3000000 is a poor fit. Change to a unit that brings the
number back near the middle of the scale.

## Estimating

An **estimate** is a value that is close to the true value. You build it from
something you already know.

A useful reference is a value you can picture.

- A door is about 2 m tall.
- A bag of sugar has a mass of 1 kg.
- A can of drink holds 330 mL.

**Example 1.** A pace is about 0.75 m. Estimate the length of a corridor that takes
8 paces.

Multiply the length of one pace by the number of paces.

$$0.75 \times 8 = 6$$

The corridor is about 6 m long.

**Example 2.** A textbook has a mass of about 1 kg. A school bag holds 4 textbooks and
a bottle of water with a mass of 0.5 kg. Estimate the mass of the bag.

Add the parts together.

$$4 \times 1 + 0.5 = 4.5$$

The bag has a mass of about 4.5 kg, which you can report as about 5 kg.

## Rounding an estimate

An estimate is not exact, so it does not need many decimal places. Round it to a
sensible accuracy. One significant figure, or the nearest whole unit, is usually
enough.

**Example 3.** A pupil estimates the length of the hall as 12.375 m. Round this to the
nearest 0.1 m.

The digit after the 3 is 7, so the 3 goes up to 4.

$$12.375 \to 12.4$$

The estimate is 12.4 m.

## The tour numbers

Three estimates belong to the chapter problem.

- A suitcase has a mass of about **18 kg**, which is under the 20 kg limit.
- A water bottle holds about **500 mL**.
- A liquid container holds at most **100 mL**.

Each estimate is a round number. None of them pretends to be exact.

## Watch out

An estimate with several decimal places claims an accuracy you do not have.

> **Watch out.** Match the accuracy to the method.
>
> A pupil guesses the mass of a bag as 3.62 kg. The guess came from counting books, so
> it is not known to the nearest 10 g.
>
> A mass of about 4 kg tells the truth about the method. The value 3.62 kg does not.
>
> **Re-check:** an estimate built from a reference gives a round number. If your answer
> has many decimal places, round it.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem. The baggage limit is 20 kg and each liquid container
holds at most 100 mL.

1. Estimate the mass of a full school bag in kilograms.
2. Estimate the capacity of a water bottle in millilitres.
3. Which unit fits the mass of a suitcase: grams, kilograms or tonnes? Give a reason.

## You should now be able to

- choose a unit that keeps a measurement between 1 and 1000;
- use a known reference to estimate a length, a mass or a capacity;
- build an estimate from the parts of an object;
- round an estimate to a sensible accuracy;
- say why an estimate with many decimal places overclaims.
