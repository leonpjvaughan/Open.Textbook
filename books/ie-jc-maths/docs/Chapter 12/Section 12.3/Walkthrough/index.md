# 12.3 Simultaneous equations by substitution — Learn

**Prerequisites:** solving linear equations from Section 12.1, and substituting into
an expression from Section 10.2.

**Objectives:** read a pair of simultaneous equations; make one letter the subject of
one equation; substitute it into the other equation; find both values and check them.

By the end of this section, you can solve a pair of simultaneous equations by
substitution.

## Two equations, two unknowns

A single equation such as $x+y=11$ has many solutions. The pair $(4,7)$ works, and so
do $(5,6)$ and $(0,11)$.

A second equation narrows the choice to one pair. Two equations together are called
**simultaneous equations**, and the solution must satisfy both of them at the same
time.

$$y=x+3 \qquad \text{and} \qquad x+y=11$$

The word *simultaneous* means *at the same time*. Only the pair $(4,7)$ makes both
statements true.

## The substitution method

The idea is to replace one letter with an expression, so that only one letter is left.
Then you have an equation you already know how to solve.

1. Make one letter the subject of one equation.
2. Substitute that expression into the **other** equation.
3. Solve the equation in one letter.
4. Substitute back to find the second letter.
5. Check both values in both original equations.

**Example 1.** Solve $y=x+3$ and $x+y=11$.

The first equation already has $y$ on its own, so substitute $x+3$ for $y$ in the second
equation.

$$x+(x+3)=11$$

$$2x+3=11 \qquad 2x=8 \qquad x=4$$

Substitute $x=4$ back into $y=x+3$.

$$y=4+3=7$$

The solution is $x=4$ and $y=7$. Check: $4+7=11$.

**Example 2.** Solve $y=2x$ and $3x+y=20$.

Substitute $2x$ for $y$.

$$3x+2x=20 \qquad 5x=20 \qquad x=4$$

Then $y=2\times4=8$. Check: $3\times4+8=20$.

**Example 3.** Solve $x=y+4$ and $x+y=10$.

Here $x$ is the subject of the first equation. Substitute $y+4$ for $x$.

$$(y+4)+y=10 \qquad 2y+4=10 \qquad 2y=6 \qquad y=3$$

Then $x=3+4=7$. Check: $7+3=10$.

## A bracket keeps a minus sign together

When the expression you substitute has more than one term, put it in a bracket. The
bracket keeps the sign with the whole expression.

**Example 4.** Solve $y=5-x$ and $2x-y=4$.

Substitute $5-x$ for $y$, and keep the bracket.

$$2x-(5-x)=4$$

$$2x-5+x=4 \qquad 3x-5=4 \qquad 3x=9 \qquad x=3$$

Then $y=5-3=2$. Check: $2\times3-2=4$.

**Example 5.** Solve $y=x-2$ and $3x+2y=16$.

Substitute $x-2$ for $y$, and keep the bracket.

$$3x+2(x-2)=16$$

$$3x+2x-4=16 \qquad 5x-4=16 \qquad 5x=20 \qquad x=4$$

Then $y=4-2=2$. Check: $3\times4+2\times2=16$.

## The chapter problem by substitution

The musical sold 200 tickets in total. Adult tickets cost €12 and student tickets cost
€8, and the money taken was €2040. Let $a$ be the number of adult tickets and $s$ the
number of student tickets.

$$a+s=200 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 12a+8s=2040$$

Make $s$ the subject of the first equation.

$$s=200-a$$

Substitute $200-a$ for $s$ in the money equation.

$$12a+8(200-a)=2040$$

$$12a+1600-8a=2040 \qquad 4a+1600=2040 \qquad 4a=440 \qquad a=110$$

Then $s=200-110=90$. So 110 adult tickets and 90 student tickets were sold. Check:
$12\times110+8\times90=1320+720=2040$.

> **Watch out.** Substitute into the *other* equation. In Example 1, putting $x+3$ back
> into $y=x+3$ gives $x+3=x+3$, which is true for every $x$ and tells you nothing. The
> common error is to use the same equation twice.
>
> **Re-check:** after you find the first value, put it into the simpler equation to find
> the second. Then put both values into the equation you did not use, and make sure it
> balances.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem.

1. Make $s$ the subject of $a+s=200$.
2. Substitute your expression into $12a+8s=2040$.
3. Solve for $a$, and then find $s$.
4. Check both values in the money equation.

In Section 12.4 you will solve the same pair by adding and subtracting the equations.

## You should now be able to

- explain why two equations are needed for two unknowns;
- make one letter the subject of one equation;
- substitute it into the other equation and solve;
- keep a bracket when the substituted expression has more than one term;
- find both values and check them in both equations.
