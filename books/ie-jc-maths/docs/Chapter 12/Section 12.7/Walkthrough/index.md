# 12.7 Choosing a method: graphic, numeric or algebraic — Learn

**Prerequisites:** solving equations from Sections 12.1 and 12.2, simultaneous
equations from Sections 12.3 and 12.4, and inequalities from Section 12.5.

**Objectives:** name the four strategies; choose the method that fits a question; solve
the same question by two methods and compare; say why one method is better here.

By the end of this section, you can choose a method that fits the question and say why.

## Four strategies

Every equation in this chapter can be solved in more than one way. The question in front
of you decides which way is best.

| Strategy | What you do | Good when |
|---|---|---|
| Algebraic | Rearrange the equation step by step | The equation is linear and tidy |
| Graphic | Read the crossing point from a graph | A graph is already drawn |
| Numeric | Use a table of values | The equation has a square or an awkward number |
| Working backwards | Undo the story step by step | The question describes a chain of actions |
| Trial and improvement | Try values and close in on the answer | No method rearranges neatly |

The first three come from the word *algebraic, graphic, numeric*. The last two are the
strategies of trial and improvement and working backwards.

## Working backwards

When a question describes a chain of actions, undo the chain from the end.

**Example 1.** I think of a number, multiply it by 3, and add 4. The result is 19. Find
the number.

Start at the end and undo each step. Subtract 4 from 19, and then divide by 3.

$$19-4=15 \qquad 15\div3=5$$

The number is 5. As an equation this is $3x+4=19$, so $x=5$.

## Trial and improvement

When the equation has a square in it, rearranging is harder. Try a value, see whether
the answer is too big or too small, and try again.

**Example 2.** Solve $x^2+x=20$ by trial and improvement.

Try $x=3$: $9+3=12$, too small. Try $x=5$: $25+5=30$, too big. Try $x=4$:
$16+4=20$, which matches. So $x=4$.

Record every trial in a table, with the value you tried and the result. The table shows
your reasoning, and it stops you going round in circles.

**Example 3.** Solve $x^2=30$ to one decimal place.

Try $x=5$: 25, too small. Try $x=6$: 36, too big. Try $x=5.5$: 30.25, a little too
big. Try $x=5.4$: 29.16, too small. The root lies between 5.4 and 5.5, so to one decimal
place $x=5.5$.

## A graph

A graph shows the answer as a crossing point. If two lines are drawn, their crossing
point solves the equation made by setting their rules equal.

**Example 4.** The graphs of $y=2x+1$ and $y=x+4$ cross at one point. Solve
$2x+1=x+4$.

Read the $x$-value of the crossing point from the graph. The lines cross at $(3,7)$, so
$x=3$. Algebra confirms it: $2x+1=x+4$, so $x=3$.

## Algebra

When the equation is linear, algebra gives an exact answer with no reading error. It is
usually the fastest method.

**Example 5.** Solve $5(x-2)=3x+4$.

$$5x-10=3x+4 \qquad 2x=14 \qquad x=7$$

## Choosing for simultaneous equations

For a pair of simultaneous equations, look at how the equations are written.

- If one equation already has a letter on its own, **substitute**.
- If the coefficients of a letter match or are opposite, **eliminate**.
- If neither holds, either method works, and elimination is often tidier.

**Example 6.** Solve $y=2x$ and $x+y=12$.

The first equation gives $y$ on its own, so substitute: $x+2x=12$, so $x=4$ and $y=8$.

**Example 7.** Solve $x+y=9$ and $x-y=3$.

The $y$ terms are opposite, so eliminate: add to get $2x=12$, so $x=6$ and $y=3$.

> **Watch out.** Match the method to the question, and finish the job. In a
> trial-and-improvement question, the common error is to stop at the first value that is
> close and not test the next one.
>
> **Re-check:** for a decimal answer, test the value below and the value above, and show
> that the answer lies between them. For a whole-number answer, show that it matches
> exactly.

## You should now be able to

- name the algebraic, graphic, numeric, working-backwards and trial-and-improvement
  strategies;
- choose a method that fits the equation in front of you;

- record the trials in a trial-and-improvement table;
- say why one method is better than another for a given question.
