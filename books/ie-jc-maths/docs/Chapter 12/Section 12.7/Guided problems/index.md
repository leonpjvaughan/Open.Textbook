# 12.7 Choosing a method: graphic, numeric or algebraic — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Solve $2x+1=9$.
2. Work out $5^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $x=4$. Subtract 1, and then divide by 2.
2. $25$. The index 2 means $5\times5$.

The first is the algebra you may choose, and the second is the kind of value a table or a
trial-and-improvement column contains.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** I think of a number, multiply it by 4, and subtract 3. The result is 17. Find the
number by working backwards.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Undo the last step first. Add 3, and then divide by 4.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$17+3=20 \qquad 20\div4=5$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The number is 5. Check: $4\times5-3=17$. Working backwards undoes the chain from the
end.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Use trial and improvement to solve $x^2=49$ for a whole number $x$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Try a whole number, and see whether its square is too big or too small.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Try $x=6$: $36$, too small. Try $x=8$: $64$, too big.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Try $x=7$: $49$, which matches. So $x=7$.

The table of trials shows that 7 sits between the too-small 6 and the too-big 8.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Use trial and improvement to solve $x^2+x=30$ for a whole number $x$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out $x^2+x$ for a few whole numbers. Start with 4 and 5.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Try $x=4$: $16+4=20$, too small. Try $x=5$: $25+5=30$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$x=5$$

The target 30 is reached exactly at $x=5$, so no value between 4 and 5 is needed.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** The graphs of $y=x+1$ and $y=3x-3$ cross at $(2,3)$. Use the graph to solve
$x+1=3x-3$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The crossing point gives both values. You want the $x$-value.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The crossing point is $(2,3)$, so $x=2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$x=2$$

Check in the equation: $2+1=3$ and $3\times2-3=3$. Both sides agree at the crossing
point.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A table of values for $y=2x+1$ is built up. Use it to solve $2x+1=11$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the value of $x$ whose $2x+1$ entry is 11.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$2x+1=11 \qquad 2x=10$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$x=5$$

The table reaches 11 when $x=5$. Check: $2\times5+1=11$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Solve $4(x+1)=2x+10$ algebraically.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Expand the bracket, and then collect the letters.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$4x+4=2x+10 \qquad 2x+4=10$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$2x=6 \qquad x=3$$

Check: $4(3+1)=16$ and $2\times3+10=16$. The algebra gives an exact answer with no
reading error.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** Solve $y=3x$ and $x+y=16$. Which method suits this pair, and why?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One equation already has a letter on its own.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Substitute $3x$ for $y$: $x+3x=16$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$4x=16 \qquad x=4$$

Then $y=3\times4=12$. Check: $4+12=16$.

Substitution suits this pair, because the first equation gives $y$ on its own.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** Solve $2x+y=11$ and $x+y=7$. Which method suits this pair, and why?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The $y$ terms are both $+y$, so they match.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Subtract the second equation from the first: $x=4$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Then $4+y=7$, so $y=3$. Check: $2\times4+3=11$.

Elimination suits this pair, because the $y$ terms already match.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**9.** The chapter problem claims that $a=110$ works in $12a+8(200-a)=2040$. Use trial
and improvement to check it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out $12a+8(200-a)$ at $a=110$. Then try a value either side.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

At $a=110$: $12\times110+8\times90=1320+720=2040$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The value is exactly 2040, so $a=110$ works.

Try $a=100$: $1200+800=2000$, which is short. Try $a=120$: $1440+640=2080$, which is
too big. The value 2040 lies between them, and $a=110$ reaches it exactly.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from an earlier section

**10.** Solve $5x-3=12$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look back at Section 12.1. Add 3 to both sides first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$5x=15$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$x=3$$

Whichever method you choose, an equation like this is solved by the same two steps. The
method decides how you reach the steps, not what they are.

</details>
</details>
</details>
