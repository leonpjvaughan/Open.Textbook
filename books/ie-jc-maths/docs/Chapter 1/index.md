# Chapter 1: Number systems and the four operations

Numbers come in families. Some are for counting, some are for measuring, and some
show that money is owed. In this chapter you learn which family a number belongs to,
and how to add, subtract, multiply and divide without losing a sign.

## The chapter problem

**The class trip budget.** Your year group is planning a trip to a climbing centre.
Here is what the quotes say.

- The bus costs €480.
- The centre charges €14 for each person.
- Lunch costs €6.50 for each person.
- 35 people are going: 32 students and 3 teachers.
- The class fund holds €620. The bus deposit of €150 and the centre deposit of €70
  have already been paid from the fund.

Before you calculate anything, make a prediction and write it down. Do you expect
the trip to cost more or less than €1000? Which calculation do you think you will
need first — adding and subtracting, or multiplying and dividing?

By the end of the chapter you can say what the trip costs in total, how much the
class fund covers, what each person pays, and whether the money works out. You will
return to this budget in every section.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- say whether a number belongs to the natural numbers, the integers or the rational
  numbers;
- add and subtract integers, including negative ones, and read a balance that has
  gone below zero;
- multiply and divide integers and say what the sign of the answer will be;
- use the order of operations and brackets to work out a calculation correctly;
- use the commutative, associative and distributive properties to choose a quicker
  route;
<!-- tier:h -->
- simplify a surd, and estimate a square root that is not a whole number.

## Where it is used

Budgets, quotes, invoices and receipts. A payslip, a phone bill and a shopping total
all need the four operations, and all of them can go wrong through a lost minus sign
or a calculation done in the wrong order. Knowing the properties of the operations
is what lets you check a total a second time and trust it.
