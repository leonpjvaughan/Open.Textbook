# 1.6 Surds and irrational numbers — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the number systems from Section 1.1, squares and square roots, and
the order of operations from Section 1.4.

**Objectives:** say whether a square root is rational or irrational; simplify a surd;
add and subtract like surds; multiply surds; estimate a surd; write a fraction with a
whole-number denominator.

By the end of this section, you can work with a square root that is not a whole number
and keep the answer exact.

## Square roots

The square root of a number is the number you square to get it.

$$\sqrt{9} = 3 \qquad \text{because} \qquad 3^2 = 9$$

The square roots of the square numbers are whole numbers. The square numbers are

$$1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, \ldots$$

Most numbers are not square numbers, and their square roots are not whole numbers.
For example, $\sqrt{10}$ sits between 3 and 4, because $3^2 = 9$ and $4^2 = 16$.

## Irrational numbers

A **rational number** can be written as a fraction of two integers. An **irrational
number** cannot. Its decimal expansion never stops and never repeats.

$$\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\ldots$$

The dots mean the digits carry on for ever with no repeating block. So $\sqrt{2}$ is
irrational. The same is true of $\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{5}$ and $\pi$.

**Why $\sqrt{2}$ is not a fraction.** Suppose it were, and write
$\sqrt{2} = \frac{a}{b}$ in its lowest terms, so $a$ and $b$ have no common factor.
Squaring both sides gives $2 = \frac{a^2}{b^2}$, and multiplying by $b^2$ gives

$$a^2 = 2b^2$$

The right-hand side is even, so $a^2$ is even, which means $a$ is even. Write
$a = 2k$. Then $4k^2 = 2b^2$, so $2k^2 = b^2$. Now $b^2$ is even, so $b$ is even too.
Both $a$ and $b$ are even, which contradicts the choice that the fraction was in its
lowest terms. So $\sqrt{2}$ cannot be written as a fraction.

A **surd** is a square root that is not a whole number, written exactly. So
$\sqrt{2}$, $5\sqrt{3}$ and $\sqrt{50}$ are surds. A surd is an exact value; the
decimal is only an approximation.

## Simplifying a surd

Use the rule that the square root of a product is the product of the square roots.

$$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b}$$

So look for the largest square factor of the number inside the root.

**Example 1.** Simplify $\sqrt{50}$.

$$50 = 25 \times 2 \qquad \sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25} \times \sqrt{2} = 5\sqrt{2}$$

**Example 2.** Simplify $\sqrt{72}$.

$$72 = 36 \times 2 \qquad \sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36} \times \sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2}$$

**Example 3.** Simplify $\sqrt{48}$.

$$48 = 16 \times 3 \qquad \sqrt{48} = \sqrt{16} \times \sqrt{3} = 4\sqrt{3}$$

Always take out the **largest** square factor. Taking out 4 from 48 leaves
$2\sqrt{12}$, and 12 still contains a square factor, so the answer is not finished.

## Like surds

Two surds are **like** when they are multiples of the same root. You add and subtract
them in the same way as algebraic terms.

$$2\sqrt{3} + 5\sqrt{3} = 7\sqrt{3} \qquad 4\sqrt{5} - \sqrt{5} = 3\sqrt{5}$$

This is the same idea as $2x + 5x = 7x$. The number in front adds, and the surd stays.

**Example 4.** Work out $\sqrt{8} + \sqrt{2}$.

Simplify first, then add.

$$\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2} \qquad 2\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{2} = 3\sqrt{2}$$

## Multiplying surds

Multiply the numbers inside the roots.

**Example 5.** Work out $\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{12}$.

$$\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{12} = \sqrt{36} = 6$$

**Example 6.** Work out $\sqrt{8} \times \sqrt{2}$.

$$\sqrt{8} \times \sqrt{2} = \sqrt{16} = 4$$

Sometimes the product is a whole number, and then the answer is not a surd at all.

## Estimating a surd

Find the two whole squares the number lies between, and then narrow it down.

**Example 7.** Estimate $\sqrt{2}$ to three decimal places.

$\sqrt{2}$ lies between 1 and 2, because $1^2 = 1$ and $2^2 = 4$. It is closer to 1,
and a calculator gives

$$\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\ldots$$

so $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$ correct to three decimal places.

## A whole-number denominator

A fraction with a surd on the bottom can be rewritten with a whole number on the
bottom. Multiply the top and the bottom by the same surd.

**Example 8.** Write $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ with a whole number on the bottom.

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \times \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$

The value has not changed, because you multiplied by $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} = 1$.

> **Watch out.** A square root does not split over an addition. The common error is
> to write $\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16}$. It is not:
>
> $$\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \qquad \text{but} \qquad \sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7$$
>
> The rule $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b}$ is about a product, not a sum.
> **Re-check:** work out the number under the root first. If it is a square number,
> the answer is a whole number.

## You should now be able to

- say whether a square root is a whole number or an irrational number;
- simplify a surd by taking out its largest square factor;
- add and subtract like surds;
- multiply two surds, and recognise when the answer is a whole number;
- estimate a surd to a required number of decimal places;
- write a fraction with a surd on the bottom as one with a whole-number denominator.
