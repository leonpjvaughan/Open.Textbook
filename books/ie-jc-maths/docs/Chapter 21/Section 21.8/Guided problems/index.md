# 21.8 Expected frequency — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Work out $300\times\frac16$.
2. Work out $\frac38$ of 240.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $50$. Divide 300 by 6, because multiplying by $\frac16$ is the same as dividing
   by 6.
2. $90$. First find $\frac18$ of 240, which is 30, then multiply by 3 to get 90.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A fair coin is tossed 200 times. Work out the expected number of heads.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply the probability by the number of tosses.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$200\times\frac12$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$200\times\frac12=100$$

The expected number of heads is 100. Half of the tosses are expected to be heads,
because the probability of a head is one half.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A fair six-sided die is rolled 90 times. Work out the expected number of 6s.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The probability of a 6 is $\frac16$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$90\times\frac16$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$90\times\frac16=15$$

The expected number of 6s is 15, which is one sixth of the 90 rolls. The observed
number will usually be near 15 rather than exactly 15.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** In the open-day game the probability of a prize is $\frac38$. The game is
played 240 times. Work out the expected number of prizes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply the probability by the number of plays.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$240\times\frac38$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$240\times\frac38=90$$

The organiser should expect about 90 prizes from 240 plays. Because $\frac38$ is
less than one half, the expected number is less than half of 240.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** In the open-day game the probability of the star prize is $\frac{1}{24}$.
The game is played 240 times. Work out the expected number of star prizes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply $\frac{1}{24}$ by 240.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$240\div24$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$240\times\frac{1}{24}=10$$

The expected number of star prizes is 10. The star prize is rare, so it is a small
share of the 90 prizes.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A spinner lands on red with probability $\frac38$. It is spun 400 times.
Work out the expected number of reds.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply the probability by the number of spins.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$400\times\frac38$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$400\times\frac38=150$$

The expected number of reds is 150. A run of 400 spins that gives 140 reds is
ordinary, and it does not on its own show that the spinner is unfair.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A biased coin lands on heads with probability $0.6$. It is tossed 250
times. Work out the expected number of heads.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply the decimal probability by the number of tosses.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$250\times0.6$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$250\times0.6=150$$

The expected number of heads is 150. The probability is more than one half, so the
expected number is more than half of 250.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** A fair die is rolled 300 times. The expected number of 6s is 50, but the die
shows 44 sixes. Work out the difference between the observed and the expected
number.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Subtract the observed number from the expected number.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$50-44$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$50-44=6$$

The observed number is 6 below the expected number. A difference of 6 in 300 rolls
is ordinary, because the expected number is a long-run average and not a promise.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** A die is rolled 300 times and shows a 6 on 44 of the rolls. Work out the
relative frequency of a 6, and compare it with the theoretical probability.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the number of sixes by the number of rolls.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\frac{44}{300}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{44}{300}=\frac{11}{75}\approx0.147$$

The theoretical probability is $\frac16\approx0.167$. The relative frequency is a
little lower, and the two values are close. More rolls would usually bring the
relative frequency closer to $\frac16$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from an earlier section

**9.** A fair six-sided die is rolled. Find the probability that the result is not
a 6.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the complement: subtract the probability of a 6 from 1.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$1-\frac16$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$P(\text{not }6)=1-\frac16=\frac56$$

Five of the 6 equally likely results are not a 6, so the probability is $\frac56$.

</details>
</details>
</details>
