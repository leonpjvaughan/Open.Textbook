# 21.2 Probability as a number from 0 to 1 — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write $\frac14$ as a decimal.
2. Write $0.5$ as a fraction in its simplest form.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $0.25$, because $1\div4=0.25$.
2. $\frac12$. The decimal $0.5$ means five tenths, so $\frac{5}{10}=\frac12$. A
   fraction and a decimal are two ways of writing the same number.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A bag holds 10 counters: 7 red and 3 blue. One counter is taken at random.
Is red likely or unlikely?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out the probability of red, then compare it with $0.5$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The probability of red is $\frac{7}{10}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Red is likely. The probability is $\frac{7}{10}=0.7$, which is more than $0.5$.
The value $0.7$ sits between an even chance and certain, so the word for it is
likely.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Write the probability $\frac25$ as a decimal and as a percentage.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the top by the bottom, then multiply by 100.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$2\div5=0.4$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac25=0.4=40\%$$

The decimal $0.4$ is less than $0.5$, so the event is less likely than not. The
percentage is $0.4\times100=40$ per cent.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A die is rolled 50 times and shows a 6 on 9 of the rolls. Work out the
relative frequency of a 6.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the number of sixes by the number of rolls.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The relative frequency is $\frac{9}{50}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{9}{50}=0.18$$

The relative frequency is $0.18$. The theoretical probability of a 6 is
$\frac16\approx0.167$, so the two are close but not equal. The relative frequency
comes from this sample of 50 rolls.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A spinner is spun 80 times and lands on green 32 times. Work out the
relative frequency of green.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the relative frequency formula: event count divided by trial count.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\frac{32}{80}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{32}{80}=\frac{4}{10}=0.4$$

The relative frequency is $0.4$, or 40 per cent. Divide the top and the bottom by
8 to simplify, or by 10 twice to reach $\frac{4}{10}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Someone says the probability of an event is $1.4$. Is that possible?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Where does $1.4$ sit compared with the top of the scale?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The top of the scale is 1, and $1.4$ is greater than 1.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

No. A probability cannot be greater than 1, because 1 is the probability of a
certain event. Nothing can be more likely than certain, so $1.4$ is not a
probability.

The same reasoning rules out a negative probability: nothing is less likely than
impossible.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A spinner is spun 200 times and lands on red 46 times. The theoretical
probability of red is $\frac14$. Compare the relative frequency with the
theoretical probability.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out the relative frequency, then write the theoretical probability as a
decimal.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The relative frequency is $\frac{46}{200}=0.23$, and $\frac14=0.25$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The relative frequency is $0.23$ and the theoretical probability is $0.25$. The
two are close, and the relative frequency is slightly lower.

A difference of $0.02$ over 200 spins is ordinary. The relative frequency is an
estimate from this sample, so it need not equal the theoretical value.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from an earlier section

**7.** A fair coin is tossed three times. How many outcomes are in the sample
space?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Each toss doubles the number of outcomes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$2\times2\times2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$2\times2\times2=8$$

There are 8 outcomes. The first toss gives 2 results, the second doubles that to
4, and the third doubles it again to 8.

</details>
</details>
</details>
