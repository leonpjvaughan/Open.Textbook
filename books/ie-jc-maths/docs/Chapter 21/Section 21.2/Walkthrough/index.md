# 21.2 Probability as a number from 0 to 1 — Learn

**Prerequisites:** fractions, decimals and percentages from Chapter 5.

**Objectives:** place an event on the scale from 0 to 1; write a probability as a
fraction, a decimal or a percentage; use words for chance; estimate a probability
from relative frequency.

By the end of this section you can say how likely an event is with a number, and
you can read a number from an experiment.

## The scale from 0 to 1

A **probability** is a number that measures how likely an event is. Every
probability sits on a scale from 0 to 1.

- A probability of $0$ means the event is **impossible**. It cannot happen.
- A probability of $1$ means the event is **certain**. It must happen.
- A probability of $\frac12$ means the event is as likely to happen as not. This
  is called an **even chance**.

Every probability is between 0 and 1, including the two ends. A number such as
$1.2$ is not a probability, and neither is $-0.1$.

## Words for chance

Words and numbers describe the same scale. People often use words when they do not
know the exact number.

| Word | Roughly where on the scale |
|---|---|
| impossible | $0$ |
| unlikely | between $0$ and $0.5$ |
| even chance | $0.5$ |
| likely | between $0.5$ and $1$ |
| certain | $1$ |

The words are not exact. "Unlikely" covers everything from a value near 0 to a
value close to one half, and two people may disagree about the word for $0.4$.
The number settles the argument.

**Example 1.** Place each event on the scale.

- The sun rises tomorrow: probability $1$, because it is certain.
- A fair die shows a 7: probability $0$, because it is impossible.
- A fair coin shows heads: probability $\frac12$, because heads and tails are
  equally likely.

## Fractions, decimals and percentages

One probability can be written in three ways. They are the same number.

$$\frac14 = 0.25 = 25\%$$

To change a fraction to a decimal, divide the top by the bottom. To change a
decimal to a percentage, multiply by 100.

**Example 2.** Write $\frac38$ as a decimal and as a percentage.

$$3\div8=0.375 \qquad 0.375\times100=37.5\%$$

The value $0.375$ is less than $0.5$, so the event is less likely than not.

## Relative frequency

An experiment gives an estimate of a probability. The estimate is called the
**relative frequency**.

$$\text{relative frequency}=\frac{\text{number of times the event happened}}{\text{number of trials}}$$

**Example 3.** A die is rolled 60 times and shows a 6 on 13 of the rolls. Work out
the relative frequency of a 6.

$$\frac{13}{60}\approx0.217$$

The theoretical probability of a 6 is $\frac16$, which is about $0.167$. The
relative frequency is higher, and that is allowed. The experiment is a sample, and
samples vary.

## Investigate: one six in six rolls?

A fair die has six faces, so the theoretical probability of a 6 is $\frac16$. In
60 rolls you would expect about 10 sixes.

**Predict:** write down how many sixes you think 60 rolls will give. Then write
down how many you think 600 rolls will give.

**Test:** roll a die 60 times and record the number of sixes. If you do not have a
die, use a random number generator to give 60 numbers from 1 to 6.

**Explain:** work out the relative frequency of a 6 in your 60 rolls. Compare it
with $\frac16$. Write a sentence saying why your result is not exactly $\frac16$,
and why 600 rolls would usually give an estimate closer to $\frac16$.

The theoretical probability is a fixed number. The relative frequency comes from
the data, so it moves about. More trials usually bring it closer.

## Watch out

> **Watch out.** A relative frequency is an **estimate** of a probability, not the
> probability itself.
>
> If 13 of 60 rolls are sixes, the relative frequency is $\frac{13}{60}$. That
> does not mean the die has a probability of $\frac{13}{60}$ of showing a 6.
>
> **Re-check:** ask where the number came from. If it came from an experiment, it
> is an estimate. If it came from counting equally likely outcomes, it is the
> theoretical probability.

A second trap is a probability outside the scale. A value of $1.2$ or $-0.1$ is not
a probability, because nothing can be more likely than certain or less likely than
impossible.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem, the open-day game. The star prize needs a 6 and an
A, and its probability is $\frac{1}{24}$.

1. Write $\frac{1}{24}$ as a decimal, to three decimal places.
2. Is the star prize closer to the impossible end or the certain end of the scale?
3. Would you describe the star prize as unlikely or as impossible? Say why.

## You should now be able to

- place an event on the scale from 0 to 1 and say what the two ends mean;
- write one probability as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage;
- use words such as impossible, unlikely, even chance, likely and certain;
- work out a relative frequency from experimental data;
- say why a relative frequency is an estimate and why more trials usually improve
  it.
