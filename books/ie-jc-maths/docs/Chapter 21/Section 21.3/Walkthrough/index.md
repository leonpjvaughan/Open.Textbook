# 21.3 Equally likely outcomes — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the sample space from Section 21.1, and fractions from
Chapter 5.

**Objectives:** decide whether the outcomes of an experiment are equally likely;
find a probability by counting outcomes; simplify the fraction.

By the end of this section you can turn a count of outcomes into a probability.

## Equally likely outcomes

Outcomes are **equally likely** when each one has the same chance as every other.
A fair coin, a fair die and a spinner with equal sectors all give equally likely
outcomes.

The word **fair** is the signal. A fair die is equally likely to show each of its
six faces.

When the outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is a count
divided by a count.

$$P(\text{event})=\frac{\text{number of outcomes in the event}}{\text{total number of outcomes in the sample space}}$$

The denominator is the size of the whole sample space. The numerator is how many
of those outcomes match the event.

## Counting the outcomes

**Example 1.** A fair six-sided die is rolled. Find the probability of an even
number.

The sample space is $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$, so there are 6 equally likely outcomes.
The even numbers are 2, 4 and 6, so 3 outcomes match.

$$P(\text{even})=\frac{3}{6}=\frac12$$

Simplify the fraction at the end. The answer is one half, which is an even chance.

**Example 2.** A spinner has 8 equal sectors, and 3 of them are red. Find the
probability that the spinner lands on red.

The 8 sectors are equal, so the outcomes are equally likely. Three of the 8 are
red.

$$P(\text{red})=\frac{3}{8}$$

The fraction $\frac38$ does not simplify. As a decimal it is $0.375$.

**Example 3.** The open-day game has 24 equally likely outcomes. Find the
probability of the star prize, which needs a 6 and an A.

Only one outcome is a 6 with an A.

$$P(\text{star prize})=\frac{1}{24}$$

As a decimal this is about $0.042$, so the star prize is unlikely.

## When the outcomes are not equally likely

A bag holds 4 green counters and 1 yellow counter. There are two colours, but the
colours are **not** equally likely. Green is more common than yellow.

The 5 counters are equally likely, because each counter is one object. So count
the counters, not the colours:

$$P(\text{green})=\frac{4}{5} \qquad P(\text{yellow})=\frac{1}{5}$$

Writing $\frac12$ for green would be wrong. The two colours do not have the same
number of counters.

## Watch out

> **Watch out.** The formula $\frac{\text{outcomes in the event}}{\text{total outcomes}}$
> works only when the outcomes are equally likely.
>
> A spinner with 3 equal red sectors and 2 equal blue sectors has 5 equally likely
> sectors, so $P(\text{red})=\frac35$. The colours are not equally likely, even
> though there are two colours.
>
> **Re-check:** ask whether each outcome has the same chance. A fair coin and a
> fair die do. A biased coin, a bent spinner and a bag with unequal colours do
> not.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem, the open-day game. The die and the spinner are
both fair, so all 24 outcomes in the sample space are equally likely.

1. How many of the 24 outcomes have the spinner on A?
2. Write the probability that the spinner lands on A, and simplify it.
3. How many outcomes give the star prize? Write its probability.

In Section 21.4 you will find the probability that a player wins nothing, by using
the complement.

## You should now be able to

- say whether the outcomes of an experiment are equally likely;
- find a probability by dividing the number of matching outcomes by the total
  number of outcomes;
- simplify the fraction, and write the probability as a decimal if it helps;
- count outcomes rather than colours when the colours are not equally likely;
- recognise the word **fair** as the signal for equally likely outcomes.
