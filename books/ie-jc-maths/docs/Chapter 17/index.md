# Chapter 17: Coordinate geometry

A map is a grid with two numbers for every place. Give the numbers and you have
found the place exactly. That is the whole idea of coordinate geometry.

This chapter joins algebra to shape. A straight line on a grid has a length, a
midpoint and a slope. Each of those is a number you can work out from the
coordinates. The slope then leads to the equation of the line, and the equation
answers questions about the line without a drawing.

You met the grid before, in Chapter 14, when you drew the graphs of functions.
This chapter uses the same grid to measure and describe the lines themselves.

## The chapter problem

**The park on the grid.** A town is planning a new park on a coordinate grid.
Each unit on the grid is 1 metre. Three places are fixed before the paths are
drawn.

- The gate is at $G(1,2)$.
- The kiosk is at $K(9,8)$.
- The pond is at $P(3,16)$.

A straight main path joins the gate to the kiosk. A bench sits at the midpoint of
the main path. A second straight path leaves the kiosk and reaches the pond.

Before you calculate anything, look at the two paths on the grid. Do you think
they meet at a right angle? Write down your guess.

By the end of the chapter you can find the length of the main path, the place for
the bench, the slope of each path, and the equation of the main path. You will
return to the park in every section, and the Review brings it all together.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- plot a point from its coordinates, and read the coordinates of a point;
- find the distance between two points and the midpoint of a segment;
- find the slope of a line from two points or from a graph;
- decide whether two lines are parallel, perpendicular or neither;
- write the equation of a line as $y=mx+c$, as $y-y_1=m(x-x_1)$, or as
  $ax+by+c=0$;
- read a straight-line graph in context and work out its rate of change.

## Where it is used

Coordinate geometry is the mathematics behind every map app. A phone finds your
position as a pair of numbers and draws the route between two pairs. A delivery
company uses the same ideas to work out distances and to sort stops into an
order.

Builders and designers use slope and coordinates to place a ramp, a roof or a
path at the right angle. Computer graphics uses them to draw every line on a
screen. In science, a straight-line graph turns a table of measurements into a
rate you can read.
