# 17.1 The coordinate plane and plotting points — Learn

**Prerequisites:** negative numbers from Chapter 1, and plotting points from
Section 14.3.

**Objectives:** name a point by its ordered pair; plot a point from its
coordinates; read the coordinates of a point from a grid; name the quadrant a
point lies in; work with a scale on a grid.

By the end of this section, you can place a point on a grid and say exactly where
it is.

## The coordinate plane

A **coordinate plane** is a flat grid made from two number lines.

- The **horizontal axis** runs left to right. It is the $x$-axis.
- The **vertical axis** runs bottom to top. It is the $y$-axis.
- The two axes cross at the **origin**, which is the point $(0,0)$.

A point is named by an **ordered pair** $(x,y)$. The order matters. The first
number is how far across to go, and the second number is how far up to go.

The point $(4,3)$ is 4 across and 3 up. The point $(3,4)$ is 3 across and 4 up.
They are different points.

## The four quadrants

The two axes cut the plane into four regions called **quadrants**. They are
numbered anticlockwise, starting from the top right.

| Quadrant | Sign of $x$ | Sign of $y$ |
|---|---|---|
| First | positive | positive |
| Second | negative | positive |
| Third | negative | negative |
| Fourth | positive | negative |

A point with two positive coordinates lies in the first quadrant. A point with a
negative $x$ and a positive $y$ lies in the second.

## Plotting a point

To plot a point, start at the origin. Move across by the first number, then up by
the second. Mark the point with a small cross.

**Example 1.** Plot the points $A(2,5)$, $B(-3,4)$, $C(-2,-3)$ and $D(4,-1)$.

- $A(2,5)$: 2 to the right, then 5 up. First quadrant.
- $B(-3,4)$: 3 to the left, then 4 up. Second quadrant.
- $C(-2,-3)$: 2 to the left, then 3 down. Third quadrant.
- $D(4,-1)$: 4 to the right, then 1 down. Fourth quadrant.

Each point uses its own pair of signs, so each one sits in a different quadrant.

## Reading a point

Reading a point works in reverse. Start at the point, go straight down to the
$x$-axis to read the first number, then go straight across to the $y$-axis to read
the second.

**Example 2.** A point sits 6 units to the left of the origin and 2 units up. Write
its coordinates.

Moving left makes the $x$-coordinate negative, so $x=-6$. Moving up makes the
$y$-coordinate positive, so $y=2$.

The point is $(-6,2)$.

## Points on the axes

A point on an axis has one coordinate equal to 0.

- A point on the $x$-axis has $y=0$. For example, $(5,0)$ is 5 to the right.
- A point on the $y$-axis has $x=0$. For example, $(0,7)$ is 7 up.

The origin is on both axes, and it is the point $(0,0)$.

**Example 3.** On which axis does the point $(0,-4)$ lie?

The $x$-coordinate is 0, so the point is on the $y$-axis. It is 4 units below the
origin.

## Using a scale

On a real map, one grid square often stands for more than one metre. Read the
**scale** first, then multiply.

**Example 4.** On a grid, one square represents 2 metres. A point is plotted 5
squares to the right of the origin. How far to the right is it?

Each square is 2 m, and there are 5 squares.

$$5 \times 2 = 10$$

The point is 10 m to the right of the origin.

## Plotting a line segment

A **line segment** is a straight piece of a line. It is named by its two
endpoints, such as the segment $AB$ from $A$ to $B$.

To draw a segment, plot both endpoints, then join them with a straight line. The
two endpoints are the only points you need, because a straight line is fixed by
two points.

**Example 5.** Plot $A(1,1)$ and $B(5,4)$, and draw the segment $AB$.

Plot $A$ 1 across and 1 up. Plot $B$ 5 across and 4 up. Join them with a straight
line. The segment goes 4 units across and 3 units up from $A$ to $B$.

## Watch out

The order of the pair is the trap.

> **Watch out.** The point $(2,6)$ and the point $(6,2)$ are different. The common
> error is to move up first and across second, which plots $(6,2)$ in place of
> $(2,6)$.
>
> The first number always goes across. The second number always goes up or down.
>
> **Re-check:** say the pair aloud as "across, then up" before you plot it. For
> $(2,6)$: 2 across, then 6 up.

A second trap is the scale. If one square stands for 2 m, then 5 squares is 10 m,
not 5 m. Read the scale before you read any coordinates.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem. The park gate is at $G(1,2)$, the kiosk is at
$K(9,8)$ and the pond is at $P(3,16)$.

1. Plot the three points on a grid that runs from 0 to 16 on both axes.
2. Write the quadrant each point lies in.
3. A signpost stands 5 m to the right of the gate and 3 m above it. Write the
   coordinates of the signpost.

In Section 17.2 you will measure the main path from the gate to the kiosk.

## You should now be able to

- name a point by its ordered pair $(x,y)$, with $x$ across and $y$ up;
- plot a point by moving across first and then up or down;
- read the coordinates of a point from a grid;
- name the quadrant from the signs of the coordinates;
- tell a point on the $x$-axis from a point on the $y$-axis;
- use the scale of a grid to turn squares into metres;
- plot a line segment from its two endpoints.
