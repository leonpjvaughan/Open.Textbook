# 11.4 Common factors and factorising — Learn

**Prerequisites:** multiplying out a single bracket from Section 11.2, and finding the
factors of a number from Section 3.1.

**Objectives:** find the highest common factor of the terms in an expression;
factorise by taking out the highest common factor; check a factorisation by expanding
it again.

By the end of this section, you can write an expression as a product of a common
factor and a bracket.

## Factorising is expanding backwards

Expanding turns a product into a sum. **Factorising** turns a sum back into a product.
The two are opposites.

$$3(x+4) \xrightarrow{\ \text{expand}\ } 3x + 12 \xrightarrow{\ \text{factorise}\ } 3(x+4)$$

A **factor** is a number or expression that divides another exactly. To factorise, you
look for a factor that every term shares.

## The highest common factor

The **highest common factor** (HCF) of the terms is the largest factor they all share.
Take it out and write it in front of a bracket.

**Example 1.** Factorise $6x+9$.

Both terms divide by 3, and 3 is the highest common factor.

$$6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)$$

Check by expanding: $3(2x+3)=6x+9$.

**Example 2.** Factorise $4x^2+8x$.

Both terms divide by 4 and by $x$. The highest common factor is $4x$.

$$4x^2 + 8x = 4x(x + 2)$$

Check: $4x(x+2)=4x^2+8x$.

**Example 3.** Factorise $5a^2b+10ab^2$.

The numbers 5 and 10 share the factor 5. Both terms contain $a$ and $b$. The highest
common factor is $5ab$.

$$5a^2b + 10ab^2 = 5ab(a + 2b)$$

**Example 4.** Factorise $x^2+x$.

Both terms divide by $x$.

$$x^2 + x = x(x + 1)$$

The second term is $x$, which is $x\times1$, so the 1 appears in the bracket.

> **Watch out.** When every term divides by the factor, the bracket still needs a term
> for each. From $x^2+x$, the common error is to write $x(x)$, leaving out the 1. The
> correct factorisation is $x(x+1)$.
>
> **Re-check:** expand the bracket again. $x(x+1)=x^2+x$, which is the expression you
> started with.
