# 11.6 The difference of two squares — Learn

**Prerequisites:** multiplying out two brackets from Section 11.3, and factorising a
quadratic from Section 11.5.

**Objectives:** recognise a difference of two squares; factorise $a^2-b^2$ as
$(a+b)(a-b)$; use the rule when a square has a coefficient.

By the end of this section, you can factorise an expression that is one square take
away another square.

## A pattern in the expansion

Expand $(x+4)(x-4)$.

$$(x+4)(x-4) = x^2 - 4x + 4x - 16 = x^2 - 16$$

The two middle terms are opposites, so they cancel. What is left is one square take
away another.

This works for any two terms.

$$(a+b)(a-b) = a^2 - b^2$$

Read it backwards to factorise.

$$a^2 - b^2 = (a+b)(a-b)$$

## Factorising a difference of two squares

Look for two terms that are both squares, with a minus sign between them.

**Example 1.** Factorise $x^2-9$.

Both terms are squares: $x^2$ and $3^2$. So $a=x$ and $b=3$.

$$x^2 - 9 = (x+3)(x-3)$$

**Example 2.** Factorise $x^2-25$.

$$x^2 - 25 = (x+5)(x-5)$$

**Example 3.** Factorise $x^2-100$.

$$x^2 - 100 = (x+10)(x-10)$$

## When a square has a coefficient

The first term can be a square with a coefficient.

**Example 4.** Factorise $4x^2-1$.

$4x^2$ is $(2x)^2$, and 1 is $1^2$. So $a=2x$ and $b=1$.

$$4x^2 - 1 = (2x+1)(2x-1)$$

**Example 5.** Factorise $9x^2-16$.

$9x^2$ is $(3x)^2$, and 16 is $4^2$.

$$9x^2 - 16 = (3x+4)(3x-4)$$

**Example 6.** Factorise $x^4-81$.

$x^4$ is $(x^2)^2$, and 81 is $9^2$.

$$x^4 - 81 = (x^2+9)(x^2-9) = (x^2+9)(x+3)(x-3)$$

The second bracket is itself a difference of two squares, so it factorises again.

> **Watch out.** The rule needs a minus sign between the squares. The expression
> $x^2+9$ has a plus sign, so it is not a difference of two squares. It does not
> factorise with whole numbers.
>
> **Re-check:** expand the two brackets and see whether the middle terms cancel. For
> $(x+3)(x-3)$ they do, which leaves $x^2-9$. With a plus sign, the middle terms would
> not cancel.

square flower bed of side $(x-2)$ metres is cut from it.

1. Write the area of the patio as $(x+2)^2-(x-2)^2$.
2. Use the difference of two squares to factorise it.
3. Simplify the factorised form.

## You should now be able to

- recognise a difference of two squares;
- factorise $a^2-b^2$ as $(a+b)(a-b)$;
- factorise an expression such as $9x^2-16$;
- factorise again when one bracket is still a difference of two squares.
