# Chapter 11: Expanding, factorising and simplifying

Two expressions can look different and still be equal. The product $(x+5)(x+2)$ and
the sum $x^2+7x+10$ describe the same rectangle. Expanding turns the product into the
sum, and factorising turns the sum back into the product. In this chapter you learn
both directions, and you learn to simplify an expression by collecting its like terms.

## The chapter problem

**The school garden plot.** The garden club has a rectangular plot. Its length is
$(x+5)$ metres and its width is $(x+2)$ metres. A square pond of side $x$ metres sits
in one corner, and the rest of the plot is planted.

The planted area is 45 square metres. The club also plans a square patio of side
$(x+2)$ metres, with a square flower bed of side $(x-2)$ metres cut from it.

Before you calculate anything, write down a prediction. Do you think $x$ is greater
than 5 or less than 5? Do you think the plot is longer than 10 metres?

By the end of the chapter you can expand the area of the plot, subtract the pond, and
find $x$ and the dimensions of the plot. You will return to this garden in every
section.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- collect like terms to simplify an expression;
- add and subtract algebraic expressions, including expressions in brackets;
- multiply out a single bracket;
- multiply out two brackets and collect the middle terms;
- take out the highest common factor from an expression;
- factorise a quadratic expression of the form $x^2+bx+c$;
- recognise and factorise the difference of two squares;

## Where it is used

Every rule that connects two amounts can be written in more than one form. A builder
works out the area of a floor as a product, and a spreadsheet works out the same area
as a sum. Engineers, scientists and programmers change the form of an expression to
suit the question in front of them. Expanding and factorising are the two directions
of that change, and simplifying is what makes the answer readable.

## Your turn to connect it

Think of a rectangle you know: a table top, a phone screen, a garden bed or a football
pitch. Write down an expression for its area, and then write the same area as a sum of
parts. If you can write the same area in two ways, you have expanded an expression and
factorised it back again.
