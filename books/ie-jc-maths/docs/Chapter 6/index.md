# Chapter 6: Ratio, proportion and rates

Some questions compare two amounts rather than counting one. A recipe for four
people, a map, a price rise and a speed limit are all comparisons of this kind. In
this chapter you learn to write a comparison as a ratio, to share an amount in a
given ratio, and to work with two quantities that grow together or in opposite
directions.

## The chapter problem

**The school tour.** Your year group is going on a tour to a museum. The coach
holds 53 seats and costs 480 euro to hire. There are 48 students and 4 teachers
going. The two classes share the coach cost in the ratio of their numbers: Class A
has 20 students and Class B has 28. The museum is 96 km away, and the journey
takes 1 hour 30 minutes. Each packed lunch costs 3.50 euro. Last year the same
coach cost 450 euro.

By the end of the chapter you can work out how much each student pays altogether,
and how this year's tour compares with last year's. You will meet this tour again
in every section, and you will solve it on the Review page.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- read and write a ratio, and explain what the order of its parts means;
- simplify a ratio by dividing every part by a common factor;
- divide a quantity in a given ratio, and check that the shares add back to the
  whole;
- recognise direct proportion and inverse proportion, and use each one;
- compare two changes using an absolute change and a relative change;
- use the relationship between speed, distance and time, and keep the units
  consistent.

## Where it is used

Sharing a bill, scaling a recipe, reading a map and mixing paint all use ratio.
Prices per kilogram, pay per hour and speed are rates, and a rate is a ratio with
units. Direct and inverse proportion describe how two quantities change together:
the more hours you work, the more you earn; the more people who share a taxi, the
less each pays. Percentages let you compare changes on amounts of different sizes,
which is how shops, banks and newspapers report them.
