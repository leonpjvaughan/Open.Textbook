# 2.2 The laws of indices — Learn

**Prerequisites:** index form and the value of a power, from Section 2.1.
Multiplication and division of whole numbers.

**Objectives:** multiply powers with the same base; divide powers with the same
base; raise a power to a power; raise a product to a power.

By the end of this section, you can simplify a product, a quotient or a power of
a power, and say which law you used.

## Multiplying powers with the same base

Write the product out and count the factors.

$$2^3\times2^2 = (2\times2\times2)\times(2\times2) = 2^5$$

Three copies of 2 and two more copies of 2 make five copies of 2. The indices add.

$$a^m\times a^n = a^{m+n}$$

**Example 1.** Simplify $3^4\times3^2$.

$$3^4\times3^2 = 3^{4+2} = 3^6$$

Check the value: $3^6 = 729$, and $3^4\times3^2 = 81\times9 = 729$. Both routes
agree.

## Dividing powers with the same base

Dividing cancels matching factors.

$$\frac{2^5}{2^2} = \frac{2\times2\times2\times2\times2}{2\times2} = 2^3$$

Five copies on top and two underneath leave three. The indices subtract. The base
must not be zero, because you cannot divide by zero.

$$a^m\div a^n = a^{m-n}\quad(a\ne0)$$

**Example 2.** Simplify $5^7\div5^4$.

$$5^7\div5^4 = 5^{7-4} = 5^3 = 125$$

Check: $5^7 = 78125$ and $5^4 = 625$, and $78125\div625 = 125$.

## A power raised to a power

$(2^3)^2$ means two copies of $2^3$ multiplied together.

$$(2^3)^2 = 2^3\times2^3 = 2^6$$

Each copy contributes three factors, so two copies contribute six. The indices
multiply.

$$(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$$

**Example 3.** Simplify $(10^4)^3$.

$$(10^4)^3 = 10^{4\times3} = 10^{12}$$

That is a 1 followed by twelve zeros.

## A product raised to a power

$(2\times5)^3$ means three copies of the product.

$$(2\times5)^3 = (2\times5)(2\times5)(2\times5) = 2^3\times5^3$$

Every factor inside the bracket is raised to the power.

$$(ab)^n = a^n b^n$$

**Example 4.** Simplify $(2x)^4$.

$$(2x)^4 = 2^4 x^4 = 16x^4$$

## Look at the brackets first

> **Watch out.** Check the brackets before you touch the indices. In
> $a^2\times a^3$ the powers are multiplied, so the indices **add**: $a^5$. In
> $(a^2)^3$ one power is raised to another, so the indices **multiply**: $a^6$.
> Small numbers settle which is which: $2^2\times2^3 = 4\times8 = 32 = 2^5$, while
> $(2^2)^3 = 4^3 = 64 = 2^6$.

**Example 5.** Simplify $\dfrac{4^5\times4^3}{4^6}$.

Add the indices on top first: $4^5\times4^3 = 4^8$. Then subtract:
$4^8\div4^6 = 4^2 = 16$.

## Test the laws

:::figure index-laws-practice

**Predict, then test.** Before you answer, say out loud which law you will use. Then
work the index out first — write $2^3 \times 2^4$ as $2^7$ in your head — and only then
work out the number.

**Explain.** When you get one wrong, do not move on. Write down which of these happened:
you added the indices when you should have multiplied them, you multiplied when you
should have added them, or the arithmetic was right and the power was wrong. The three
mistakes have three different fixes.

A new question arrives every time you ask for one, so you can stay on this until you
are getting three in a row.

## Progress check

Back to the photocopier. The 141% button multiplies the width by $\sqrt2$, which
you will meet properly in Section 2.4. For now, write $\sqrt2$ as $2^{\frac12}$.

1. Press 141% twice. Use the power law to work out $\left(2^{\frac12}\right)^2$.
   What does that say about the width after two presses?
2. The area is multiplied by 2 each press. After two presses, write the area
   multiplier as a power of 2, and work out its value.
3. An A2 sheet is twice as wide and twice as tall as an A4 sheet, so its area is
   four times as big. Does your answer to question 2 agree?

## You should now be able to

- multiply powers with the same base by adding the indices;
- divide powers with the same base by subtracting the indices;
- raise a power to a power by multiplying the indices;
- raise a product to a power;
- decide which law a question needs by looking at the brackets.
