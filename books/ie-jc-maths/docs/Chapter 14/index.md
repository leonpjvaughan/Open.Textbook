# Chapter 14: Functions and graphs

A function is a rule that takes an input and gives exactly one output. When you put
petrol in a car, the cost depends on the number of litres. When you throw a ball, its
height depends on the time. A graph shows that rule as a picture, so you can see the
whole story at once.

## The chapter problem

**The garden bed against the wall.** The school garden club has **12 m of fencing**
for a rectangular flower bed. One long side of the bed is the school wall, so only
three sides need fencing. The club calls the width of the bed $x$ metres.

The two widths use $2x$ metres of fence, so the fenced length is $(12-2x)$ metres. The
area of the bed is

$$A = x(12-2x)$$

Before you calculate anything, make a prediction and write it down. Do you think the
biggest area comes from a bed that is wide and short, or from one that is narrow and
long? Write down a width you think gives the biggest area.

By the end of the chapter you can graph the area, find the width that gives the
biggest area, and say what that area is. You will return to this bed in every section.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- say what a function is, and explain why one rule is a function and another is not;
- name the input, the output, the domain and the range of a function;
- use function notation such as $f(x)$ and find the output for a given input;
- find the input that gives a chosen output;
- draw the graph of a linear function from a table of values, and read values from it;
- draw the graph of a quadratic function and read its roots and its highest or lowest
  point;
- read a graph in context and say what its shape tells you.

## Where it is used

Graphs are everywhere. A phone bill, a car journey, a cooling cup of tea and the
height of a thrown ball are all functions, and their graphs show the pattern at a
glance. Scientists plot data to spot a rule, and engineers use graphs to find the
largest value or the smallest cost. Reading a graph is how you turn a table of numbers
into an answer to a question.
