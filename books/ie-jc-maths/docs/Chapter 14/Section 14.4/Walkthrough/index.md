# 14.4 Graphing quadratic functions — Learn

**Prerequisites:** plotting points and drawing a graph from Section 14.3, multiplying
two brackets from Section 11.3, and factorising a quadratic from Section 11.5.

**Objectives:** make a table of values for a quadratic function; draw the curve; find
the roots from the graph; read the highest or lowest point; build a quadratic from its
roots.

By the end of this section, you can draw the graph of a quadratic function and read its
roots and its highest or lowest point.

## The simplest quadratic

A **quadratic function** has an $x^2$ in its rule. The simplest one is $y=x^2$.

**Example 1.** Make a table of values for $y=x^2$ for $x=-3$, $-2$, $-1$, $0$, $1$,
$2$ and $3$.

| $x$ | $-3$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $y$ | 9 | 4 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 4 | 9 |

Plot the points. The graph is a smooth curve, not a set of straight segments. It is
called a **parabola**, and it looks like a letter U.

The lowest point is $(0,0)$. It is the **minimum** of the graph, and it is where the
curve turns around.

## The curve is symmetrical

Look at the table again. The value for $x=-2$ is 4, and the value for $x=2$ is also 4.
The value for $x=-3$ is 9, and so is the value for $x=3$.

The left side of the curve is a mirror image of the right side. The curve is
**symmetrical** about the vertical line through its lowest point. For $y=x^2$ that line
is the vertical axis.

A square hides the sign of its input, so $(-2)^2$ and $2^2$ are equal. That is where
the symmetry comes from.

## The roots

A **root** of a function is an input for which the output is 0. On the graph, a root
is a place where the curve crosses the horizontal axis.

For $y=x^2-4$, the roots are the solutions of

$$x^2-4 = 0$$

$$x^2 = 4$$

$$x = -2 \quad \text{or} \quad x = 2$$

The curve crosses the horizontal axis at $x=-2$ and at $x=2$. Between those two values
the curve is below the axis, so the output is negative there.

## Building a quadratic from its roots

If you know the roots, you can write down the function.

A root at $x=2$ means that the output is 0 when the input is 2. The bracket $(x-2)$ is
0 at $x=2$, so $(x-2)$ is a factor. In the same way, a root at $x=5$ gives the factor
$(x-5)$.

**Example 2.** A quadratic has roots 2 and 5. Write its rule and expand it.

$$y = (x-2)(x-5)$$

Multiply the two brackets.

$$y = x^2-7x+10$$

Check: at $x=2$, $4-14+10=0$. At $x=5$, $25-35+10=0$. Both roots work.

**Example 3.** A quadratic has roots $-3$ and $1$. Write its rule and expand it.

A root at $-3$ gives the factor $(x+3)$, because substituting $-3$ makes that bracket
zero.

$$y = (x+3)(x-1) = x^2+2x-3$$

## A quadratic that opens downwards

When the number in front of $x^2$ is negative, the curve opens downwards. Its highest
point is the **maximum** of the graph.

The garden bed has a rule like this. Its area is

$$A(x) = 12x-2x^2$$

The number in front of $x^2$ is $-2$, so the curve opens downwards and has a maximum.

**Example 4.** Make a table for $A(x)=12x-2x^2$ for $x=0$, $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, $5$ and
$6$.

| $x$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $A(x)$ | 0 | 10 | 16 | 18 | 16 | 10 | 0 |

Plot the points and join them with a smooth curve. The curve rises from $(0,0)$ to a
highest point, and then falls back to $(6,0)$.

- The greatest area is $18\ \text{m}^2$, at a width of 3 m. That is the maximum point.
- The roots are 0 and 6, where the curve crosses the horizontal axis.
- The curve is symmetrical about the vertical line through the maximum, so the widths
  2 m and 4 m give the same area, and the widths 1 m and 5 m do the same.

**Example 5.** Find the greatest area from the table.

The largest value in the bottom row is 18, at $x=3$.

$$A(3) = 12\times3-2\times3^2 = 36-18 = 18$$

The greatest area is $18\ \text{m}^2$, and the width that gives it is 3 m. The fenced
length is then $12-6=6$ m.

> **Watch out.** A quadratic graph is a smooth curve, not a line and not a set of
> straight segments between the points.
>
> The common error is to join the points of the garden-bed table with straight lines.
> That makes a pointed shape with a corner at the top. The real curve is rounded at the
> maximum.
>
> **Re-check:** the curve must pass through every point of the table, and it must be
> symmetrical. The value at $x=2$ and the value at $x=4$ are both 16, so the curve is
> the same height on both sides of the maximum.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem. The area of the garden bed is $A(x)=12x-2x^2$ square
metres.

1. Copy and complete the table for $x=0$, $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, $5$ and $6$.
2. Plot the points and draw the smooth curve.
3. Read the greatest area from the graph, and the width that gives it.
4. Read the two roots from the graph, and say what they mean for the bed.

In Section 14.5 you will read this graph as the story of the garden bed, and compare it
with other graphs from real situations.

## You should now be able to

- make a table of values for a quadratic function;
- draw the smooth curve of a quadratic function;
- read the roots from the graph, and explain what a root is;
- read the maximum or the minimum from the graph;
- build a quadratic from its roots and expand it;
- explain why the curve is symmetrical.
