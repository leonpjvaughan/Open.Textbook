# 14.5 Interpreting graphs in context — Learn

**Prerequisites:** reading a value from a graph from Section 14.3, and the maximum and
roots of a quadratic graph from Section 14.4.

**Objectives:** read the shape of a graph as the story of a situation; work out a rate
from a graph; find a maximum or a minimum; find where two graphs meet; give an
approximate reading.

By the end of this section, you can read a graph in context and say what its shape
tells you.

## A graph tells a story

In a real problem the axes carry the two amounts. The horizontal axis is often time,
and the vertical axis is the amount that changes.

A **distance-time graph** is the clearest example.

- A steep line means the object is moving fast.
- A flat line means the object is not moving, because the distance is not changing.
- A line that goes down means the object is coming back towards the start.

**Example 1.** A car travels 60 km in the first hour, stops for an hour, and then
travels another 40 km in one hour.

The graph goes up steeply for the first hour, is flat for the second hour, and then
goes up less steeply. The flat part is the stop. The second part is less steep than the
first, because the car covers 40 km in an hour rather than 60 km.

## Investigate a journey graph

**Predict.** Before you move the controls, predict what the graph will do while the car
is stopped, and whether the last stage will be steeper or less steep than the first.

:::figure journey-graph

**Test.** Set the first speed to 60 km/h, the stop to 1 hour and the last speed to
40 km/h. Then set the stop to 0 and watch the flat part disappear. Finally make the last
speed larger than the first and look at the two slopes.

**Explain.** Write two or three sentences. Say what the flat part of the graph means,
and why a steeper line means a faster stage.

## A rate from a straight line

A straight-line graph changes at a steady rate. The rate is the change in the vertical
amount divided by the change in the horizontal amount.

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\text{change in the vertical amount}}{\text{change in the horizontal amount}}$$

For a distance-time graph this rate is the speed.

**Example 2.** Find the speed during the first hour of the journey above.

$$\text{speed} = \frac{60\ \text{km}}{1\ \text{hour}} = 60\ \text{km/h}$$

**Example 3.** A tap fills a tank with 200 litres in 4 minutes. Find the rate.

$$\text{rate} = \frac{200\ \text{litres}}{4\ \text{minutes}} = 50\ \text{litres per minute}$$

The graph of the volume against the time is a straight line, because the water flows at
a steady rate.

## Curves and changing rate

When the rate changes, the graph is a curve rather than a straight line.

A cup of water cooling from $90^\circ$C to $20^\circ$C over 7 minutes falls by
$70^\circ$C in 7 minutes. That is an average rate of

$$\frac{70}{7} = 10^\circ\text{C per minute}$$

The real cooling is faster at the start and slower later, so the graph is a curve that
falls quickly and then levels out. The average rate is the change in the temperature
divided by the time, which is the rate you would get from a straight line between the
first and last points.

## Maximum and minimum points

The highest point of a graph is the **maximum**, and the lowest point is the
**minimum**. In a real problem, the maximum or the minimum is often the answer.

**Example 4.** A ball is thrown straight up. Its height in metres after $t$ seconds is
$h(t)=20t-5t^2$. Make a table for $t=0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ and $4$.

| $t$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $h(t)$ | 0 | 15 | 20 | 15 | 0 |

The graph rises from 0 to a maximum of 20 m, and then falls back to 0. The ball reaches
its greatest height of 20 m after 2 seconds, and it lands after 4 seconds.

The curve is symmetrical, so the ball is at 15 m on the way up at $t=1$ and at 15 m on
the way down at $t=3$.

## Where two graphs meet

When two graphs are drawn on the same axes, the point where they cross is the input for
which the two functions give the same output. That input is the solution of
$f(x)=g(x)$.

**Example 5.** The graphs of $y=2x-1$ and $y=x+2$ cross at $x=3$. Check this.

$$2\times3-1 = 5 \qquad 3+2 = 5$$

Both graphs have height 5 at $x=3$, so the point $(3,5)$ is on both lines. The crossing
point is the solution of $2x-1=x+2$.

## Reading a graph approximately

A graph read from a grid gives an approximate answer. If the point lies between two
grid lines, the honest answer is a value between them.

**Example 6.** The graph of $y=x^2-3$ is a curve. Read the value of $y$ when $x=1.5$.

The point is halfway between $x=1$ and $x=2$ on the grid. Work it out to check your
reading.

$$1.5^2-3 = 2.25-3 = -0.75$$

So the reading is about $-0.75$, which lies between $-1$ and $-0.5$.

## The garden bed graph

The graph of the area of the garden bed is $A(x)=12x-2x^2$ square metres.

- The maximum is $18\ \text{m}^2$, at a width of 3 m. This answers the club's question:
  the biggest bed has a width of 3 m and an area of $18\ \text{m}^2$.
- The roots are 0 and 6. An area of 0 means no bed at all, which happens at a width of
  0 m and at a width of 6 m.
- The graph is above the horizontal axis between the two roots, so every width from 0 m
  to 6 m gives a positive area.

> **Watch out.** The maximum of a graph is the highest **point**, not the largest input.
>
> For the garden bed, the common error is to say that the greatest width, 6 m, gives
> the greatest area. The width 6 m gives an area of 0. The greatest area is at the
> highest point of the curve, which is a width of 3 m.
>
> **Re-check:** read the height of the graph, not the position along the horizontal
> axis. The highest point is at $(3,18)$, so the answer is a width of 3 m and an area of
> $18\ \text{m}^2$.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem. The area of the garden bed is $A(x)=12x-2x^2$ square
metres.

1. Read the maximum point from the graph of $A$.
2. Say what the width and the area are at that point.
3. Read the two roots, and explain what each one means for the bed.
4. The club wants the greatest possible area. What width should it build, and what area
   will it get?

The Review brings the width, the length and the area together for the finished bed.

## You should now be able to

- read a distance-time graph and say what its flat and steep parts mean;
- work out a rate from a straight-line graph;
- read a maximum or a minimum from a graph and say what it means;
- find the input where two graphs meet;
- give an approximate reading from a graph, between two values;
- explain what the roots of the garden-bed graph mean.
