# 18.1 Pythagoras' theorem — Learn

**Prerequisites:** square numbers from Chapter 3, square roots, and the meaning of a
right angle.

**Objectives:** name the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle; state Pythagoras'
theorem; use the theorem to check whether a triangle contains a right angle.

By the end of this section, you can write down the rule that links the three sides
of a right-angled triangle, and use it to test a triangle.

## The right-angled triangle

A **right-angled triangle** has one angle of 90°. The right angle is marked with a
small square.

The longest side is called the **hypotenuse**. It is always the side opposite the
right angle. The other two sides are the **legs**.

## The theorem

Pythagoras' theorem says:

> In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the
> squares of the other two sides.

If the legs are $a$ and $b$, and the hypotenuse is $c$, then

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$

The squares are real areas. A square drawn on the hypotenuse has the same area as
the two squares drawn on the legs, added together.

**Example 1.** A right-angled triangle has legs of 3 cm and 4 cm. Find the area of
the square on the hypotenuse.

The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle. Put the two legs into the theorem.

$$c^2 = 3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$$

The square on the hypotenuse has an area of 25 cm². The hypotenuse itself is
$\sqrt{25}=5$ cm.

## Checking for a right angle

The theorem works in reverse. If the two shorter sides squared add to the longest
side squared, then the triangle is right-angled.

**Example 2.** A triangle has sides of 6 m, 8 m and 10 m. Is it right-angled?

The longest side is 10 m, so it would be the hypotenuse.

$$6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100$$

$$10^2 = 100$$

The two values match, so the triangle is right-angled.

**Example 3.** A triangle has sides of 4 cm, 5 cm and 6 cm. Is it right-angled?

$$4^2 + 5^2 = 16 + 25 = 41$$

$$6^2 = 36$$

Here $41 \neq 36$, so the triangle is not right-angled.

## Watch out

> **Watch out.** The hypotenuse is always the longest side, and it is always
> opposite the right angle. The common error is to add the hypotenuse into the sum,
> as in $5^2+4^2=3^2$ for a 3–4–5 triangle.
>
> The hypotenuse goes on its own side of the equation. For a 3–4–5 triangle the true
> statement is $3^2+4^2=5^2$.
>
> **Re-check:** find the longest side first. It must be the hypotenuse, and it must
> sit alone.

A second trap is forgetting to square each side. The theorem is about areas, so the
numbers are squared before they are added.

The top is 15 m away in a straight line, and the height is $h$.

## You should now be able to

- name the hypotenuse as the side opposite the right angle;
- state Pythagoras' theorem as $a^2+b^2=c^2$;
- square the two legs and add them to find the square of the hypotenuse;
- test whether a triangle is right-angled by comparing the two values;
- keep the hypotenuse alone on one side of the equation.
