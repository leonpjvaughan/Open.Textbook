# 18.1 Pythagoras' theorem — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Work out $6^2$ and $9^2$.
2. Work out $\sqrt{49}$ and $\sqrt{144}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $36$ and $81$. Squaring means multiplying the number by itself.
2. $7$ and $12$. The square root asks which number squared gives the value inside.

Both skills return in this section. The theorem squares the sides and adds them,
and you may need a square root at the end.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A right-angled triangle has legs of 3 cm and 4 cm. Write the equation for
Pythagoras' theorem, with the hypotenuse called $c$, and find $c$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The hypotenuse is the missing side, so it sits alone. The two legs are added.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$c^2 = 3^2+4^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$c^2 = 3^2+4^2 = 9+16 = 25$$

$$c = \sqrt{25} = 5$$

The hypotenuse is 5 cm.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Find the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle with legs of 6 m and 8 m.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Square both legs, add, then take the square root.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$c^2 = 6^2+8^2 = 36+64$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$c^2 = 36+64 = 100$$

$$c = \sqrt{100} = 10$$

The hypotenuse is 10 m.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Find the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle with legs of 5 cm and 12 cm.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

This is another whole-number answer. Look for a square number.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$c^2 = 5^2+12^2 = 25+144$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$c^2 = 169$$

$$c = \sqrt{169} = 13$$

The hypotenuse is 13 cm.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A triangle has sides of 7 cm, 24 cm and 25 cm. Is it right-angled?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The longest side is the possible hypotenuse. Square the two shorter sides and add
them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$7^2+24^2 = 49+576$, and $25^2 = 625$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$7^2+24^2 = 49+576 = 625$$

$$25^2 = 625$$

The two values match, so the triangle is right-angled.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A triangle has sides of 4 m, 6 m and 8 m. Is it right-angled?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare $4^2+6^2$ with $8^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$16+36 = 52$, and $8^2 = 64$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$4^2+6^2 = 16+36 = 52$$

$$8^2 = 64$$

Here $52\neq64$, so the triangle is not right-angled.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A square has sides of 9 cm. A diagonal splits it into two right-angled
triangles. Find the length $d$ of the diagonal, correct to one decimal place.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The diagonal is the hypotenuse. Each leg of the triangle is a side of the square.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$d^2 = 9^2+9^2 = 81+81$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$d^2 = 162$$

$$d = \sqrt{162} = 12.727\ldots$$

The diagonal is 12.7 cm, to one decimal place.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**7.** In a right-angled triangle, which side is the hypotenuse, and why?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the position of the right angle.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle. It is also the longest side of
the triangle, because the right angle opens wider than either acute angle.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** The chapter problem. You stand 12 m from the school building. The top is
15 m away in a straight line, and the height is $h$. Write the equation that links
$h$, 12 and 15.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The 15 m line is the longest side, so it is the hypotenuse.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The height and the 12 m are the two legs, so $h^2+12^2=15^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$h^2+12^2=15^2$$

So $h^2+144=225$. You will solve this in Section 18.2.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from an earlier section

**9.** Round 4.67 to one decimal place.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look at the second decimal digit. It decides whether the first decimal goes up.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The digit after the first decimal is 7, which is 5 or more.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$4.7$. The hundredths digit is 7, so you round the tenths digit up from 6 to 7.

Rounding comes back in this chapter, because many lengths are not whole numbers.

</details>
</details>
</details>
