# 19.2 Axial symmetry and reflection — Learn

**Prerequisites:** reading coordinates from Chapter 17, and translation from
Section 19.1.

**Objectives:** explain what axial symmetry means; reflect a point or a shape in
the $x$-axis, the $y$-axis, the line $y=x$, or a line such as $x=2$ or $y=1$.

By the end of this section you can flip any shape over a line and write down
where every point lands.

## Axial symmetry

**Axial symmetry** means line symmetry. A shape has axial symmetry when a line
divides it into two halves that are mirror images. The line is an **axis of
symmetry**, or a **line of symmetry**.

A butterfly has axial symmetry. So does the letter A, if you draw it in block
capitals. Fold the shape along the axis and the two halves fit exactly.

## Reflection

A **reflection** flips a shape over a line. The line is the **mirror line**. Each
point and its image are the same perpendicular distance from the mirror line, on
opposite sides.

The mirror line is the **perpendicular bisector** of the segment joining a point
to its image. That means the line cuts the segment in half at a right angle.

A reflection keeps lengths and angles the same, so the image is congruent to the
object. A reflection does change the way the shape faces: it reverses the
orientation. A clockwise label on the object becomes anticlockwise on the image.

## Reflecting in the axes

The two coordinate axes are the easiest mirror lines.

| Mirror line | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $x$-axis | $(x,y)\to(x,-y)$ | $(3,2)\to(3,-2)$ |
| $y$-axis | $(x,y)\to(-x,y)$ | $(3,2)\to(-3,2)$ |
| $y=x$ | $(x,y)\to(y,x)$ | $(2,5)\to(5,2)$ |

A reflection in the $x$-axis changes the sign of the $y$-coordinate. A reflection
in the $y$-axis changes the sign of the $x$-coordinate. A reflection in the line
$y=x$ swaps the two coordinates.

**Example 1.** Reflect the point $P(3,2)$ in the $y$-axis.

The $y$-axis is the mirror line, so the $x$-coordinate changes sign.

$$P(3,2)\to(-3,2)$$

The image is $(-3,2)$. The point $P$ is 3 units right of the axis, and its image
is 3 units left.

**Example 2.** Reflect the point $P(2,5)$ in the line $y=x$.

The line $y=x$ runs at 45°, so the coordinates swap.

$$P(2,5)\to(5,2)$$

The image is $(5,2)$.

## Reflecting in other lines

For a vertical line $x=k$, the image is the same distance on the other side. The
rule is

$$(x,y)\to(2k-x,\ y)$$

For a horizontal line $y=k$, the rule is

$$(x,y)\to(x,\ 2k-y)$$

**Example 3.** Reflect the point $P(4,1)$ in the line $x=2$.

The point is 2 units right of the line, so the image is 2 units left.

$$P(4,1)\to(2\times2-4,\ 1)=(0,1)$$

The image is $(0,1)$.

**Example 4.** Reflect the point $P(1,6)$ in the line $y=3$.

The point is 3 units above the line, so the image is 3 units below.

$$P(1,6)\to(1,\ 2\times3-6)=(1,0)$$

The image is $(1,0)$.

## Watch out

> **Watch out.** A reflection in the $y$-axis is not the same as a reflection in
> the line $y=x$. The common error is to swap the coordinates when the mirror
> line is the $y$-axis.
>
> For the $y$-axis, the image of $(3,2)$ is $(-3,2)$: only the $x$-coordinate
> changes sign. For the line $y=x$, the image of $(3,2)$ is $(2,3)$: the two
> coordinates swap.
>
> **Re-check:** find the mirror line first. A vertical line changes the
> $x$-coordinate; the diagonal line $y=x$ swaps the pair.

A second trap is the distance. The image is the same distance from the line as
the point, but on the other side. For the line $x=2$, the point $(4,1)$ is
2 units away, so the image is at $x=0$, not at $x=-4$.

## You should now be able to

- explain what axial symmetry means;
- reflect a point in the $x$-axis or the $y$-axis by changing one sign;
- reflect a point in the line $y=x$ by swapping the coordinates;
- reflect a point in a vertical or horizontal line using $2k-x$ or $2k-y$;
- reflect every vertex of a shape and keep the image congruent;
- remember that a reflection reverses the orientation of a shape.
