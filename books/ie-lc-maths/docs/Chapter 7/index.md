# Chapter 7: Differentiation

A moving car has a speed, and a tank being filled has a rate. Both describe how fast a
quantity is changing, and both are found with one tool. That tool is **differentiation**.
The value tells you how much there is. The derivative tells you how fast it is changing
right now.

This chapter builds the derivative from first principles, then collects the rules that make
it quick to use. It finishes by reading what the derivative says about a graph: where a
curve rises and falls, where it turns, and where it bends.

## The chapter problem

**When is the tank filling fastest?** Water flows into a tank. After $t$ minutes the volume
of water in the tank is

$$V(t) = 12t^2 - t^3 \text{ litres}, \qquad 0 \le t \le 8.$$

The tank is empty at $t = 0$. The rate at which the volume changes is the derivative
$V'(t)$, measured in litres per minute.

By the end of the chapter you can work out $V'(t)$, find the time at which this rate is
greatest, and say what that time means for the tap. The tank fills fastest at one instant,
and the derivative finds it.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- find a derivative from first principles, using the limit of an average gradient;
- differentiate powers, constants and polynomials, including negative and fractional powers;
- use the product rule, the quotient rule and the chain rule;
- find a second derivative and use it to describe concavity;
- read a derivative as a rate of change, with its units, in a real context;
- find stationary points, classify them, and sketch the curve;
- differentiate exponential and trigonometric functions.

## Where it is used

Every quantity that changes has a rate of change. Speed is the rate of change of distance,
acceleration is the rate of change of speed, and power is the rate of change of energy. A
business studies the rate at which costs rise. A doctor reads the rate at which a fever
falls. Engineers find the maximum of a strength or the minimum of a cost by finding where a
derivative is zero.

## Your turn to connect it

Where would knowing how fast something is changing right now be more useful than knowing
its value?

Think of one quantity that changes. It might be the water level in a bath, the number of
people in a queue, the charge left in a phone, or the temperature of a room. Write down what
the quantity is, and write down one decision that the **rate** would help you make.

You will be able to describe that rate precisely by the end of the chapter.
