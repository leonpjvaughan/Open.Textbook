# 15.1 Axioms and the rules of deduction — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Work out $180 - 65 - 48$.
2. An angle measures $132^\circ$. Is it acute, obtuse or reflex?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $180 - 65 - 48 = 67$. Subtract in order, or add the two smaller numbers first.
2. Obtuse. It is more than $90^\circ$ and less than $180^\circ$, so it is an ordinary
   angle that is wide open. A reflex angle is more than $180^\circ$.

If either answer felt uncertain, look again at angles in Chapter 12 before you carry on.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** State the Two Points Axiom.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

It is about how many lines pass through two given points.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Count the lines: none, exactly one, or many?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

There is exactly one line through any two given points. Two points fix a line, and no
second line passes through both.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Write the converse of "if a shape is a square, then it has four right angles", and
say whether the converse is true.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Swap the "if" part and the "then" part.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The new statement begins "if a shape has four right angles ...".

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Converse: "if a shape has four right angles, then it is a square."

The converse is false. A rectangle has four right angles and is not a square.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** $M$ is the midpoint of $[AB]$, and $|AB| = 26$ cm. Find $|AM|$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The midpoint cuts the segment into two equal parts.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Divide the whole length by $2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$|AM| = \frac{|AB|}{2} = \frac{26}{2} = 13 \text{ cm}$$

Both halves are $13$ cm, and $13 + 13 = 26$, which checks.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** $C$ lies between $A$ and $B$ on the line $AB$. Given $|AC| = 9$ cm and
$|CB| = 15$ cm, find $|AB|$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The two parts add to the whole.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Add $|AC|$ and $|CB|$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$|AB| = |AC| + |CB| = 9 + 15 = 24 \text{ cm}$$

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** $D$ is inside the angle $\angle BAC$. Given $|\angle BAC| = 118^\circ$ and
$|\angle BAD| = 45^\circ$, find $|\angle DAC|$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The two parts add to the whole angle.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Subtract the known part from the whole.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$|\angle DAC| = 118^\circ - 45^\circ = 73^\circ$$

Check: $45 + 73 = 118$, so the two parts add to the whole angle.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A student claims: "Every quadrilateral with perpendicular diagonals is a rhombus."
Explain why the claim is false, and give a counterexample.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You need one shape with perpendicular diagonals that is not a rhombus.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Think of a kite. Its diagonals cross at a right angle.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The statement is a false converse. It is true that a rhombus has perpendicular
diagonals, but the other direction does not hold.

A kite has perpendicular diagonals and is not a rhombus, because a rhombus needs all four
sides equal. The kite has only two pairs of adjacent equal sides.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 12

**7.** In a triangle, two of the angles are $35^\circ$ and $90^\circ$. The third angle is
claimed to be $55^\circ$. Explain how the third angle follows from the two known angles,
and state which rule you used.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The three angles of a triangle add to $180^\circ$. You will prove this in Section 15.4.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Add the two known angles, then subtract from $180^\circ$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$35 + 90 = 125 \qquad 180 - 125 = 55^\circ$$

The third angle is $55^\circ$. The rule is that the three angles of a triangle add to
$180^\circ$.

Notice that this rule is not an axiom. It has to be proved, and the proof comes in
Section 15.4.

</details>
</details>
</details>
