# Chapter 15: Synthetic geometry: foundations

Geometry has a starting point. A few statements we accept without proof, and then
everything else follows by careful reasoning. This chapter is about that beginning: the
statements we accept, the first results we can prove from them, and the way a proof is
written down.

## The chapter problem: a proof you can build on

A glazier cuts triangular panes of glass for a stained-glass window. The first pane has
two angles of $65^\circ$ and $48^\circ$. The glazier must cut the third corner to the
right size, and must be sure the rule holds for every triangle, not only this one.

**First contact — no working yet.** Predict the size of the third angle, and write your
guess down. Then predict whether the three angles still add to the same total when the
triangle is made taller and flatter. Keep both predictions. You will check them in
Section 15.4.

By the end of the chapter you can find the third angle of the pane. You can also prove
that the angles of any triangle add to $180^\circ$, and say exactly which statements the
proof rests on.

**Where it is used:** every geometrical fact in the course, and the reasoning behind it.

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- say what an axiom is, and state the first five;
- use the words theorem, proof, corollary, converse and implies correctly;
- prove that vertically opposite angles are equal, and use angles on a line and at a
  point;
- decide whether two triangles are congruent, and write the congruence statement with
  the vertices in matching order;
- use alternate and corresponding angles at parallel lines;
- prove that the angles of a triangle add to $180^\circ$, and use the exterior angle;
- use the isosceles triangle theorem and its converse, and compare sides with angles;
- recognise perpendicular lines, and use the perpendicular bisector;
- carry out constructions 1 to 6 with a straight edge and compass;
- write a formal proof with a reason for every line.

## Where it is used

Stained glass, carpentry and metalwork, where a shape is cut to a drawing and checked
against a template. Architecture and structural engineering, where a truss is built from
triangles and the angles must close exactly. Surveying and navigation, where a direction
is fixed by measuring angles. Computer graphics and animation, where every shape is a
mesh of triangles. Reasoning about shapes is also the first place a student meets a
mathematical proof, and the same habit of giving a reason is used in every later chapter.

## Your turn to connect it

Which geometrical fact do you rely on most without knowing why it is true?

Choose one. It might be that the angles of a triangle add to $180^\circ$, that a straight
line is $180^\circ$, or that the two base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal.
Write it down, and write one sentence saying where you have used it. By the end of the
chapter you will be able to prove it, or to say exactly which accepted statement it
rests on.
