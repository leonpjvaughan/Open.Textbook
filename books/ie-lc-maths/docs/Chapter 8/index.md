# Chapter 8: Integration

Differentiation turns a quantity into its rate of change. Integration goes the other way.
It turns a rate back into a total, and it measures the area under a graph. Those two ideas
turn out to be the same idea, and that is the heart of this chapter.

The value of a function tells you how much there is. Its integral tells you how much has
built up.

## The chapter problem

**How much water does the tank collect?** Rain runs off a roof into a water tank. The water
enters at a rate

$$r(t) = 12t - 3t^2 \text{ litres per minute}, \qquad 0 \le t \le 4,$$

where $t$ is the number of minutes since the rain began. The tank is empty at $t = 0$.

The rate is not constant. It starts at zero, rises, and then falls, so the water enters
faster at some moments than at others. Three questions follow from the model.

- How much water is in the tank after four minutes?
- When is the water entering fastest, and what is that rate?
- What is the average rate of flow over the four minutes?

By the end of the chapter you can answer all three. The total volume is an integral. The
greatest rate comes from the graph of $r$, which is a curve you already know how to read.
The average rate is an integral divided by the time.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- recognise integration as the reverse of differentiation;
- find an antiderivative of a power, a sum and a constant multiple, and add the constant
  of integration;
- evaluate a definite integral and read it as a signed area;
- state the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and use it on the standard functions;
- find the area between a curve and the $x$-axis, splitting where the curve crosses the
  axis;
- use a substitution to integrate a function of a function;
- find the average value of a function over an interval and read a definite integral as
  accumulated change.

## Where it is used

A rate that changes over time is the normal case, not the exception. Water flowing into a
tank, a car whose speed varies, a power station whose output rises and falls, a population
that grows quickly and then slows — each is a rate. The integral adds the rate up to give
the total, and the total is usually the number that matters.

Area is the same calculation in a different dress. Surveyors find the area of an irregular
field by cutting it into strips. Engineers find the work done by a force from the area
under a force–distance graph. The integral is the exact version of adding up thin strips.

## Your turn to connect it

Think of a quantity that changes at a rate you can picture. It might be the level of water
in a bath, the charge left in a phone, the number of people in a queue, or the amount of
rain falling on a garden.

Write down the rate and the total. Which one do you usually notice, and which one do you
usually want to know? You will be able to move between them by the end of the chapter.
