# Chapter 4: Algebra: expressions and equations

Algebra is arithmetic with a letter standing in for a number you do not know yet.
This chapter builds expressions from patterns, draws them, and then solves the
equations and inequalities that come out of them. Every idea here is used in the
next chapter on complex numbers, and in every later chapter that models a real
situation.

## The chapter problem

**The break-even point.** A small business makes candles and sells them at a
market.

- The equipment and the stall cost **€240**, whatever the number of candles.
- The wax, wick and jar cost **€4** for each candle.
- Each candle sells for **€10**.

The business makes a loss while it has sold too few candles, and a profit once it
has sold enough. The number where the money in equals the money out is the
**break-even point**, and finding it is the job of this chapter.

**First contact — no working yet.** Predict how many candles the business must
sell before it makes any money. Write your estimate down before you read on.

By the end of the chapter you can build the cost and income expressions, solve
for the break-even point exactly, and say what happens on either side of it.
Section 4.8 adds one more realistic detail — a discount on large orders — which
turns the income line into a curve and gives the business a second break-even
point.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- write an expression for the $n$th term of a pattern;
- represent a relationship in a table and on a graph, and read it back;
- find the formula behind a table of values;
- name the gradient and the $y$-intercept of a line and say what they mean;
- evaluate and simplify an expression, including one with brackets;
- solve a linear equation and check the answer;
- solve two simultaneous equations in two unknowns;
- solve a quadratic equation by factorising and by formula;
- solve a linear inequality and show it on a number line;
- solve a quadratic inequality and an inequality with a modulus.

## Where it is used

Pricing and break-even, planning a budget, working out a phone or taxi bill, and
any relationship you can write as a formula. A formula is a rule you can use
again without repeating the thinking, and it lets you answer "what if" questions
by substitution instead of by experiment.
