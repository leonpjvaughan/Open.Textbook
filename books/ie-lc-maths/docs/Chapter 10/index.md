# Chapter 10: Counting and probability

Probability asks how likely something is. To answer that, you first need to know how many
outcomes are possible, and counting is the skill this chapter builds. Once the outcomes are
counted, the chance is a fraction: the number that favour the event, over the number
possible.

## The chapter problem

**The lucky dip.** At the school open day, a stall has a bag of counters. There are
$5$ red counters and $3$ blue counters, so $8$ counters in total. You draw two counters,
one after the other, without looking. The counters are not put back.

The stall awards three outcomes:

- **Small prize** — both counters are red.
- **Star prize** — the two counters have different colours.
- **No prize** — both counters are blue.

Before you count anything, write down your guess. Which do you think is more likely, the
small prize or the star prize? Keep the guess, because you will check it.

By the end of the chapter you can count the possible draws, work out each probability
exactly, and say which prize you are most likely to win. You can also answer the
stallholder's follow-up: if the game is played again and again, how likely is a particular
number of red counters?

## By the end of this chapter you can

- count outcomes with the fundamental principle of counting;
- count arrangements with permutations, and selections with combinations;
- describe a sample space and use set language for events;
- use the axioms of probability and the complement rule;
- find a conditional probability;
- test whether two events are independent;
- use the binomial distribution for a fixed number of repeated trials.

## Where it is used

Every game of chance, every lottery and every raffle is counted before it is priced.
Insurance and weather forecasting use the same rules. In science, counting outcomes
underpins the analysis of experiments, and in computing, counting is how passwords, keys
and error codes are sized. Any time a decision depends on how likely an outcome is, the
mathematics of this chapter is behind it.

## Your turn to connect it

Where have you had to judge a chance, without doing any counting? A weather forecast, a
game at a fair, a team's chance of winning, or a guess on a quiz.

Choose one. Write down what the possible outcomes are, and which one you think is most
likely. You will be able to say something precise about it by the end of the chapter.
