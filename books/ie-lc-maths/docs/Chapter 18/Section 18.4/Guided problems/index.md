# 18.4 Perpendicularity and the angle between vectors — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Work out $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Find the length of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $2 \times 3 + 1 \times 5 = 6 + 5 = 11$.
2. $\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Show that $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and
$\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$ are perpendicular.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out the dot product.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$2 \times 3 + 3 \times (-2)$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$2 \times 3 + 3 \times (-2) = 6 - 6 = 0$$

The dot product is zero, so the vectors are perpendicular.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Find the angle between $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The dot product is $1$, and the lengths are $1$ and $\sqrt{2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\cos\theta = \frac{1}{1 \times \sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$

So $\theta = 45^\circ$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Find the angle between $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and
$\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out the dot product first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$1 \times 0 + 0 \times 1 = 0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The dot product is zero, so $\cos\theta = 0$ and $\theta = 90^\circ$. The two vectors lie
along the axes and meet at a right angle.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Find a vector perpendicular to $\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Swap the components and change the sign of one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Swapping gives $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$, and changing one sign gives
$\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

One perpendicular vector is $\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$. Check:

$$5 \times (-2) + 2 \times 5 = -10 + 10 = 0$$

There are two obvious answers, because changing the other sign also works.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Find $k$ so that $\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ k \end{pmatrix}$ is perpendicular to
$\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Set the dot product equal to zero.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$4 \times 2 + k \times (-3) = 0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$8 - 3k = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad k = \frac{8}{3}$$

Check: $8 - 3 \times \dfrac{8}{3} = 8 - 8 = 0$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** The points are $A(0, 0)$, $B(3, 4)$ and $C(7, 1)$. Show that the angle at $B$ is a
right angle.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Write the two sides that meet at $B$ as vectors, then take their dot product.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\vec{BA} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix}$ and
$\vec{BC} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\vec{BA} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 - 3 \\ 0 - 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix} \qquad \vec{BC} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 - 3 \\ 1 - 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$\vec{BA} \cdot \vec{BC} = (-3) \times 4 + (-4) \times (-3) = -12 + 12 = 0$$

The dot product is zero, so the angle at $B$ is a right angle.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 12

**7.** A triangle has sides $5$ cm, $5$ cm and a right angle between them. Find the angle
opposite the longest side, and say what kind of triangle it is.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The two equal sides meet at $90^\circ$, so the other two angles are equal.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The angles in a triangle add to $180^\circ$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The two equal sides meet at $90^\circ$, and the remaining two angles are equal because the
triangle is isosceles. They add to $180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$, so each is
$45^\circ$. The triangle is right-angled and isosceles. This is the same $45^\circ$ that
appears in the chapter problem.

</details>
</details>
</details>
