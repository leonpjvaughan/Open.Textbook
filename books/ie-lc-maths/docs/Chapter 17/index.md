# Chapter 17: Synthetic geometry: circles

A circle looks simple. It has one centre and one radius, and every point of the circle is
the same distance from the centre. That single fact, used carefully, gives a whole family
of theorems. Chords, angles, tangents and similar triangles all follow from it.

## The chapter problem: the round-tower window

A stonemason is cutting a circular window for a round tower. The opening is a circle of
radius $25$ cm, with centre $O$. The mason sets out the design on a board: first the
full-size circle, then the stone bars and glass panes that fill it.

**First contact — no working yet.** The design has a triangle with one side along a
diameter of the circle. The other two sides are $30$ cm and $40$ cm. Predict the size of
the angle opposite the diameter, and write your guess down.

Then predict which of two straight bars lies closer to the centre: a bar $40$ cm long, or
a bar $48$ cm long. Write that down too. Keep both predictions. You will check them in
Sections 17.1 and 17.2.

By the end of the chapter you can find the centre of a circle from a chord, work out the
angles where panes meet, test whether a bar is a tangent, and set out the drawing with a
straight edge and compass. You can also prove why the angle in a semicircle is a right
angle, and use similar triangles to find a length inside a circle.

**Where it is used:** setting out any round work, and the reasoning behind it.

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- name the parts of a circle, and use the radius, the chord and the tangent;
- use the perpendicular from the centre to a chord, and find the centre from a chord;
- use the angle at the centre and the angle in a semicircle;
- use the angles in the same segment, and the angles in a cyclic quadrilateral;
- use the tangent and the radius at the point of contact, and find a tangent length;
- find the circumcentre, the incentre, the centroid and the orthocentre of a triangle;
- carry out constructions 16 to 22 with a straight edge and compass;
- prove the angle at the centre theorem, and prove a result by contradiction;
- use similar triangles formed by two chords that cross inside a circle.

## Where it is used

Architecture and stonework, where every arch, window and dome is set out from a centre.
Engineering, where a wheel, a gear or a pipe is measured from its centre and its rim.
Surveying, where a curve is checked by offsets taken from a chord. Navigation and
astronomy, where a circle in the sky gives a bearing. Design, where a round table or a
circular logo is laid out from a radius. The theorems also matter wherever a right angle
must be tested without a square, because the angle in a semicircle gives one for free.
