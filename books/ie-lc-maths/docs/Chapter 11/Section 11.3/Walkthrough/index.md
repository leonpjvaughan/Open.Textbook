# 11.3 Representing data: charts and histograms — Learn

**Prerequisites:** reading a table; working out a fraction of $360$.

**Objectives:** choose a suitable display for a data set; read a bar chart, a pie chart,
a stem-and-leaf plot and a histogram; build a frequency table.

By the end of this section you can show a data set so that its pattern is visible, and
you can read a display that someone else has drawn.

## Frequency tables

The first step is usually to count. A **frequency** is the number of times a value
occurs. A **frequency table** lists the values and their frequencies.

| Number of pets | Frequency |
|---|---|
| $0$ | $12$ |
| $1$ | $8$ |
| $2$ | $15$ |
| $3$ | $5$ |

The total frequency is $12 + 8 + 15 + 5 = 40$, so the table describes $40$ homes.

## Charts for categorical data

A **bar chart** shows one bar for each category. The bars are separated by gaps, because
the categories are not numbers and there is no order between them.

A **pie chart** shows each category as a slice of a circle. A full circle is
$360^\circ$, so a category with frequency $f$ out of a total $n$ gets an angle of

$$\frac{f}{n} \times 360^\circ$$

**Example 1.** A survey of $200$ people records favourite sport. $25$ choose hurling.
Find the angle of the hurling slice in a pie chart.

$$\frac{25}{200} \times 360^\circ = 45^\circ$$

## Stem-and-leaf plots

A **stem-and-leaf plot** keeps the data values while showing their shape. The stem is the
leading digit and the leaf is the last digit.

```text
1 | 2 4 5 8
2 | 1 3 7 9
3 | 1 4
```

This plot holds ten values: $12$, $14$, $15$, $18$, $21$, $23$, $27$, $29$, $31$ and
$34$. The smallest is $12$ and the largest is $34$. A stem-and-leaf plot is useful for a
small data set, because no value is lost.

## Histograms

A **histogram** shows numerical data grouped into classes. Each bar covers a class, and
the bars touch, because the classes sit next to each other on a number line.

When the classes all have the same width, the height of a bar is its frequency, and the
area of the bar is proportional to the frequency. When the classes have different widths,
the height is the **frequency density**:

$$\text{frequency density} = \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{class width}}$$

**Example 2.** A histogram has a class of width $5$ with frequency density $6$. How many
values are in the class?

$$\text{frequency} = \text{frequency density} \times \text{class width} = 6 \times 5 = 30$$

> **Watch out.** A histogram and a bar chart look similar, and they are not the same. A
> bar chart has gaps between the bars and shows categories. A histogram has no gaps
> between the bars and shows numerical classes. A histogram of ages with bars $0$-$9$,
> $10$-$19$ and $20$-$29$ has no room for a gap: the numbers run on without a break.

## Choosing a display

- **Categorical data** — a bar chart or a pie chart.
- **A small numerical data set** — a stem-and-leaf plot, which keeps every value.
- **A large numerical data set** — a histogram, which groups the values into classes.
- **Change over time** — a line plot, with the time on the horizontal axis.

**Example 3.** A teacher records the times of $200$ runners in a race. Which display
suits the data, and why?

The times are numerical and there are many of them, so a histogram suits the data. It
groups the times into classes such as $20$-$29$ minutes and $30$-$39$ minutes, and the
shape of the group is visible.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The poll reported $520$ Yes voters and $480$ No voters out
of $1000$.

Work out the angle of the Yes slice in a pie chart.

$$\frac{520}{1000} \times 360^\circ = 187.2^\circ$$

The Yes slice is slightly more than half the circle, because $520$ is slightly more than
half of $1000$. The pie chart shows a small lead, and it does not show whether that lead
is reliable. Section 11.7 supplies the number that does.

## You should now be able to

- build a frequency table from raw data;
- choose a bar chart, a pie chart, a stem-and-leaf plot or a histogram;
- work out the angle of a pie-chart slice;
- read a histogram with equal or unequal class widths;
- explain the difference between a bar chart and a histogram.
