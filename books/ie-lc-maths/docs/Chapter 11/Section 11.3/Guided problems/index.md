# 11.3 Representing data: charts and histograms — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write $\dfrac{25}{200}$ in its simplest form.
2. Find $40\%$ of $250$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. Divide top and bottom by $25$: $\dfrac{25}{200} = \dfrac{1}{8}$.
2. $40\%$ of $250 = 0.4 \times 250 = 100$.

The first answer is the fraction of the total in one category, which is exactly what a
pie chart turns into an angle.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A survey of $200$ people records favourite sport. $25$ choose hurling. Find the
angle of the hurling slice in a pie chart.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A full circle is $360^\circ$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$\frac{25}{200} \times 360^\circ$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{25}{200} \times 360^\circ = \frac{1}{8} \times 360^\circ = 45^\circ$$

The hurling slice is $45^\circ$. All the slices must add to $360^\circ$, so checking that
total is a quick way to catch a mistake.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A frequency table has frequencies $12$, $8$, $15$ and $5$. Find the total
frequency.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the frequencies.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$12 + 8 + 15 + 5$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$12 + 8 + 15 + 5 = 40$$

The table describes $40$ items in total. Every pie-chart angle is a fraction of this
$40$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A histogram has a class of width $5$ with frequency density $6$. How many values
are in the class?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Frequency density is frequency divided by class width.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Multiply the density by the width.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{frequency} = 6 \times 5 = 30$$

There are $30$ values in the class. The area of the bar is proportional to the frequency,
which is why the height alone is not the frequency when the widths differ.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A stem-and-leaf plot holds the values $12$, $14$, $15$, $18$, $21$, $23$, $27$,
$29$, $31$ and $34$. Find the median.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

There are $10$ values, so the median lies between the $5$th and the $6$th.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The $5$th value is $21$ and the $6$th value is $23$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{21 + 23}{2} = 22$$

The median is $22$. A stem-and-leaf plot keeps every value, so the median can be read
straight from the plot.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A teacher records the times of $200$ runners. Explain why a histogram suits the
data better than a bar chart.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Are the times categories or numbers?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Numerical data grouped into classes are shown by touching bars.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The times are numerical and there are many of them. A histogram groups them into classes
such as $20$-$29$ minutes, and the bars touch because the classes run on without a break.
A bar chart has gaps and is used for categories, such as the runners' clubs.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Back to the chapter problem. The poll reported $520$ Yes voters out of $1000$.
Work out the angle of the Yes slice in a pie chart.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the fraction of the total that voted Yes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$\frac{520}{1000} \times 360^\circ$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{520}{1000} \times 360^\circ = 0.52 \times 360^\circ = 187.2^\circ$$

The Yes slice is a little more than half the circle. The display shows the size of the
lead, and it says nothing about whether the lead is reliable.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 9

**7.** An arithmetic sequence begins $4, 7, 10, \dots$ Find the $15$th term.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The common difference is $7 - 4 = 3$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$T_n = a + (n - 1)d$$

Here $T_n$ is the $n$th term, $a$ is the first term and $d$ is the common difference.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$T_{15} = 4 + 14 \times 3 = 4 + 42 = 46$$

The number of steps is $14$, not $15$. A line plot of a sequence has the same even spacing
that the common difference describes.

</details>
</details>
</details>
