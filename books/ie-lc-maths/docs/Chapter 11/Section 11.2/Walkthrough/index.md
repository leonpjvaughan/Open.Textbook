# 11.2 Sampling methods and bias — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 11.1 — population, sample and a simple random sample.

**Objectives:** describe a systematic sample and a convenience sample; name the main
sources of bias; tell a sample survey, an observational study and a designed experiment
apart.

By the end of this section you can choose a sample method, and you can say what would
make the result wrong even when the arithmetic is right.

## Choosing the sample

A **simple random sample** gives every member of the population the same chance of being
chosen. It is the fairest method, and it needs a numbered list of the whole population.

A **systematic sample** chooses every $k$th member from a list, after a random start. It
is quick when the list already exists.

**Example 1.** A list holds $400$ names. Describe a systematic sample of $50$ names.

Divide $400$ by $50$ to get $8$. Choose a random starting point from $1$ to $8$, then
take every $8$th name from there. This gives $50$ names. The random start matters: taking
the first $50$ names on the list would pick whatever the list happens to be ordered by.

A **convenience sample** uses whoever is easiest to reach, such as the people in one
classroom. It is quick, and it is usually biased, because the convenient people are not a
random part of the population.

## What makes a sample biased

A sample is **biased** when it does not represent the population. The arithmetic can
still be correct, and the conclusion will still be wrong. Four causes appear again and
again.

- **Sampling bias.** The method favours part of the population. Asking only people in a
  gym about exercise is sampling bias.
- **Non-response.** Some people chosen for the sample do not answer. If the people who
  answer differ from those who do not, the sample is biased.
- **Self-selection.** People choose themselves, as in a phone-in vote. The people who
  feel strongly are over-represented.
- **Question wording.** A question that suggests an answer is called a **leading
  question**. "Do you agree that the council wastes money?" pushes the reader towards
  Yes.

> **Bias.** A sample is biased when the method of choosing or questioning makes some
> parts of the population more likely to appear than others. Bias is a fault in the
> method, and a larger sample does not remove it.

## Types of study

Once the sample is chosen, the study takes one of three forms.

- A **sample survey** asks the sample questions, such as a poll or a questionnaire.
- An **observational study** observes the sample without changing anything, such as
  recording the heights of a group of students.
- A **designed experiment** deliberately applies a treatment and compares the result
  with a control. A medical trial of a new drug is a designed experiment.

Only a designed experiment can show that a treatment causes a change. A survey or an
observational study can show a relationship, and it cannot show which way the cause
runs. Section 11.10 returns to that point.

## Stratified, cluster and quota samples

Three more methods are named in the course, and each suits a different situation.

- A **stratified sample** splits the population into groups that share a characteristic,
  then takes a simple random sample from each group. If a school has $360$ girls and
  $240$ boys, a stratified sample of $60$ takes $\dfrac{60 \times 360}{600} = 36$ girls
  and $24$ boys, so the sample has the same mix as the school.
- A **cluster sample** splits the population into groups, chooses whole groups at
  random, and measures everyone in the chosen groups. Choosing four streets at random
  and visiting every house in them is a cluster sample.
- A **quota sample** fills fixed numbers from named groups, with the interviewer
  choosing who to ask. It is quick, and it is not random within each group, so it can be
  biased.

A **designed experiment** uses **randomisation** to decide who receives the treatment.
Random allocation means the two groups differ only by chance, so a difference in the
outcome can be credited to the treatment. The group that does not receive the treatment
is the **control group**, and it shows what would have happened without it.

Studies also raise **ethical issues**. A trial must not withhold a treatment known to
work, and it needs the informed consent of each participant. An observational study must
protect privacy, especially with medical data.

<!-- /tier:h -->

> **Watch out.** A bigger sample is not automatically a better one. A voluntary
> phone-in with $100000$ responses is more biased than a random sample of $1000$, because
> the people who chose to respond are not the population. Bias is fixed by the method,
> not by the size.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The polling company telephoned $1000$ voters and reported
$52\%$ Yes.

Suppose the company had used a convenience sample instead, asking the first $1000$ people
in a city centre on a Saturday. Name one group that would be under-represented, and say
which cause of bias that is.

People who work on Saturdays, people who live outside the city centre, and people who
cannot travel far would all be under-represented. That is sampling bias, because the
method favours the people who happen to be in the centre.

Keep the two proportions, $0.52$ Yes and $0.48$ No. Section 11.3 shows how to display
them.

- describe a simple random sample and a systematic sample;
- explain why a convenience sample is usually biased;
- name the four common causes of bias;
- tell a sample survey, an observational study and a designed experiment apart;
- explain how randomisation and a control group support a causal claim.
