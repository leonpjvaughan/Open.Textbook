# 11.2 Sampling methods and bias — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write $\dfrac{360}{600}$ in its simplest form.
2. Find $25\%$ of $800$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. Divide top and bottom by $120$: $\dfrac{360}{600} = \dfrac{3}{5}$.
2. $25\%$ of $800 = 0.25 \times 800 = 200$.

The first answer is the fraction of a population in one group. A stratified sample keeps
that fraction in the sample, which is the idea in the Higher part of this section.

</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** A list holds $400$ names. A researcher takes every $8$th name. How many names are
in the sample?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the size of the list by the step.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$400 \div 8$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$400 \div 8 = 50 \text{ names}$$

The sample has $50$ names. A systematic sample also needs a random start, so that the
first name is not always the same one.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A researcher asks the first $30$ people who leave a shopping centre on a Tuesday
morning. Explain why this sample may be biased.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Who is in a shopping centre on a Tuesday morning?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

People who work full time are not there.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

People who work full time, people who are at school and people who live far away are
unlikely to be in the centre on a Tuesday morning. The method favours whoever is convenient to
reach, so it is a convenience sample and it is biased.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A charity sends out $800$ questionnaires and $320$ are returned. Work out the
response rate as a percentage.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the number returned by the number sent.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$$\frac{320}{800} \times 100$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{320}{800} \times 100 = 40\%$$

The response rate is $40\%$. The $480$ people who did not reply may differ from the
$320$ who did, so non-response can bias the result.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** A radio station invites listeners to phone in and vote on a new bus route. Explain
why the result may not represent the town.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Who decides to phone in?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

People with a strong opinion are more likely to call.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The sample is self-selected. Listeners who feel strongly, or who have the time to call,
are over-represented, and listeners who do not hear the programme are missing. A large
number of calls does not repair the bias.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A survey asks: "Do you agree that the council wastes money on unnecessary
projects?" Explain why this question may bias the answers.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Does the question suggest an answer?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The words "wastes" and "unnecessary" already judge the spending.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The question is a leading question. It uses the loaded words "wastes" and
"unnecessary", so it invites the reader to agree. A fairer question would ask whether
the reader approves of the council's spending, without judging it in the wording.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** Back to the chapter problem. The polling company chose its $1000$ voters by giving
every voter in the country the same chance of being selected. Name the sampling method,
and say one thing that could still bias the result.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The method is the one where every member has the same chance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The bias would come after the sample is chosen.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The method is a simple random sample. Even so, voters who are never at home, or who
refuse to answer, are missing. If those voters differ from the ones who answer, the
result is biased by non-response.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## Higher-level practice

**7.** A school has $360$ girls and $240$ boys, so $600$ students. A stratified sample
of $60$ students is taken. How many girls and how many boys are in the sample?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Keep the same fraction in the sample as in the school.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Girls: $\dfrac{60 \times 360}{600}$. Boys: $\dfrac{60 \times 240}{600}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{60 \times 360}{600} = 36 \text{ girls}, \qquad
\frac{60 \times 240}{600} = 24 \text{ boys}$$

The sample has the same mix as the school, because the fraction of girls in the sample
is $\dfrac{36}{60} = \dfrac{3}{5}$, the same as $\dfrac{360}{600}$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** A researcher chooses four streets at random from a town, then visits every house
in those four streets. Name the sampling method, and say one advantage of it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Whole groups are chosen, not individual people.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The advantage is about travel and cost.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

This is a cluster sample: whole groups, the streets, are chosen at random and everyone
in them is measured. It is cheaper and quicker than a simple random sample spread across
the whole town, because the researcher visits only four streets.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 10

**9.** A fair coin is tossed $3$ times. Find the probability of exactly $2$ heads.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

There are $\dbinom{3}{2}$ ways to choose which two tosses are heads.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Let $X$ be the number of heads, so $P(X = 2)$ means the probability of exactly $2$ heads.

$$P(X = 2) = \binom{3}{2}\left(\frac12\right)^2\left(\frac12\right)$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$P(X = 2) = \binom{3}{2}\left(\frac12\right)^3 = \frac{3}{8}$$

The $3$ counts the arrangements HHT, HTH and THH. Sampling in this section is also a
question of counting and chance, which is why the two chapters sit together.

</details>
</details>
</details>
