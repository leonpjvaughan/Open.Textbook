# Chapter 11: Statistics

Statistics is how we learn about a whole group of people or things without measuring
every one of them. We take a **sample**, work out numbers from it, and use those numbers
to say something about the **population**. That step from a part to a whole is powerful,
and it is also where statistics goes wrong.

## The chapter problem

**The poll that got it wrong.** In the days before a referendum, a polling company
telephoned a random sample of $1000$ voters. The company reported that $52\%$ would vote
Yes and $48\%$ would vote No. A newspaper printed the headline "Yes on course for
victory".

On polling day, $48\%$ voted Yes and $52\%$ voted No.

The poll was not a lie, and the voters did not change their minds in a way that explains
the gap. A sample of $1000$ people gave an answer that was about four points away from
the truth. Before you read on, write down your guess: how far out can a sample of $1000$
reasonably be?

By the end of the chapter you can work out how far out that sample could be, put a
number on the uncertainty, and say exactly what a margin of error does and does not
promise. You will also be able to test whether the poll's result is even different from a
$50$-$50$ split.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- tell a population from a sample, and a parameter from a statistic;
- choose a sample without bias, and recognise the ways bias creeps in;
- represent data with a bar chart, a pie chart, a stem-and-leaf plot or a histogram;
- find the mean, median, mode and range of a data set, and the mean from a frequency
  table;
- find the standard deviation and the interquartile range, and choose which measure of
  spread to report;
- use the normal distribution and the empirical rule;
- work out a margin of error for a proportion, and read a confidence interval;
- describe the sampling distribution of the mean;
- carry out a large-sample test of a population mean and interpret a $p$-value;
- measure correlation and draw a line of best fit;
- spot a statistic that is used to mislead.

## Where it is used

Opinion polls, market research and quality control all rest on a sample. So do medical
trials, weather records and the league tables on a school website. When a company tests
one hundred light bulbs instead of ten thousand, or a hospital reports a waiting time
from a sample of patients, the mathematics of this chapter decides how much the answer
can be trusted. Every claim of the form "the survey says" is a claim about a sample.

## Your turn to connect it

Where have you seen a percentage from a survey used to argue for something? A poll
before an election, a review score for a film, a claim in an advertisement, or a
headline about what "most people" think.

Choose one. Write down who was asked, how many were asked, and what the percentage was.
Keep it. By the end of the chapter you will be able to say how much of that claim is
supported, and how much is the sample talking.
