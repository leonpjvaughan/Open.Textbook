# 11.11 Statistical reasoning and misused statistics — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. A study finds that two variables move together. What does that show, and what does it
   not show?
2. A sample is chosen by asking the first 50 people who walk into a shop. Name one reason
   the sample may not represent the population.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. It shows a **correlation** — the variables move together. It does not show that one
   causes the other. A third variable may drive both, which Section 11.10 showed.
2. The people in one shop at one time of day are not a random sample of the population.
   Whole groups may be missing: people who work, people who cannot reach that shop, or
   people who shop elsewhere. That is **bias**, from Section 11.2.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A bar chart shows sales of €96m, €98m and €100m, and its vertical axis starts at
€95m. Work out the true percentage rise from €96m to €100m, and say how the chart makes it
look.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Percentage change is the change divided by the original, times 100.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{100 - 96}{96} \times 100$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\frac{100 - 96}{96} \times 100 = 4.17\%$$

The rise is about $4\%$, which is small. On the chart the bars stand $1$, $3$ and $5$ units
above the axis, so the third looks **five times** as tall as the first. An axis starting at
$0$ would show three bars of almost equal height.

The numbers on the bars are correct. The axis is what misleads, and that is the point: a
misleading graph does not have to contain a false number.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** A report says "1 000 people in a million are affected". Express this as a rate per
100 000 people, and say which way of writing it is clearer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide by the population, then multiply by 100 000.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{1000}{1\,000\,000} \times 100\,000$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$100 \text{ people per } 100\,000$$

That is the same as one person in a thousand. The two statements are identical, and the
second is far less alarming — which is why the first was chosen. When a statistic is
written as a large count without its base, find the base before you react to the size.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** A treatment "doubles the risk" of a side effect. The risk rises from one person in a
million to two in a million. Work out the increase in cases per million, and say why the
two statements sound so different.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Doubling is about the **ratio**. The increase is about the **difference**.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$2 - 1$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The increase is **one extra case per million people**.

Both statements are true. The risk has doubled, which is a statement about the ratio. The
absolute increase is one person in a million, which is a statement about the difference.
"Doubles the risk" sounds alarming and "one extra case in a million" does not, and a
careful reader wants both, because the decision depends on the difference.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** An advertisement says "two out of three dentists recommend this brush". Express it as
a percentage and state the sample size.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Two out of three, and then count the dentists.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{2}{3} \times 100$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Two out of three is $66.7\%$, and the sample was **three** dentists.

The percentage is accurate and the claim is worthless. Three dentists chosen from one
clinic say nothing about dentists in general — who was asked, and how they were chosen, is
what decides whether the number can be generalised. A percentage from a tiny or biased
sample is not repaired by being written precisely.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** A poll of 1 000 people reports $52\%$ Yes with a margin of error of $3.2$ percentage
points. Write down the confidence interval and say whether the poll can call the result.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The interval runs from the result minus the margin to the result plus the margin.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$52 - 3.2$ and $52 + 3.2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The interval is from $48.8\%$ to $55.2\%$.

The interval includes values below $50\%$, so a No result is inside it. The poll cannot
separate the two sides — and it cannot predict the outcome, because a poll measures opinion
now with an uncertainty attached. The poll itself is honest; the misuse would be in a
headline that turns it into a forecast.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A study finds that people who eat more breakfast cereal weigh less. In the study,
the people who ate more cereal also exercised more. Explain why the study does not show
that cereal causes weight loss.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What else differs between the two groups?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Exercise is a third variable that could affect weight on its own.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The study reports a correlation between cereal and weight. But the two groups also differ
in how much they exercise, and exercise affects weight directly.

So the pattern could be explained by exercise alone, with the cereal riding along. A third
variable that drives both is called a **confounding variable**, and until it is controlled
for — by comparing people who exercise the same amount — the cereal has no case to answer.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Section 11.5

**7.** A class of 5 students has heights $150$, $152$, $155$, $158$, $160$ cm. Work out the
mean, and say whether a mean of 5 people tells you much about the class.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add, then divide by 5.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\dfrac{150 + 152 + 155 + 158 + 160}{5}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\text{mean} = \frac{775}{5} = 155\ \text{cm}$$

The mean describes these 5 students exactly and says almost nothing about the class they
came from. Five students is a very small sample, so the mean could move by several
centimetres with a different five. The number is correct; how far it can be generalised is
a separate question, and this section is about asking it.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**8.** Write down the first three questions you would ask about any statistical claim, and
say why each one matters.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start with who was measured, then how they were chosen.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The questions in the Learn page are in order for a reason: each one can end the enquiry.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

1. **Who or what is the population, and who was actually measured?** If the sample is not
   from the population the claim is about, nothing later can repair it.
2. **How was the sample chosen, and who was left out?** A random sample supports a
   generalisation; a convenience sample or self-selected sample does not.
3. **How large was the sample, and what is the margin of error?** A small sample is noisy,
   and the margin tells you how much of the result is uncertainty.

Ask them in that order, because a failure at step 1 makes steps 2 and 3 irrelevant. A large
number of responses from a biased sample is still biased — size does not fix method.

</details>
</details>
</details>
