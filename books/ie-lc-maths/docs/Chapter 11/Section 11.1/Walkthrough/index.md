# 11.1 Populations and samples — Learn

**Prerequisites:** reading a table; working with percentages.

**Objectives:** name the population and the sample in a statistical study; explain why a
sample is often used instead of a census; describe a simple random sample; classify a
variable as categorical or numerical.

By the end of this section you can say who a study is about, who was actually measured,
and whether the numbers describe the whole group or only a part of it.

## The population and the sample

Every statistical study starts with a question about a group. The whole group is the
**population**. The part we actually measure is the **sample**.

> **Population and sample.** The **population** is the whole group the question is
> about. The **sample** is the part of the population we measure or ask.

A study that measures every member of the population takes a **census**. A study that
measures a part takes a **sample survey**.

**Example 1.** A school wants to know how many hours its $800$ students study each week.
It asks $50$ students. Name the population and the sample.

The population is the $800$ students in the school. The sample is the $50$ students who
were asked.

## Why we take a sample

A census sounds better, because it measures everyone. It is often impossible, too slow
or too expensive.

- **Cost and time.** Asking $800$ students takes far longer than asking $50$.
- **Destructive testing.** A factory cannot test the life of every bulb by leaving it on
  until it fails, because the test destroys the bulb. It tests a sample.
- **Access.** A health survey cannot reach every adult in the country.

A sample is only useful if it tells us about the population. That happens when the
sample is **representative**: the sample has roughly the same mix of people or items as
the population. A sample that is not representative is **biased**.

> **Representative sample.** A sample is representative when it reflects the population
> it came from. A biased sample does not, so conclusions drawn from it are not reliable.

## Simple random samples

The fairest way to choose a sample is a **simple random sample**. Every member of the
population has the same chance of being chosen, and each choice is independent of the
others.

**Example 2.** Describe how to choose a simple random sample of $40$ students from a
numbered list of $800$.

Number the students from $1$ to $800$. Use a random number generator to produce $40$
different numbers between $1$ and $800$. The students with those numbers form the sample.
Every student has the same chance of being picked, so the sample is not biased by who is
near the door or who volunteers.

## Parameters and statistics

The numbers we care about have different names, depending on whether they describe the
population or the sample.

> **Parameter and statistic.** A **parameter** is a number that describes the population.
> A **statistic** is a number worked out from a sample. We use a statistic to estimate a
> parameter.

The population mean is written $\mu$, and the sample mean is written $\bar{x}$. A poll
reports a **sample proportion**, such as $0.52$, and uses it to estimate the population
proportion $p$. The true $p$ is what the poll is trying to find, and it is never known
exactly.

## Types of data

We also classify each variable, because the type decides which chart and which average
make sense.

- **Categorical** data put each item in a group, such as eye colour or county.
- **Numerical** data are numbers. **Discrete** numerical data can be counted, such as the
  number of siblings. **Continuous** numerical data can be measured, such as height or
  time, and any value in a range is possible.

**Example 3.** Classify each variable: the number of pets in a home, the height of a
student, and a student's favourite subject.

The number of pets is discrete numerical, because it is a count. The height is continuous
numerical, because it is a measurement. The favourite subject is categorical, because it
names a group.

> **Watch out.** A number is not always numerical data. A jersey number or a phone number
> is a label written with digits, so it is categorical. You cannot find the mean of a set
> of jersey numbers in any useful sense.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The polling company telephoned a random sample of $1000$
voters before the referendum.

Write down the population and the sample. Then say whether the poll took a census, and
what number the company was trying to estimate.

The population is all the voters in the referendum, and the sample is the $1000$ voters
who were asked. The poll was a sample survey, not a census. The company was trying to
estimate the population proportion $p$ of Yes voters, using the sample proportion
$0.52$.

Keep the number $1000$. Section 11.7 turns it into a margin of error.

## You should now be able to

- name the population and the sample in a study;
- explain why a sample is used instead of a census;
- describe how to choose a simple random sample;
- tell a parameter from a statistic;
- classify a variable as categorical or numerical.
