# Chapter 16: Synthetic geometry: quadrilaterals, similarity and area

Chapter 15 built the foundations. It gave us the axioms, the first theorems, and the
angle sum of a triangle. This chapter builds on that base. We prove the theorems of the
parallelogram, meet similar triangles, prove Pythagoras' theorem, and finish with area.

Every result in this chapter is proved from the results of Chapter 15. Nothing here asks
you to take a new statement on trust.

## The chapter problem: the new field

A farmer has bought a new field. On the plan the field is a quadrilateral $ABCD$. The
side $AB$ is $60$ m, the side $BC$ is $25$ m, and the corner at $B$ is a right angle.

The farmer has three jobs. A fence must run along the diagonal from $A$ to $C$. Grass
seed must be ordered for the whole field, so the area is needed. A gate must be placed
$24$ m along the side $AB$, with a dividing fence from the gate to the opposite side.

**First contact — no working yet.** Predict the length of the diagonal $AC$, and predict
the area of the field. Write both numbers down. Then predict whether a parallelogram with
one right angle must be a rectangle. Keep all three predictions. You will check them as
the chapter goes on.

By the end of the chapter you can find the diagonal, find the area, and place the gate.
You can also prove that the opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal, prove that the
diagonal of the field has the length you predicted, and say exactly which results each
proof rests on.

**Where it is used:** land measurement and surveying, carpentry and roofing, engineering
drawings, and the design of any structure built from straight edges.

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- name the quadrilaterals, and say what makes a parallelogram, a rectangle, a rhombus
  and a square;
- prove that the opposite sides and opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal;
- use the converses, and decide from the sides or the diagonals whether a quadrilateral
  is a parallelogram;
- prove that the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect one another;
- recognise similar triangles, and find a missing side from the ratio of the sides;
- divide a segment in a given ratio, and use a line parallel to one side of a triangle;
- prove Pythagoras' theorem, and use its converse;
- find the area of a rectangle, a parallelogram, a triangle and a trapezium;
- carry out constructions 7 to 15 with a straight edge and compass.

## Where it is used

Land is bought, sold and divided by its shape and its area. A surveyor proves that a
plot is the shape the deed says it is, then measures its area for the price. A carpenter
checks that a door frame is a rectangle by comparing its diagonals, because equal
diagonals are the quickest test on site. A roofer finds the length of a rafter with
Pythagoras' theorem, and a mapmaker uses similar triangles to turn a small drawing into a
distance on the ground. The same reasoning appears in computer graphics, where every
surface is built from triangles and quadrilaterals.

## Your turn to connect it

Where have you seen a shape checked rather than measured?

Think of a builder, a tiler, a gardener or a machinist. Each one needs a shape to be
right, and each one checks it with a short rule instead of a long measurement. A tiler
checks a right angle with the $3$, $4$, $5$ triangle. A carpenter checks a rectangle by
comparing its diagonals. A gardener checks that a bed is a parallelogram by looking at
opposite sides.

Choose one check you have seen or used. Write it down, and write one sentence saying what
makes the check work. By the end of the chapter you will be able to prove that your check
is reliable.
