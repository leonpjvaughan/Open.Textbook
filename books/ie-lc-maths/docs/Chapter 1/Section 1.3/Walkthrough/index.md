# 1.3 Geometric construction of √2 and √3 — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 1.2 — surds; Pythagoras' theorem; compass and
straightedge constructions from the geometry course.

**Objectives:** construct $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{3}$ on the number line; explain
why each construction marks an exact point.

By the end of this section you can mark an irrational length on a number line
using only a compass and a straight edge.

## A length you can draw but not write

Section 1.2 showed that $\sqrt{2}$ is not a fraction. That is a statement about
arithmetic. It does not mean the length is out of reach. A square of side $1$ has
a diagonal of length $\sqrt{2}$, and you can draw that square.

Pythagoras' theorem gives the length $d$:

$$d = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$$

So $\sqrt{2}$ is a real distance. The number line has a point for it, and the
construction finds that point exactly.

## Constructing √2

1. Draw a number line and mark $0$ and $1$.
2. Erect a square of side $1$ with its left side standing on the number line at
   $0$.
3. Draw the diagonal of the square. Its length is $\sqrt{2}$.
4. Open the compass to that diagonal.
5. Put the compass point at $0$ and draw an arc that crosses the number line.
   The crossing point is at $\sqrt{2}$.

The arc carries the length from the square to the number line. Nothing is
measured with a ruler, so nothing is rounded.

## Constructing √3

Once $\sqrt{2}$ is on the line, use it as a leg of a new right triangle.

1. Draw a right triangle with one leg of length $1$ and the other of length
   $\sqrt{2}$.
2. Its hypotenuse has length

$$\sqrt{1^2 + (\sqrt{2})^2} = \sqrt{1 + 2} = \sqrt{3}$$

3. Open the compass to that hypotenuse, put the point at $0$, and mark the arc on
   the number line.

The crossing point is at $\sqrt{3}$.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Find the diagonal of a square of side $3$.

$$d = \sqrt{3^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}$$

The diagonal of a square of side $s$ is always $s\sqrt{2}$, so scaling the unit
square scales its diagonal by the same factor.

**Example 2.** Construct $\sqrt{5}$ on the number line.

Use a right triangle with legs $1$ and $2$, because

$$\sqrt{1^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{5}$$

Draw the triangle, open the compass to its hypotenuse, and mark the distance from
$0$. Any whole number can be written as a sum of two squares, and then drawn the
same way.

**Example 3.** Show that $\sqrt{2}$ lies between $1.4$ and $1.5$.

Square all three numbers:

$$1.4^2 = 1.96 \qquad (\sqrt{2})^2 = 2 \qquad 1.5^2 = 2.25$$

Since $1.96 < 2 < 2.25$, the square roots are in the same order, so
$1.4 < \sqrt{2} < 1.5$.

> **Watch out.** A construction is exact, but a measurement is not. If you measure
> the diagonal of the unit square with a ruler, you get a value near $1.41$ cm,
> and the last figure depends on how carefully you look. The compass arc does not
> depend on a ruler at all. Say that the construction marks the exact point
> $\sqrt{2}$, and that $1.414$ is only a decimal approximation.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The estimator rounded the measurements before
calculating, and that rounding is what lost the contract.

Construct $\sqrt{2}$ on the number line from a unit square, then construct
$\sqrt{3}$ from the triangle with legs $1$ and $\sqrt{2}$. Explain why each point
is exact.

Now compare the two kinds of number. The construction marks the exact point
$\sqrt{2}$, while the decimal $1.414$ is rounded. The estimator used a rounded
number in place of an exact one, and Section 1.7 measures the cost.

## You should now be able to

- explain why the diagonal of a unit square has length $\sqrt{2}$;
- construct $\sqrt{2}$ on the number line with a compass and a straight edge;
- construct $\sqrt{3}$ using the triangle with legs $1$ and $\sqrt{2}$;
- construct the square root of any whole number that is a sum of two squares;
- explain why a construction is exact while a measurement is approximate.
