# Chapter 1: Number systems and arithmetic

Numbers come in families. You have counted with whole numbers, owed money with
negative numbers, and shared a pizza with fractions. This chapter puts those
families in order, adds the numbers that are not fractions at all, and then turns
to powers and to the accuracy of an answer.

## The chapter problem: The error that cost a contract

A flooring company quotes to carpet a room measuring $4.35$ m by $3.28$ m.

- Carpet costs €32.40 per square metre.
- The quote adds $5\%$ for waste and offcuts.
- It adds a $10\%$ profit margin.
- The estimator rounded the two measurements to $4$ m and $3$ m before starting.

The quote came to €449.06, and the job lost money.

**First contact — no working yet.** Estimate the area of the room. Does rounding
the lengths to $4$ m and $3$ m make the quote too high or too low? Write down your
prediction before you read on.

By the end of the chapter you can work out the quote the company should have
sent, find where the rounding should have happened, and say how much precision a
chain of calculations actually needs.

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- place a number in the systems $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$ and
  $\mathbb{R}$;
- simplify a surd and rationalise a denominator;
- construct $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{3}$ on the number line;
- work out an absolute value and solve a simple modulus equation;
- use the laws of indices with whole-number, negative and fractional indices;
- solve an equation where the unknown is an index;
- write a number in scientific notation and round it to a required accuracy.

## Where it is used

Quotations, measurements, financial calculations, engineering and science all
rest on this arithmetic. A builder estimates materials, a laboratory reports a
result to the right number of significant figures, and a bank compounds interest
with a power. The same skills decide whether a figure is trustworthy.
