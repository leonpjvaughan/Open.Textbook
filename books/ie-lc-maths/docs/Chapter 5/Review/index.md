# Chapter 5: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The cubic with an impossible root.** Solve $x^3 - 5x^2 + 11x - 15 = 0$.

**One real root.** Try small whole numbers. $x = 3$ gives $27 - 45 + 33 - 15 = 0$, so
$x = 3$ is a root. Dividing by $(x - 3)$ leaves the quadratic factor $x^2 - 2x + 5$.

**The quadratic.** Its discriminant is $4 - 20 = -16$, which is negative — so the two
remaining roots are not real. Solving by formula:

$$x = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2} = \frac{2 \pm 4i}{2} = 1 \pm 2i$$

**All three roots.** $x = 3$, $x = 1 + 2i$ and $x = 1 - 2i$.

**Why the pair.** The coefficients of the cubic are real, so the conjugate root theorem
applies: a complex root forces its conjugate to be a root too. A cubic has exactly three
roots, so it has either three real roots or one real root and a conjugate pair. Here it is
the second case.

**The check.** For $x^3 - 5x^2 + 11x - 15$: the sum of the roots should be $-b/a = 5$, and
$3 + (1 + 2i) + (1 - 2i) = 5$. The product should be $-d/a = 15$, and
$3(1 + 2i)(1 - 2i) = 3 \times 5 = 15$. Both agree, and the imaginary parts cancelled in
both — which is what a real sum and a real product require.

**The diagram.** The three roots plotted on an Argand diagram are $(3, 0)$, $(1, 2)$ and
$(1, -2)$. The two complex roots are reflections of each other in the real axis, and both
are $\sqrt{5}$ from the origin.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, decide **what is being asked** and say
why, before you calculate. Two of them are deliberately questions where the obvious first
move is wrong.

1. Simplify $i^{43}$, $i^{100}$ and $i^{4k+2}$ for a whole number $k$.
2. Work out $(4 - 3i)(2 + 5i)$ and $(4 - 3i)^2$.
3. Write $\dfrac{2 + i}{3 - 2i}$ in the form $a + bi$.
4. Find the modulus of $-8 + 6i$ and the distance between $-8 + 6i$ and $3 + 6i$.
5. Find $x$ and $y$: $(3x + 1) + 7i = 10 + (2y - 1)i$.
6. Solve $x^2 + 6x + 25 = 0$ and check one root by substitution.
7. Show that $x = 2$ is a root of $x^3 - 3x^2 + 4x - 12 = 0$ and find all three roots.
8. A real cubic has $1 + i$ as a root and $x = 2$ as another. Write down the third root and
   the cubic itself.
9. Explain why $|z|^2 = z\bar z$, using $z = a + bi$.
10. A student says "$i = \sqrt{-1}$, so $i^2 = \sqrt{-1} \times \sqrt{-1} = \sqrt{1} = 1$".
    Explain the error and state the correct value.

## What you can now do

- write a complex number in the form $a + bi$ and name its real and imaginary parts;
- simplify any power of $i$ by dividing the index by 4;
- add, subtract and multiply complex numbers, replacing $i^2$ with $-1$;
- find the conjugate and divide by multiplying top and bottom by it;
- plot a complex number on the Argand diagram, and find its modulus and the distance
  between two points;
- solve a real cubic by finding one root and dividing, and say why the complex roots come
  in a pair;
- build the quadratic with a given conjugate pair of roots.

## Thinking back

Look at the quantity you chose at the start of the chapter — a position on a map, a force,
a colour, or something else with two parts.

You can now describe it the way complex numbers describe a point in the plane: two real
numbers carried together, with a rule for combining them. Write down what the two parts
are, and what adding two of them would mean, and what the modulus would represent — a
distance, a size, an intensity.

If it does not fit — some two-part quantities do not behave like complex numbers, because
the multiplication rule is different — that is worth writing down too. Saying *why* a
model does not apply is part of the mathematics, and it is often more useful than forcing
it to fit.
