# 5.1 The imaginary unit and complex numbers — Practise with help

## Warm-up: remember

1. Solve $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$.
2. What is the discriminant of $x^2 + 2x + 5$, and what does its sign tell you?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers and feedback</summary>

1. $(x - 2)(x - 3) = 0$, so $x = 2$ or $x = 3$.
2. $b^2 - 4ac = 4 - 20 = -16$. A negative discriminant means there are no real roots —
   which is exactly the case this chapter gives an answer for.

</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Write down $i^2$, $i^3$ and $i^4$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start from $i^2 = -1$ and multiply by $i$ each time.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = -1 \cdot i$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$i^2 = -1 \qquad i^3 = -i \qquad i^4 = 1$$

Then $i^5 = i$, and the pattern repeats every four powers.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**2.** Simplify $i^{38}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide 38 by 4 and use the remainder.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$38 = 4 \times 9 + 2$, so $i^{38} = i^2$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$i^{38} = i^2 = -1$$

The remainder is 2, and $i^2 = -1$.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**3.** Write $\sqrt{-25}$ and $\sqrt{-7}$ in terms of $i$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Split the negative sign out: $\sqrt{-25} = \sqrt{25}\sqrt{-1}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$\sqrt{-1} = i$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$\sqrt{-25} = 5i \qquad \sqrt{-7} = \sqrt{7}\,i$$

$7$ has no whole-number square root, so $\sqrt{-7}$ stays as $\sqrt{7}i$. That is a
perfectly good exact answer — do not round it.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**4.** Write down the real and imaginary parts of $-2 + 6i$ and of $4i$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The imaginary part is the number multiplying $i$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

For $4i$, write it as $0 + 4i$ first.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

- $-2 + 6i$: real part $-2$, imaginary part $6$.
- $4i = 0 + 4i$: real part $0$, imaginary part $4$.

Writing $4i$ as $0 + 4i$ is the trick for reading the parts off a number with no visible
real part. Without it, the real part is easy to miss.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**5.** Solve $x^2 + 16 = 0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange to $x^2 = -16$, then take the square root of both sides.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$x = \pm\sqrt{-16}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$x^2 = -16 \qquad x = \pm 4i$$

There are two solutions, $4i$ and $-4i$. A quadratic always has two roots; here both are
purely imaginary.

</details>
</details>
</details>

**6.** A student writes: "$i$ is a variable, so $i^2 = -1$ can be solved to give
$i = \pm\sqrt{-1}$." Explain what is wrong with this.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Is $i$ an unknown to be found, or a definition?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$i$ is defined by $i^2 = -1$, not derived from it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$i$ is not a variable or an unknown to be solved for. It is **defined** as the number
whose square is $-1$. So $i^2 = -1$ is not an equation with a solution; it is the rule
that creates $i$.

The confusion comes from the square root. $\sqrt{-1}$ is not a real number, so writing
$i = \sqrt{-1}$ and treating the square-root rules as usual leads to contradictions — for
instance $\sqrt{-1}\sqrt{-1} = \sqrt{1} = 1$, which is wrong. Keep $i$ as a symbol
defined by $i^2 = -1$ and never apply real square-root rules to it.

</details>
</details>
</details>

## A question from Chapter 4

**7.** Solve $x^2 - 4x + 13 = 0$, and say what the discriminant tells you before you
start.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the quadratic formula. Watch the discriminant.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$b^2 - 4ac = 16 - 52 = -36$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The discriminant is $16 - 52 = -36$, which is negative, so there are no real roots. There
are still two complex roots:

$$x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm 6i}{2} = 2 \pm 3i$$

So $x = 2 + 3i$ or $x = 2 - 3i$. The two roots are conjugates, which Section 5.5 explains
is not an accident.

</details>
</details>
</details>
