# Chapter 5: Complex numbers

Some equations have no solution among the numbers you have met so far. $x^2 = -1$ is the
simplest one: no real number squares to a negative. This chapter invents the number that
does, and then shows that the arithmetic you already know carries over to it unchanged.

## The chapter problem

**The cubic with an impossible root.** You are asked to solve

$$x^3 - 5x^2 + 11x - 15 = 0$$

You can find one root by trying small whole numbers: $x = 3$ works. Dividing by
$(x - 3)$ leaves $x^2 - 2x + 5 = 0$, and this quadratic has **no real roots** — its
discriminant is $4 - 20 = -16$, which is negative.

By the end of the chapter you can say what the other two roots are, why they come as a
pair, and what they look like on a diagram. The equation has three roots, and two of them
are the reason this chapter exists.

## By the end of this chapter you can

- say what $i$ is and write a complex number in the form $a + bi$;
- add, subtract and multiply complex numbers, and simplify powers of $i$;
- find the complex conjugate and use it to divide complex numbers;
- plot a complex number on the Argand diagram and find its modulus;
- use the conjugate root theorem to find the roots of a real cubic.

## Where it is used

Electrical engineering, where alternating current is described by complex numbers;
signal processing and control; quantum mechanics; and any area of mathematics where
rotations or two-dimensional quantities appear. Every time you see a number of the form
$a + bi$, two real quantities are being carried together.

## Your turn to connect it

Have you met a quantity that needs **two** numbers to describe it, where the two are
combined in a way that is not simply a pair? A position on a map (two coordinates), a
force (size and direction), a colour (three, but the same idea).

Choose one. Write down what the two numbers are and what combining two of the quantities
would mean. You will be able to say something precise about it by the end of the chapter.
