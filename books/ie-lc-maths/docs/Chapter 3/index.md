# Chapter 3: Length, area and volume

Length is a distance along a line. Area is the amount of surface a shape covers. Volume is
the amount of space a solid takes up. This chapter measures all three, first on flat shapes
and then on solids, and it ends with the two roundest solids of all: the cylinder and the
sphere.

## The chapter problem: The two rain tanks

A sports club collects rainwater from the roof of its clubhouse. The club has room for one
tank, and two tanks are on offer.

- **Tank A** is a cylinder. Its base is a circle of diameter $1.2$ m, and its height is
  $1.2$ m.
- **Tank B** is a sphere of diameter $1.2$ m.

Both tanks are made from sheet metal that costs €45 per square metre. The club wants to
know three things: how much water each tank holds, how much metal each one needs, and
which tank gives more water for the money.

**First contact — no working yet.** Look at the two descriptions. Both tanks are $1.2$ m
across. Which one do you think holds more water? Write down your estimate, and write down
how many times more you expect it to hold. You will not need any formula for this first
guess, and there is no wrong answer yet.

By the end of the chapter you can work out both volumes exactly, work out the metal for
each tank, and give the club a clear answer. One of the two answers is a genuine surprise,
and Section 3.7 shows why it happens.

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- find the perimeter of any polygon and the area of a rectangle, triangle, parallelogram
  and trapezium;
- find the area of a composite shape by splitting it or subtracting from it;
- find the circumference and the area of a circle from its radius or its diameter;
- find the volume of a prism and of a cylinder;
- describe the net of a prism, a cylinder and a sphere;
- find the surface area of a prism and the curved surface area of a cylinder;
- use the length, area and volume ratios of similar figures;
- find the volume and the surface area of a sphere, and of a hemisphere.

## Where it is used

Anyone who buys paint needs area, because paint is sold by the square metre. Anyone who
orders concrete needs volume, because concrete is sold by the cubic metre. A farmer
measures a field in hectares, a plumber sizes a cylinder in litres, and a packaging
engineer works out how much cardboard a box needs from its net. The same formulas decide
how much a job costs.

## Your turn to connect it

Containers come in shapes that were chosen for them. A drinks can is a cylinder. A milk
carton is a box. A hot-water cylinder is a cylinder with rounded ends. A gas bottle is a
sphere with a neck.

Choose one container you have used. Write down its shape, and write down one reason you
think that shape was chosen. You will be able to say something quantitative about that
choice by the end of the chapter.
