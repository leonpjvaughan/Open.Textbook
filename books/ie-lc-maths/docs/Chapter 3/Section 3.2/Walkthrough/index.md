# 3.2 The circle: circumference and area — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 3.1 — area of a rectangle; multiplication and rounding of
decimals.

**Objectives:** find the circumference of a circle from its radius or diameter; find the
area of a circle from its radius or diameter; use $\pi$ exactly and as a decimal.

By the end of this section you can measure the distance around a circle and the surface
inside it.

## The number π

The **circumference** of a circle is the distance around it, and the **diameter** is the
distance across it through the centre. For every circle in the world, the circumference
divided by the diameter gives the same number:

$$\frac{\text{circumference}}{\text{diameter}} = \pi$$

$\pi$ (the Greek letter pi) is a little more than $3$. Its decimal never stops and never
repeats, and the first few figures are $3.14159\ldots$ The exact value is written $\pi$, and
that is the best way to write an answer unless the question asks for a decimal.

## Circumference

Because the circumference $C$ divided by the diameter $d$ is $\pi$,

$$C = \pi d$$

The radius $r$ is half the diameter, so $d = 2r$ and

$$C = 2\pi r$$

**Example 1.** Find the circumference of a circle of diameter $14$ cm.

$$C = \pi \times 14 = 14\pi \approx 43.98 \text{ cm}$$

**Example 2.** Find the circumference of a circle of radius $5$ m.

$$C = 2\pi \times 5 = 10\pi \approx 31.42 \text{ m}$$

Both formulas say the same thing. Use the one that matches the measurement you are given.

## Area

The area $A$ of a circle is

$$A = \pi r^2$$

Only the radius appears, and it is squared. The square of the radius is multiplied by
$\pi$, not the other way around.

**Example 3.** Find the area of a circle of radius $6$ cm.

$$A = \pi \times 6^2 = 36\pi \approx 113.10 \text{ cm}^2$$

**Example 4.** Find the area of a circle of diameter $20$ m.

The radius is half of $20$, so $r = 10$ m.

$$A = \pi \times 10^2 = 100\pi \approx 314.16 \text{ m}^2$$

## Why the area formula works

Cut a circle into many thin wedges and fit them together alternately. The wedges form a
shape close to a rectangle. Its height is the radius $r$, and its width is half the
circumference, $\pi r$. So

$$A \approx r \times \pi r = \pi r^2$$

The more wedges you cut, the closer the shape comes to a rectangle. That is why the area is
$\pi r^2$ and not $\pi r$ or $2\pi r$.

> **Watch out.** The radius and the diameter are different lengths, and mixing them up
> changes the answer. A circle of diameter $10$ cm has radius $5$ cm, so
> $A = \pi \times 5^2 = 25\pi \approx 78.5 \text{ cm}^2$. Using $10$ as the radius gives
> $100\pi$, which is four times too big. Square the radius first, then multiply by $\pi$:
> $\pi r^2$ means $\pi \times (r^2)$, never $(\pi r)^2$.

## Investigate: measure a circle

Find a round object — a tin, a cup or a coin. You need a piece of string, a ruler and a
sheet of paper.

**Predict.** Without measuring, write down your estimate of the circumference of the object
and your estimate of its diameter. Then write down the ratio you expect:
circumference divided by diameter.

**Test.** Wrap the string once around the object, mark the length, and measure it with the
ruler. Measure the diameter across the widest part. Divide the circumference by the
diameter, and write the result to 2 decimal places.

**Explain.** Write down your ratio and compare it with $\pi$. A small object and a large
object give the same ratio, so the ratio belongs to the circle, not to the object. Write
one sentence saying what your measurement shows, and one sentence saying why the
measurement is not exactly $3.14159\ldots$

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The base of Tank A is a circle of diameter $1.2$ m. The lid is
the same circle, and Tank B is a sphere of the same diameter.

Work out the circumference and the area of that circle of diameter $1.2$ m. Give the
circumference in metres and the area in square metres, each in exact form and then to
2 decimal places.

Keep both exact values. The area $0.36\pi$ is used again in Section 3.3 for the volume, and
the circumference $1.2\pi$ is used in Section 3.4 for the net.

## You should now be able to

- state what $\pi$ is and why it is the same number for every circle;
- find the circumference from a radius or a diameter;
- find the area from a radius or a diameter, squaring the radius first;
- write an answer in exact form with $\pi$, and as a decimal to a required accuracy.
