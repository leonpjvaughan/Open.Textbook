# 3.6 Similar figures: length, area and volume ratios — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 3.1 — area; Section 3.3 — volume; Section 3.5 — surface area;
ratio and proportion.

**Objectives:** find the scale factor from a pair of corresponding lengths; use the scale
factor to find an area ratio and a volume ratio; find a scale factor from an area or a
volume.

By the end of this section you can predict what happens to an area and to a volume when
every length is multiplied by the same number.

## Similar figures

Two figures are **similar** when they have the same shape but not necessarily the same
size. Every angle is equal, and every length in one figure is the same multiple of the
matching length in the other.

That common multiple is the **scale factor**, written $k$.

If a rectangle $2$ cm by $3$ cm is enlarged with scale factor $3$, the new rectangle is
$6$ cm by $9$ cm. Every length has been multiplied by $3$.

## Areas use k²

The small rectangle has area $2 \times 3 = 6 \text{ cm}^2$. The large one has area
$6 \times 9 = 54 \text{ cm}^2$. And

$$\frac{54}{6} = 9 = 3^2$$

The area has been multiplied by $k^2$, not by $k$. This happens because the enlargement
stretches the shape in two directions, and each direction contributes one factor of $k$.

$$\text{area ratio} = k^2$$

## Volumes use k³

A solid is stretched in three directions, so the volume is multiplied by $k^3$.

$$\text{volume ratio} = k^3$$

**Example 1.** A cuboid $2$ cm by $3$ cm by $4$ cm has volume $24 \text{ cm}^3$. It is
enlarged with scale factor $3$. Find the new volume.

$$24 \times 3^3 = 24 \times 27 = 648 \text{ cm}^3$$

The new cuboid is $6$ cm by $9$ cm by $12$ cm, and $6 \times 9 \times 12 = 648$, so the
rule agrees with the direct calculation.

## Why a cylinder follows the same rule

A cylinder of radius $r$ and height $h$ has volume $\pi r^2 h$. Enlarge it with scale
factor $k$, so the new radius is $kr$ and the new height is $kh$.

$$V_{\text{new}} = \pi (kr)^2 (kh) = \pi k^2 r^2 \cdot k h = k^3 \pi r^2 h = k^3 V_{\text{old}}$$

where $V_{\text{old}}$ and $V_{\text{new}}$ are the volumes before and after the
enlargement.

The two powers of $k$ from the radius and the one from the height make $k^3$. The surface
area works the same way, because every face is a flat area, and every flat area is
multiplied by $k^2$.

**Example 2.** Two similar cylinders have heights $4$ cm and $10$ cm. The small one has a
volume of $48 \text{ cm}^3$. Find the volume of the large one.

The scale factor is $k = \dfrac{10}{4} = 2.5$.

$$V = 48 \times 2.5^3 = 48 \times 15.625 = 750 \text{ cm}^3$$

**Example 3.** Two similar triangles have areas $25 \text{ cm}^2$ and $64 \text{ cm}^2$.
Find the ratio of their corresponding sides.

The area ratio is $\dfrac{64}{25}$, so the length ratio is
$\sqrt{\dfrac{64}{25}} = \dfrac{8}{5}$. The scale factor is $1.6$.

> **Watch out.** The scale factor applies to lengths, and only to lengths. If two similar
> solids have a scale factor of $2$, their volumes are in the ratio $8 : 1$, not
> $2 : 1$. Doubling every length of a box multiplies its volume by $8$, because the box
> doubles in three directions. Check the direction of the question: a length ratio is
> square-rooted to give an area ratio, and an area ratio is square-rooted to give a length
> ratio.

## Progress check

Back to the chapter problem. The club is offered a larger version of Tank A. Every length
of the larger tank is twice the matching length of Tank A, so it is similar to Tank A with
scale factor $2$.

Tank A has volume $0.432\pi \text{ m}^3$ and total surface area $2.16\pi \text{ m}^2$.
Work out the volume and the surface area of the larger tank, using the ratios rather than
the formulas.

Then compare the two ratios. The volume is multiplied by $8$ and the surface area by $4$,
so the metal per cubic metre has changed. Work out the new metal per cubic metre and
compare it with Tank A. This is the same comparison that Section 3.7 makes between Tank A
and the sphere.

## You should now be able to

- say what makes two figures similar, and find the scale factor $k$;
- use $k^2$ for an area ratio and $k^3$ for a volume ratio;
- find a scale factor from a pair of areas or volumes by taking a square root or a cube
  root;
- explain why an enlargement stretches a volume in three directions.
