# Chapter 3: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

**The two rain tanks.** Tank A is a closed cylinder of diameter $1.2$ m and height
$1.2$ m. Tank B is a sphere of diameter $1.2$ m. The sheet metal costs €45 per square
metre. Which tank gives more water for the money?

Both tanks have radius $r = 0.6$ m.

**Tank A.** The volume of a cylinder is $\pi r^2 h$, so

$$V_A = \pi \times 0.6^2 \times 1.2 = 0.432\pi \approx 1.36 \text{ m}^3$$

That is about $1360$ litres. The total surface area of a closed cylinder is
$2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h$, so

$$A_A = 2\pi \times 0.36 + 2\pi \times 0.6 \times 1.2 = 0.72\pi + 1.44\pi = 2.16\pi \approx 6.79 \text{ m}^2$$

At €45 per square metre, the metal costs

$$2.16\pi \times 45 = 97.2\pi$$

which is €305.36 to the nearest cent.

**Tank B.** The volume of a sphere is $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, so

$$V_B = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi \times 0.6^3 = 0.288\pi \approx 0.905 \text{ m}^3$$

That is about $905$ litres. The surface area of a sphere is $4\pi r^2$, so

$$A_B = 4\pi \times 0.36 = 1.44\pi \approx 4.52 \text{ m}^2$$

The metal costs

$$1.44\pi \times 45 = 64.8\pi$$

which is €203.58 to the nearest cent.

**The comparison.** Tank A holds more water, and it needs more metal:

$$\frac{V_A}{V_B} = \frac{0.432\pi}{0.288\pi} = 1.5, \qquad \frac{A_A}{A_B} = \frac{2.16\pi}{1.44\pi} = 1.5$$

The two ratios are equal. Tank A holds $1.5$ times as much water and needs $1.5$ times as
much metal, so the metal per cubic metre is the same for both:

$$\frac{A_A}{V_A} = \frac{2.16\pi}{0.432\pi} = 5, \qquad \frac{A_B}{V_B} = \frac{1.44\pi}{0.288\pi} = 5$$

Both tanks need exactly $5$ square metres of metal for every cubic metre of water. The
cost per litre is €0.225 for each one. The surprise is that there is no better-value tank
here: the two shapes cost the same per litre.

**Where the coincidence comes from.** The height of Tank A is twice its radius. Write
$h = 2r$:

$$A_A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r(2r) = 6\pi r^2, \qquad V_A = \pi r^2 (2r) = 2\pi r^3, \qquad \frac{A_A}{V_A} = \frac{3}{r}$$

For the sphere:

$$A_B = 4\pi r^2, \qquad V_B = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3, \qquad \frac{A_B}{V_B} = \frac{3}{r}$$

The two are equal for every radius, not only for $r = 0.6$ m. A cylinder whose height is
its diameter has exactly the same metal per unit volume as a sphere of the same radius.

**The answer for the club.** Since the metal per litre is the same, the club should choose
on capacity and on cost. Tank A holds $1.5$ times as much water for $1.5$ times the money,
so both tanks are fair value. If the club wants the most water and can pay €305.36, it
should take Tank A. If it has about €200 to spend, Tank B is the honest choice.

**The better idea.** Section 3.6 gives the club a third option. A similar tank with every
length twice as large holds $2^3 = 8$ times the water and needs only $2^2 = 4$ times the
metal. Its metal per cubic metre is $\dfrac{5}{2} = 2.5$, half of either tank. For any
shape, a larger version of it costs less per litre, because the volume grows as $k^3$ and
the surface area only as $k^2$. The best value is the largest similar tank that fits and
that the club can afford.

## Mixed practice — choose the method first

Work through these without notes. For each one, say **which method you will use and why**
before you calculate. The questions mix every section of the chapter, and two of them ask
for part of a solid rather than all of it.

1. A cylinder has radius $4$ cm and height $9$ cm. Find its volume and its total surface
   area.
2. A trapezium has parallel sides $5$ m and $11$ m, and a perpendicular height of $3$ m.
   Find its area.
3. A circle has diameter $18$ cm. Find its circumference and its area.
4. Two similar cuboids have corresponding lengths $4$ cm and $10$ cm. Find the ratio of
   their volumes.
5. A hemisphere has radius $7$ cm. Find its total surface area.
6. A cylinder has radius $6$ cm and height $5$ cm. Find the width and the area of the
   rectangle in its net.
7. A tank holds $3.6 \text{ m}^3$ of water. How many litres is that?
8. A sphere of radius $6$ cm has volume $288\pi \approx 904.78 \text{ cm}^3$. Write this
   volume to 2 significant figures.
9. Two cylindrical tins have the same height. The first has radius $3$ cm and the second
   has radius $6$ cm. How many times does the second tin hold?
10. A cylinder has radius $10$ cm and height $15$ cm, and it has no lid. Find its surface
    area.
11. A water tank is a cylinder of diameter $2$ m and height $3$ m. The club wants to know
    how much paint covers the **curved side only**. Which formula applies, and why?

## What you can now do

- find the perimeter of a polygon and the area of a rectangle, a triangle, a parallelogram
  and a trapezium;
- find the area of a composite shape by splitting it or subtracting from it;
- find the circumference and the area of a circle, halving a diameter first;
- find the volume of a prism from its cross-section, and of a cylinder from $\pi r^2 h$;
- convert between cubic metres, cubic centimetres and litres;
- describe the net of a prism and of a cylinder, and explain why a sphere has no flat net;
- find the curved surface area and the total surface area of a cylinder, and adjust for a
  missing lid;
- use $k$, $k^2$ and $k^3$ for the length, area and volume ratios of similar figures;
- find the volume and the surface area of a sphere, and the total surface of a hemisphere;
- compare two containers by the material each one needs for every litre it holds.

## Thinking back

Return to the container you chose at the start of the chapter — a drinks can, a milk
carton, a hot-water cylinder or a gas bottle.

Now you can say something quantitative about it. Write down:

- the shape of the container, and which section of this chapter measures it;
- an estimate of its volume, and the formula you would use to check it;
- how much material its surface needs, and whether the lid or the base is counted;
- one reason the shape was chosen, and whether that reason is about cost, strength,
  stacking or the way the contents pour.

If the container is a sphere or a hemisphere, say how you handled the curved surface and
the flat face. Saying which faces you counted is part of the mathematics, and it is the
step that most answers leave out.
