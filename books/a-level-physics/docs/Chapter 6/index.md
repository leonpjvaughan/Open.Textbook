# Chapter 6: Waves

## The chapter problem: the concert hall check

A hall is being prepared for a concert with a sound and light show. The
technician has to check the equipment before the audience arrives.

These are the facts to use all the way through the chapter.

- The two main loudspeakers are **1.20 m** apart. A signal generator drives them
  **in phase** at **1.00 kHz**. The speed of sound in the hall is
  **340 m s⁻¹**.
- The light show uses a red laser of wavelength **633 nm**.
- The laser beam passes through a **double slit** whose two slits are
  **0.25 mm** apart, and the pattern falls on a screen **2.50 m** away.
- The same beam also falls on a **diffraction grating** ruled with
  **300 lines per millimetre**.
- A **polarising filter** sets the plane of polarisation of the beam, and a
  second filter is turned until its axis is at **60°** to the first.
- A guitar string is fixed at both ends. It is **0.650 m** long, its mass per
  unit length is **4.0 × 10⁻⁴ kg m⁻¹**, and its tension is **81 N**. It is meant
  to sound the note E₄, which has a frequency of **330 Hz**.

By the end of the chapter you will find the wavelength of the sound and the angle
of the first loud band. You will find the spacing of the light fringes and the
angles of the grating maxima. You will work out the wave speed and the lowest
frequency of the guitar string. Then you will decide whether the string is tuned
to E₄ and what the technician must change.

> **First contact: no working yet.** Predict. The two loudspeakers play the same
> note, so in some places the sound is loud and in others it is quiet. Suppose
> the technician changes the note to a higher one. Do the loud and quiet places
> stay where they are, or do they move? Write down your answer and one sentence
> of reasoning.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>If you are not sure where to start</summary>

The places where the sound is loud depend on how the two waves arrive together.
That depends on the distance between the two paths, measured in wavelengths. A
different note has a different wavelength, even though the speakers have not
moved.
</details>

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- describe a progressive wave, and tell a transverse wave from a longitudinal
  wave;
- use $v = f\lambda$ and $T = \dfrac{1}{f}$;
- find the phase difference between two points on a wave;
- state the principle of superposition, and explain constructive and destructive
  interference;
- use path difference to decide whether two waves reinforce or cancel;
- explain what makes two sources coherent;
- use $w = \dfrac{\lambda D}{s}$ for the fringes from two slits;
- describe diffraction, and explain how the size of a gap changes the pattern;
- use $d\sin\theta = n\lambda$ for a diffraction grating, and find the highest
  order it can give;
- explain what polarisation is and why only transverse waves can show it;
- describe what a polarising filter does to light, and what happens when two are
  crossed;
- explain how a stationary wave forms, and define a node and an antinode;
- find the harmonics of a string and of an air column open at one or both ends;
- use $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$ for a wave on a string.

## Where it is used

A sound engineer places the speakers at a concert and uses interference to
understand why some seats sound better than others. Noise-cancelling headphones
superpose a wave on the noise so the two cancel. A spectroscopist uses a
diffraction grating to split starlight into its wavelengths, and so find out what
a star is made of. Polarising sunglasses cut the glare that reflects from water
and roads. A guitar maker chooses the string's length, tension and thickness to
set its note. Medical ultrasound and radio antennas both depend on the same wave
ideas.
