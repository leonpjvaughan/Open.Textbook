# 6.2 Diffraction and polarisation — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check. Angles from a grating are measured from the straight-through
direction.

## Warm-up: remember

1. A wave has a wavelength of 0.60 m and a frequency of 550 Hz. Find its speed.
2. Two points on a wave are $90^\circ$ apart in phase. What fraction of a
   wavelength is the distance between them?
3. Write $6.33 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$ in nanometres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $v = f\lambda = 550 \times 0.60 = 330\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.
2. $90^\circ$ is one quarter of $360^\circ$, so the distance is $\dfrac{\lambda}{4}$.
3. $6.33 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m} = 633\,\text{nm}$.

If question 1 goes wrong, use $v = f\lambda$. If question 2 goes wrong, remember
that a full cycle is $360^\circ$. If question 3 goes wrong, one nanometre is
$10^{-9}\,\text{m}$.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A diffraction grating has 500 lines per millimetre. Find the spacing $d$
between neighbouring slits, in metres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The spacing is one divided by the number of lines per metre. Change the lines per
millimetre into lines per metre first.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$500\,\text{lines mm}^{-1} = 5.00 \times 10^5\,\text{lines m}^{-1}$. Then use
$d = \dfrac{1}{N}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
N &= 5.00 \times 10^{5}\,\text{m}^{-1} \\
d &= \frac{1}{N} = \frac{1}{5.00 \times 10^{5}} = 2.00 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The slits are $2.00 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$ apart.

</details>

**2.** Light of wavelength 633 nm falls on a grating of spacing
$2.00 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$. Find the angle of the first-order maximum.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $d\sin\theta = n\lambda$ with $n = 1$. Change the wavelength to metres first.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out $\sin\theta = \dfrac{633 \times 10^{-9}}{2.00 \times 10^{-6}}$, then take
the inverse sine.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin\theta &= \frac{n\lambda}{d} = \frac{633 \times 10^{-9}}{2.00 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.3165 \\
\theta &= 18.5^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

The first-order maximum is at $18.5^\circ$ from the straight-through direction.

</details>

**3.** For the grating and light in question 2, find the highest order the
grating can give.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The order is possible while $\sin\theta$ is no more than 1, which means
$n\lambda \le d$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Divide the slit spacing by the wavelength: $\dfrac{d}{\lambda}$. The highest
order is the largest whole number below that value.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ \frac{d}{\lambda} = \frac{2.00 \times 10^{-6}}{633 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.16 $$

The order must be a whole number and $n\lambda$ must be no more than $d$. The
largest such number is 3, so the highest order is 3. Order 4 would need
$\sin\theta = 1.27$, which is impossible.

</details>

**4.** A grating of spacing $1.667 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$ gives its
first-order maximum at $22.0^\circ$. Find the wavelength of the light.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange the grating equation to make the wavelength the subject.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

With $n = 1$, the wavelength is $d\sin\theta$. Find $\sin 22.0^\circ$ first.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\sin\theta &= n\lambda \\
\lambda &= d\sin\theta = 1.667 \times 10^{-6} \times \sin 22.0^\circ \\
        &= 1.667 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.3746 = 6.24 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The wavelength is $6.24 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$, which is 624 nm.

</details>

**5.** Plane-polarised light passes through an analyser whose axis is at
$45^\circ$ to the plane of polarisation. Find the fraction of the intensity that
passes through.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The intensity follows the square of the cosine of the angle.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out $\cos 45^\circ$ first, then square it.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ \frac{I}{I_0} = \cos^2 45^\circ = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 = 0.50 $$

Half of the intensity passes through the analyser.

</details>

**6.** Explain why light can be polarised but sound cannot. Include the words
transverse and longitudinal in your answer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Polarisation is about the direction in which the vibration happens, compared with
the direction of travel.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say what the particles do in each type of wave, and which direction a filter can
pick out.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Light is a transverse wave, so its electric field vibrates at right angles to the
direction of travel. That vibration has a direction across the beam, and a
polarising filter can select the component along one line. Sound is a
longitudinal wave. Its particles vibrate along the direction of travel, so there
is no crossways direction for a filter to select. Polarisation is therefore
possible only for transverse waves.

</details>
