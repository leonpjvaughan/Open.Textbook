# Chapter 6: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

The technician is checking a hall before a concert. The loudspeakers are 1.20 m
apart and are driven in phase at 1.00 kHz. The speed of sound is
$340\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. The laser has a wavelength of 633 nm and is used
with a double slit 0.25 mm apart and a screen 2.50 m away, and with a grating of
300 lines per millimetre. The guitar string is 0.650 m long, has a mass per unit
length of $4.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{kg}\,\text{m}^{-1}$, and a tension of 81 N.
It is meant to sound E₄, 330 Hz.

**1. The wavelength of the sound.**

$$ \lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{340}{1.00 \times 10^{3}} = 0.340\,\text{m} $$

**2. The first loud band.** The speakers are coherent, so the maxima lie where
$s\sin\theta = n\lambda$. For the first maximum, $n = 1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin\theta &= \frac{\lambda}{s} = \frac{0.340}{1.20} = 0.2833 \\
\theta &= 16.5^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

A listener hears the first loud band at $16.5^\circ$ to the centre line. On the
centre line the two paths are equal, so the path difference is zero and the sound
is loud there too.

**3. The spacing of the light fringes.**

$$ w = \frac{\lambda D}{s} = \frac{633 \times 10^{-9} \times 2.50}{0.25 \times 10^{-3}} = 6.33 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m} $$

The fringes are 6.33 mm apart.

**4. The grating spacing.** The grating has 300 lines per millimetre, which is
$3.00 \times 10^{5}$ lines per metre.

$$ d = \frac{1}{N} = \frac{1}{3.00 \times 10^{5}} = 3.33 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m} $$

**5. The first-order angle.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin\theta &= \frac{\lambda}{d} = \frac{633 \times 10^{-9}}{3.33 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.190 \\
\theta &= 10.9^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

The first-order maximum is at $10.9^\circ$ from the straight-through direction.
Because the first order is well clear of the centre, the audience sees several
bright spots.

**6. The highest order the grating can give.** The order must satisfy
$n\lambda \le d$.

$$ \frac{d}{\lambda} = \frac{3.33 \times 10^{-6}}{633 \times 10^{-9}} = 5.26 $$

The highest whole number is 5, so the grating gives orders 0 to 5, with five
maxima on each side of the centre.

**7. The speed of a wave on the guitar string.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{81}{4.0 \times 10^{-4}}} \\
  &= \sqrt{2.025 \times 10^{5}} = 450\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

**8. The fundamental frequency of the string.**

$$ f_1 = \frac{v}{2L} = \frac{450}{2 \times 0.650} = 346\,\text{Hz} $$

**9. The polarised light.** The analyser axis is at $60^\circ$ to the plane of
polarisation, so the fraction of the intensity that passes is

$$ \frac{I}{I_0} = \cos^2 60^\circ = 0.25 $$

One quarter of the light passes through, so the spot is dimmer but still
visible.

**10. The judgement on the string.** The string sounds 346 Hz, and the note E₄ is
330 Hz. The measured frequency is too high, so the string is too tight. The
technician must **slacken** it. The tension needed is

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_2 &= T_1\left(\frac{f_2}{f_1}\right)^2 \\
    &= 81 \times \left(\frac{330}{346}\right)^2 = 73.6\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

The tension should be reduced from 81 N to about 74 N. This final judgement is a
reasoned answer. It is not the kind of claim a computer can check, so compare
your reasoning with the evidence above.

**11. The first prediction.** The loud and quiet places **move** when the note
changes. Their positions depend on the path difference measured in wavelengths,
and a higher note has a shorter wavelength. The same path difference is then a
different number of wavelengths, so the places of reinforcement and cancellation
are in new positions.

## What you can now do

- describe a progressive wave, and tell a transverse wave from a longitudinal
  one;
- use $v = f\lambda$ and $T = \dfrac{1}{f}$;
- find a phase difference and a path difference;
- state the principle of superposition, and decide whether waves reinforce or
  cancel;
- explain what makes two sources coherent;
- use the fringe formula for two slits;
- describe diffraction, and explain how the gap size changes the pattern;
- use the grating equation, and find the highest order a grating can give;
- explain polarisation, and work out the light through an analyser;
- explain how a stationary wave forms;
- find the harmonics of a string and of an air column;
- use $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$ for a wave on a string;
- check whether an instrument is tuned to the note it should sound.
