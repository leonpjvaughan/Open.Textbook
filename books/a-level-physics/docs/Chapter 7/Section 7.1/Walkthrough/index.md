# 7.1 Internal energy and specific heat capacity — Learn

**Prerequisites:** work, energy and power from Section 3.3; current, potential
difference and electrical power from Section 5.1; SI prefixes from Section 1.1.

**By the end of this section you can:** explain what internal energy is; explain
how temperature and internal energy differ; use $E = mc\Delta\theta$; find a
specific heat capacity from an experiment; explain what happens to the energy
during a change of state; use $E = mL$; and read a heating curve.

## 1. Internal energy

A **system** is the object, or the group of objects, that we are studying. Its
**internal energy** is the total kinetic energy plus the total potential energy
of all the particles in it. The particles are always moving, so they always have
kinetic energy. They also pull on one another, so they have potential energy.

The **temperature** of a system tells you the **average** random kinetic energy
of its particles. It does not tell you the total energy stored.

This difference matters. A full bath of warm water at 40 °C stores far more
internal energy than a teaspoon of boiling water at 100 °C. The bath is cooler,
but it contains very many more particles.

Heating a system transfers energy to it. Its particles move faster on average, so
its internal energy rises and its temperature rises. Doing work on a system, for
example by rubbing or by compressing it, also transfers energy and changes its
internal energy.

> **Watch out.** Temperature is not the energy stored. A hot spark from a firework
> is at a very high temperature, but it stores very little internal energy. A bath
> is at a lower temperature, but it stores far more. Temperature measures the
> average kinetic energy of one particle. Internal energy is the total over all
> the particles.

## 2. Specific heat capacity

The energy needed to warm a system depends on its mass, the material it is made
of, and the temperature change. These are linked by

$$ E = mc\Delta\theta $$

where $E$ is the energy transferred, in joules (J); $m$ is the mass, in
kilograms (kg); $c$ is the **specific heat capacity**, in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹; and
$\Delta\theta$ is the temperature change.

The **specific heat capacity** of a substance is the energy needed to raise the
temperature of **1 kg** of it by **1 K**. Water has a large specific heat
capacity, 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. That is why water takes a long time to warm up, and
why it carries energy around a central heating system.

A change of 1 °C is the same as a change of 1 K, so $\Delta\theta$ is the same
number in either unit.

**Worked example 1.** A pressure cooker holds 1.50 kg of water at 20 °C. The
specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Find the energy needed to
heat the water to 121 °C.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= mc\Delta\theta \\
  &= 1.50 \times 4200 \times (121 - 20) \\
  &= 1.50 \times 4200 \times 101 \\
  &= 6.36 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

The water needs $6.36 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}$ to reach its boiling point. The
temperature change is 101 K, which is the same as 101 °C.

## 3. Changes of state and latent heat

A **change of state** happens when a substance turns from one state to another.
Ice melts to water, water boils to steam, steam condenses back to water, and
water freezes to ice.

During a change of state the temperature does not change. The energy supplied is
used to break the bonds between the particles, not to speed them up. The
potential energy of the particles rises, so the internal energy rises, but their
average kinetic energy stays the same.

The **specific latent heat** of a substance is the energy needed to change the
state of **1 kg** of it, with no change in temperature:

$$ E = mL $$

where $E$ is the energy transferred (J), $m$ is the mass (kg), and $L$ is the
specific latent heat (J kg⁻¹). There are two kinds to know:

- the **specific latent heat of fusion**, for melting and freezing;
- the **specific latent heat of vaporisation**, for boiling and condensing.

**Worked example 2.** The specific latent heat of vaporisation of water is
$2.26 \times 10^{6}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$. Find the energy needed to turn
0.050 kg of boiling water into steam at the same temperature.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= mL \\
  &= 0.050 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6} \\
  &= 1.13 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

Boiling 0.050 kg of water needs $1.13 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}$. Notice that this
is a lot of energy for a small mass. Breaking the bonds between water molecules
takes much more energy than warming them.

## 4. Heating and changing state together

Many real processes do both: they warm a substance and then change its state.
Work out the energy for each stage, then add the two amounts.

**Worked example 3.** Find the total energy needed to heat 1.50 kg of water from
20 °C to 121 °C and then turn 0.050 kg of it into steam. Use
$c = 4200\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$ and
$L = 2.26 \times 10^{6}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\text{heat}} &= mc\Delta\theta \\
  &= 1.50 \times 4200 \times 101 = 6.36 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J} \\
E_{\text{boil}} &= mL \\
  &= 0.050 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6} = 1.13 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J} \\
E_{\text{total}} &= 6.36 \times 10^{5} + 1.13 \times 10^{5}
  = 7.49 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

The two stages need very different amounts of energy for very different masses.
This is the chapter problem, and the Review returns to it.

**Worked example 4.** A student measures the specific heat capacity of
aluminium. An aluminium block of mass 1.0 kg is heated by a 12 V, 2.0 A heater
for 300 s. Its temperature rises by 8.0 K. Find the specific heat capacity of the
aluminium.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= IVt \\
  &= 2.0 \times 12 \times 300 = 7200\,\text{J} \\
c &= \frac{E}{m\Delta\theta} \\
  &= \frac{7200}{1.0 \times 8.0} = 900\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The specific heat capacity is $900\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$. In
the experiment, some energy warms the room and the block loses energy to its
surroundings, so the true value is a little lower than this.

**Worked example 5.** A 700 W heater warms 2.0 kg of water for 300 s. Find the
temperature rise. The specific heat capacity of water is
$4200\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= Pt = 700 \times 300 = 2.1 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J} \\
\Delta\theta &= \frac{E}{mc}
  = \frac{2.1 \times 10^{5}}{2.0 \times 4200} = 25\,\text{K}
\end{aligned}
$$

The water warms by 25 K.

## 5. Reading a heating curve

A graph of temperature against time for a substance heated at a steady rate is a
**heating curve**. It has sloping parts where the substance is warming up, and
flat parts where it is changing state. The flat parts are called plateaus.

During a plateau, the energy supplied is changing the state, so the temperature
stays the same. The internal energy still rises, because the potential energy of
the particles rises.

The length of a plateau shows the latent heat. For water, the boiling plateau is
much longer than the melting plateau. The specific latent heat of vaporisation is
$2.26 \times 10^{6}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$, while the specific latent heat of
fusion is only $3.34 \times 10^{5}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$. The same mass of
water needs about seven times as much energy to boil as to melt.

> **Watch out.** A plateau does not mean that the energy has stopped arriving.
> Energy is still being supplied at a steady rate, and the internal energy is
> still rising. The temperature is constant because the energy is changing the
> arrangement of the particles, not their average speed. If you read the plateau
> as "no energy", every later calculation will be too small.

## Progress check

Return to the pressure cooker. It holds 1.50 kg of water at 20 °C, and the water
boils at 121 °C.

1. Find the temperature change as the water is heated to boiling.
2. Find the energy needed to heat the water to boiling. Use
   $c = 4200\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$.
3. Find the energy needed to turn 0.050 kg of the boiling water into steam. Use
   $L = 2.26 \times 10^{6}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$.
4. Explain why the temperature stays at 121 °C while the water boils.

If question 1 goes wrong, subtract 20 from 121. If question 2 goes wrong, use
$E = mc\Delta\theta$ with $\Delta\theta = 101\,\text{K}$. If question 3 goes
wrong, use $E = mL$. If question 4 goes wrong, say what the energy is doing to
the bonds between the particles.
