# 9.1 Gravitational fields — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check. Measure every distance from the **centre** of the planet, and
keep the minus sign when you write a gravitational potential.

## Warm-up: remember

1. A satellite has a mass of 500 kg and a weight of 4500 N. Find the
   gravitational field strength at its position.
2. A car goes round a bend of radius 50 m at a speed of
   $20\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. Find its centripetal acceleration.
3. How much energy is needed to lift a 5.0 kg mass through a height of 2.0 m?
   Use $g = 9.81\,\text{N}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $g = \dfrac{W}{m} = \dfrac{4500}{500} = 9.0\,\text{N}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$.
2. $a = \dfrac{v^{2}}{r} = \dfrac{20^{2}}{50} = 8.0\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-2}$.
3. $E = mgh = 5.0 \times 9.81 \times 2.0 = 98\,\text{J}$.

If question 1 goes wrong, revisit weight and the field strength in Section 3.2.
If question 2 goes wrong, revisit circular motion in Section 3.5. If question 3
goes wrong, revisit gravitational potential energy in Section 3.3.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Two spheres, each of mass 1000 kg, are 2.0 m apart. Find the
gravitational force between them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Both masses are the same, so the numerator of Newton's law is $Gm^{2}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $F = \dfrac{GMm}{r^{2}}$ with
$M = m = 1000\,\text{kg}$ and $r = 2.0\,\text{m}$. Square the distance first.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= \frac{GMm}{r^{2}}
   = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1000 \times 1000}{2.0^{2}} \\
  &= \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-5}}{4.0}
   = 1.7 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

The force is $1.7 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{N}$. Gravity is very weak between
everyday masses, which is why you do not notice it.
</details>

**2.** Mars has a mass of $6.42 \times 10^{23}\,\text{kg}$ and a radius of
$3.39 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$. Find the gravitational field strength at its
surface.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At the surface, the distance $r$ is the radius of the planet.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Use $g = \dfrac{GM}{r^{2}}$, and square $3.39 \times 10^{6}$ before you divide.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
g &= \frac{GM}{r^{2}}
   = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.42 \times 10^{23}}{(3.39 \times 10^{6})^{2}} \\
  &= \frac{4.28 \times 10^{13}}{1.15 \times 10^{13}}
   = 3.73\,\text{N}\,\text{kg}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The field strength at the surface of Mars is
$3.73\,\text{N}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$. A 70 kg astronaut would weigh 261 N there.
</details>

**3.** The gravitational field strength at the Earth's surface is
$9.81\,\text{N}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$. Find the field strength at a distance of
$2R$ from the centre, where $R$ is the Earth's radius.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

You do not need $G$ or the mass of the Earth. The field strength follows an
inverse-square law.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out what happens to $g$ when $r$ becomes $2R$: the distance is doubled, so
the field strength is divided by $2^{2}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
g = \frac{GM}{(2R)^{2}} = \frac{GM}{4R^{2}} = \frac{g_{\text{surface}}}{4}
  = \frac{9.81}{4} = 2.45\,\text{N}\,\text{kg}^{-1}
$$

The field strength at $2R$ is $2.45\,\text{N}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$, one quarter of
the surface value.
</details>

**4.** Find the gravitational potential at the surface of Mars, using the data
in question 2.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The potential is the energy per unit mass, and it is negative.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Use $V = -\dfrac{GM}{R}$ with the same numbers as question 2, and keep the minus
sign.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= -\frac{GM}{R}
   = -\frac{4.28 \times 10^{13}}{3.39 \times 10^{6}} \\
  &= -1.26 \times 10^{7}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The potential is $-1.26 \times 10^{7}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$. Moving a 1 kg
mass from the surface to infinity needs $1.26 \times 10^{7}\,\text{J}$.
</details>

**5.** A satellite orbits the Earth in a circle of radius
$7.0 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$. Find its orbital speed and its period.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Gravity provides the centripetal force, so set the two force expressions equal.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Use $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$ with
$GM = 3.98 \times 10^{14}\,\text{m}^{3}\,\text{s}^{-2}$, then divide the
circumference by the speed.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{7.0 \times 10^{6}}} \\
  &= \sqrt{5.69 \times 10^{7}} = 7.5 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}
  = \frac{2\pi \times 7.0 \times 10^{6}}{7.5 \times 10^{3}}
  = 5.9 \times 10^{3}\,\text{s}
$$

The speed is $7.5\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ and the period is 5900 s, or about
98 minutes.
</details>

**6.** Find the escape speed from the Moon. The mass of the Moon is
$7.35 \times 10^{22}\,\text{kg}$ and its radius is
$1.74 \times 10^{6}\,\text{m}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At escape the kinetic energy equals the energy needed to reach infinity.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Use $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{R}}$. Calculate $2GM$ first, because the
factor of 2 is often dropped.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{\text{esc}} &= \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}
   = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 7.35 \times 10^{22}}{1.74 \times 10^{6}}} \\
  &= \sqrt{5.63 \times 10^{6}} = 2.4 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The escape speed from the Moon is $2.4\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}$, about one
fifth of the escape speed from the Earth.
</details>

**7.** A 200 kg satellite is moved from the Earth's surface, where the potential
is $-6.25 \times 10^{7}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$, to a higher orbit where the
potential is $-5.00 \times 10^{7}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$. Find the energy
needed.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The energy depends on the potential difference, not on either potential alone.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Find $\Delta V$ by subtracting the two negative values carefully. Then multiply
by the mass.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\Delta V = (-5.00 \times 10^{7}) - (-6.25 \times 10^{7})
         = 1.25 \times 10^{7}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}
$$

$$
E = m\,\Delta V = 200 \times 1.25 \times 10^{7}
  = 2.5 \times 10^{9}\,\text{J}
$$

The energy needed is $2.5 \times 10^{9}\,\text{J}$.
</details>

**8.** Explain why an astronaut in orbit feels weightless even though the
gravitational field strength there is $8.69\,\text{N}\,\text{kg}^{-1}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Weightlessness is about the reading on a balance, not about the force of
gravity.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say what force acts on the astronaut and the spacecraft, and what happens to
both of them because of it.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Gravity still acts on the astronaut, with a force of about 608 N. The astronaut
and the spacecraft are both falling towards the Earth with the same
acceleration, because gravity provides the centripetal force for the orbit. The
astronaut does not press on the floor of the spacecraft, so a balance would read
zero. This is free fall, not a lack of gravity.
</details>
