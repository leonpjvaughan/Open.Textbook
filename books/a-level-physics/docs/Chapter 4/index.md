# Chapter 4: Materials

## The chapter problem: the crane cable

A crane lifts a steel beam on a building site. The beam hangs from one vertical
steel cable.

These are the facts to use all the way through the chapter.

- The beam has a mass of **2500 kg**.
- The cable is **20.0 m** long when no load hangs on it.
- The cable has a cross-sectional area of **1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m²**.
- The steel has a Young modulus of **2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa**.
- The cable has a breaking stress of **1.5 × 10⁹ Pa**.
- Lifting rules need a safety factor of **5**. The working stress must not
  exceed one fifth of the breaking stress, which is **3.0 × 10⁸ Pa**.

By the end of the chapter you will find the stress and the strain in the cable.
You will work out how far the cable stretches and the energy stored in it. You
will then decide whether the cable is safe for the beam, and find the heaviest
beam it can lift under the safety rule.

> **First contact: no working yet.** Estimate. The cable is 20 m long. Under the
> load it stretches a little. Does it stretch by about a millimetre, about a
> centimetre, or about a metre? Write down your estimate and one sentence of
> reasoning.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>If you are not sure where to start</summary>

The extension is the original length multiplied by the strain. The strain is
the stress divided by the Young modulus. The stress is the load divided by the
cross-sectional area. Estimate the stress first, then the strain, then the
extension.
</details>

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- explain why engineers use stress and strain instead of force and extension;
- calculate the tensile stress in a wire or cable;
- calculate the tensile strain, and state that it has no unit;
- define the Young modulus, and use it to find a stress, a strain or an
  extension;
- find the spring constant from the gradient of a force-extension graph;
- describe the limit of proportionality, the elastic limit and plastic
  deformation;
- find the energy stored in a stretched material from the area under its
  force-extension graph;
- use $E = \tfrac{1}{2}F\Delta L$ when the graph is a straight line through the
  origin;
- check whether a cable is safe for a load.

## Where it is used

A structural engineer chooses the cables of a bridge, a lift or a crane. The
choice depends on the stress the cable will carry, not on the load alone. A
climbing rope is made to stretch, so it can absorb the energy of a fall. A
dentist chooses a wire for a brace that bends by the right amount. The same
ideas explain why a thin wire breaks under a load that a thick bar of the same
steel holds safely.
