# Chapter 4: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

The crane lifts a steel beam of mass 2500 kg on a cable 20.0 m long. The cable
has a cross-sectional area of $1.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$, the steel has a
Young modulus of $2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\text{Pa}$, and the working stress must
not exceed $3.0 \times 10^{8}\,\text{Pa}$.

**1. The load in the cable.** The cable carries the weight of the beam.

$$ W = mg = 2500 \times 9.81 = 24\,525\,\text{N} $$

**2. The stress in the cable.**

$$ \sigma = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{24\,525}{1.5 \times 10^{-4}} = 1.635 \times 10^{8}\,\text{Pa} $$

The stress is about $1.64 \times 10^{8}\,\text{Pa}$, or $164\,\text{MPa}$.

**3. The strain in the cable.**

$$ \varepsilon = \frac{\sigma}{E} = \frac{1.635 \times 10^{8}}{2.0 \times 10^{11}} = 8.175 \times 10^{-4} $$

The strain is about $8.18 \times 10^{-4}$. It has no unit.

**4. The extension of the cable.** The strain is the extension divided by the
original length.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon &= \frac{\Delta L}{L} \\
\Delta L &= \varepsilon L = 8.175 \times 10^{-4} \times 20.0 \\
         &= 1.635 \times 10^{-2}\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The cable stretches by about $16.4\,\text{mm}$. That is a little over a
centimetre, so the first estimate of a millimetre was too small and an estimate
of a metre was far too large.

**5. The stiffness of the cable.** The gradient of the force-extension graph is

$$ k = \frac{EA}{L} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{11} \times 1.5 \times 10^{-4}}{20.0} = 1.5 \times 10^{6}\,\text{N}\,\text{m}^{-1} $$

**6. The energy stored in the cable.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= \frac{1}{2}F\Delta L \\
  &= \frac{1}{2} \times 24\,525 \times 1.635 \times 10^{-2} \\
  &= 2.00 \times 10^{2}\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

The cable stores about $200\,\text{J}$ of elastic potential energy.

**7. Is the cable safe for the beam?** The stress is
$1.635 \times 10^{8}\,\text{Pa}$, and the working limit is
$3.0 \times 10^{8}\,\text{Pa}$. The stress is below the limit, so the cable is
safe for the beam under the rule.

**8. The heaviest beam.** The greatest force is the working stress multiplied by
the area.

$$ F_{\text{max}} = 3.0 \times 10^{8} \times 1.5 \times 10^{-4} = 4.5 \times 10^{4}\,\text{N} $$

The greatest mass is

$$ m_{\text{max}} = \frac{4.5 \times 10^{4}}{9.81} = 4.6 \times 10^{3}\,\text{kg} $$

The cable may lift a beam of about 4600 kg.

**9. The judgement.** A second beam of 5000 kg gives a load of
$5000 \times 9.81 = 49\,050\,\text{N}$, so the stress would be

$$ \frac{49\,050}{1.5 \times 10^{-4}} = 3.27 \times 10^{8}\,\text{Pa} $$

That is above the working limit of $3.0 \times 10^{8}\,\text{Pa}$, so the cable
may not lift the 5000 kg beam under the safety rule. A cable of area at least
$1.64 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2$ would be needed. This final judgement is a
reasoned answer. It is not the kind of claim a computer can check, so compare
your reasoning with the evidence above.

## What you can now do

- explain why engineers use stress and strain rather than force and extension;
- calculate a tensile stress, a tensile strain and a Young modulus;
- use the Young modulus to find an extension;
- find the spring constant from the gradient of a force-extension graph;
- describe the limit of proportionality, the elastic limit and plastic
  deformation;
- find the energy stored from the area under a force-extension graph;
- use $E = \tfrac{1}{2}F\Delta L = \tfrac{1}{2}k(\Delta L)^2$ for a straight-line
  graph;
- check whether a cable is safe for a given load, using a working stress.
