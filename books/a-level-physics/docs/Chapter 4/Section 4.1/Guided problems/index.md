# 4.1 Stress, strain and the Young modulus — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check. Write the unit at every step.

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write $3.0\,\text{mm}^2$ in $\text{m}^2$.
2. Write $2.4 \times 10^{-4}$ as an ordinary decimal.
3. Find the area of a circle of diameter $0.80\,\text{mm}$, in $\text{m}^2$, to
   two significant figures.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $3.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$. The millimetre is squared, so the factor is
   $10^{-6}$.
2. $0.000\,24$.
3. The radius is $0.40\,\text{mm} = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{m}$, so
   $A = \pi r^2 = 5.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2$.

If question 1 goes wrong, look again at the prefix table in Section 1.1 and
remember that the power applies to the prefix as well. If question 3 goes
wrong, halve the diameter before you square it, and change the millimetre to a
metre first.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A wire of cross-sectional area $4.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$ carries a
force of $80\,\text{N}$. Find the stress.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Stress is force divided by cross-sectional area. The force is already in
newtons and the area is already in square metres.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $\sigma = \dfrac{F}{A}$ with $F = 80$ and $A = 4.0 \times 10^{-6}$, then
work out the division.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ \sigma = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{80}{4.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 2.0 \times 10^{7}\,\text{Pa} $$

The stress is $2.0 \times 10^{7}\,\text{Pa}$, which is $20\,\text{MPa}$.
</details>

**2.** A wire of original length $2.00\,\text{m}$ extends by $1.2\,\text{mm}$.
Find the strain.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The extension and the original length must have the same unit before you divide
them. The answer is a ratio, so it has no unit.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Change $1.2\,\text{mm}$ to $1.2 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$, then divide by
$2.00\,\text{m}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta L &= 1.2\,\text{mm} = 1.2 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m} \\
\varepsilon &= \frac{\Delta L}{L} = \frac{1.2 \times 10^{-3}}{2.00} = 6.0 \times 10^{-4}
\end{aligned}
$$

The strain is $6.0 \times 10^{-4}$, which is $0.060\%$.
</details>

**3.** A material has a stress of $9.0 \times 10^{7}\,\text{Pa}$ and a strain
of $4.5 \times 10^{-4}$. Find its Young modulus.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The definition of the Young modulus divides the stress by the strain.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $E = \dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$ and substitute the two values.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} = \frac{9.0 \times 10^{7}}{4.5 \times 10^{-4}} = 2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\text{Pa} $$

The Young modulus is $2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\text{Pa}$, which is the value for
steel.
</details>

**4.** A wire has a diameter of $0.50\,\text{mm}$ and carries a force of
$20\,\text{N}$. Find the stress in the wire.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the cross-sectional area first. It is the area of a circle, so you need the
radius, not the diameter.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Halve the diameter to get $0.25\,\text{mm}$, change it to metres, then use
$A = \pi r^2$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= 0.25\,\text{mm} = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{m} \\
A &= \pi r^2 = \pi \times (2.5 \times 10^{-4})^2 = 1.96 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2 \\
\sigma &= \frac{F}{A} = \frac{20}{1.96 \times 10^{-7}} = 1.0 \times 10^{8}\,\text{Pa}
\end{aligned}
$$

The stress is $1.0 \times 10^{8}\,\text{Pa}$, to two significant figures.
</details>

**5.** A copper wire has a Young modulus of $1.2 \times 10^{11}\,\text{Pa}$, a
length of $1.50\,\text{m}$ and a cross-sectional area of
$3.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2$. A force of $36\,\text{N}$ stretches it. Find
the extension.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $\Delta L = \dfrac{FL}{AE}$. The force and the length go on the top, and the
area and the Young modulus go on the bottom.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Substitute $F = 36$, $L = 1.50$, $A = 3.0 \times 10^{-7}$ and
$E = 1.2 \times 10^{11}$ into the rearranged formula.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta L &= \frac{FL}{AE} \\
         &= \frac{36 \times 1.50}{3.0 \times 10^{-7} \times 1.2 \times 10^{11}} \\
         &= \frac{54}{3.6 \times 10^{4}} = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The wire extends by $1.5\,\text{mm}$.
</details>

**6.** A steel wire and a copper wire are the same length and carry the same
force. Their cross-sectional areas are equal. Explain which wire stretches more,
and why. The Young modulus of steel is $2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\text{Pa}$ and of
copper is $1.2 \times 10^{11}\,\text{Pa}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

When the length, the area and the force are fixed, the extension is inversely
proportional to the Young modulus. The smaller value of $E$ gives the larger
extension.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write the ratio of the two extensions, then put the larger value of $E$ on the
bottom of the fraction.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The two wires carry the same stress, because the force and the area are equal.
The strain is the stress divided by the Young modulus, so the smaller Young
modulus gives the larger strain. Copper has the smaller value,
$1.2 \times 10^{11}\,\text{Pa}$, so the copper wire stretches more. The ratio is

$$ \frac{\Delta L_{\text{copper}}}{\Delta L_{\text{steel}}} = \frac{E_{\text{steel}}}{E_{\text{copper}}} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{11}}{1.2 \times 10^{11}} = 1.7 $$

The copper wire extends 1.7 times as far as the steel wire.
</details>
