# 1.2 Estimation and the limitations of measurement — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check.

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write $2.5\,\text{mm}^2$ in $\text{m}^2$.
2. Write $0.000\,30\,\text{m}$ in standard form.
3. Work out $\dfrac{0.2}{20}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $2.5 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$, because the prefix is squared.
2. $3.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{m}$.
3. $0.010$.

If question 1 goes wrong, go back to the prefix table in Section 1.1 and square
the factor. If question 2 goes wrong, count the places the decimal point moves.
If question 3 goes wrong, remember that dividing by 20 is not the same as
dividing by 2.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A room is 8.0 m long, 5.0 m wide and 3.0 m high. The density of air is
$1.2\,\text{kg}\,\text{m}^{-3}$. Estimate the mass of air in the room to one
significant figure.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the volume of the room first. The mass is density multiplied by volume.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $V = 8.0 \times 5.0 \times 3.0$ and then $m = \rho V$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= 8.0 \times 5.0 \times 3.0 = 120\,\text{m}^3 \\
m &= 1.2 \times 120 = 144\,\text{kg} \\
  &= 1 \times 10^2\,\text{kg} \quad (1\ \text{significant figure})
\end{aligned}
$$

To one significant figure the estimate is $1 \times 10^2\,\text{kg}$, which is
about $10^2\,\text{kg}$.
</details>

**2.** A micrometer reads to the nearest 0.01 mm. State its resolution, the
uncertainty in one reading, and one reason it is a better choice than a metre
rule for the diameter of a wire.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The resolution is the smallest change the instrument can show. The reading
uncertainty is half of that.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The resolution is 0.01 mm, so the reading uncertainty is 0.005 mm. Now compare
that with the 0.5 mm uncertainty of a metre rule.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The resolution is 0.01 mm. The uncertainty in one reading is half the smallest
division, so 0.005 mm. A wire may be about 0.30 mm across, and the metre rule
uncertainty of 0.5 mm is larger than the wire itself. The micrometer reads to a
much smaller division, so the percentage uncertainty in the diameter is much
smaller.
</details>

**3.** Classify each error as systematic or random, and say how to reduce it.
(a) A ruler has a worn end that removes 2 mm from every reading. (b) A student
varies the moment at which a stopwatch is pressed. (c) A thermometer reads
0.3 °C low at every temperature.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Ask whether the error shifts every reading the same way, or scatters them.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

For each one, decide whether repeating the measurement would change the error. If
it would not, the error is systematic.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

(a) Systematic. Every reading is 2 mm too large, so repeating will not help.
Start the measurement from a different mark on the rule, or allow for the 2 mm.
(b) Random. The press time is sometimes early and sometimes late, so repeat the
timing and take the mean.
(c) Systematic. Every reading is 0.3 °C too low, so the thermometer must be
checked against a known fixed point and the readings corrected.
</details>

**4.** A student times 25 oscillations in one measurement. The total time has an
uncertainty of 0.3 s. Find the uncertainty in the period.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The period is the total time divided by the number of oscillations. The
uncertainty is divided in the same way.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Divide 0.3 s by 25 to find the uncertainty in one period $T$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ \text{uncertainty in } T = \frac{0.3\,\text{s}}{25} = 0.012\,\text{s} $$

Timing more oscillations reduces the uncertainty in the period, because the same
uncertainty in the total time is shared over more oscillations.
</details>

**5.** A book has 500 sheets and is 5.0 cm thick. Estimate the thickness of one
sheet in millimetres.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the total thickness by the number of sheets. Keep the unit with the
number.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out $\dfrac{5.0\,\text{cm}}{500}$, then change centimetres to millimetres.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ \text{thickness} = \frac{5.0\,\text{cm}}{500} = 0.010\,\text{cm} = 0.10\,\text{mm} $$

One sheet is 0.10 mm thick. Measuring 500 sheets divides the reading uncertainty
of the ruler by 500.
</details>

**6.** You must measure the thickness of one page of this book. Choose between
measuring one page directly with a metre rule and measuring a block of 500 pages.
State which method you would use, and give two reasons.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the thickness of one page with the smallest division of a metre rule.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Decide whether a single page can produce a reading larger than the smallest
division of the rule.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Measure the block of 500 pages. One page is about 0.1 mm thick, which is much
smaller than the 1 mm smallest division of a metre rule, so a direct reading
would be all uncertainty. Measuring the block gives a reading many times larger
than the smallest division, and dividing by 500 divides the reading uncertainty
by 500. The method also averages out the small variations between pages.
</details>
