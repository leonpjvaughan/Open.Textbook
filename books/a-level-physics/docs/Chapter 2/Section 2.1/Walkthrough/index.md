# 2.1 Scalars and vectors — Learn

**Prerequisites:** SI units and prefixes from Section 1.1; standard form; the
idea that a quantity is a number with a unit; the compass directions north,
south, east and west.

**By the end of this section you can:** tell a scalar quantity from a vector
quantity; write a vector as a magnitude and a direction; use positive and
negative signs for direction along a line; add vectors that act along one line;
and multiply a vector by a scalar.

## 1. Two kinds of quantity

A **scalar** has a magnitude only. Mass, time, temperature and energy are
scalars. A mass of 5 kg and a temperature of 20 °C need no direction to make
sense.

A **vector** has a magnitude and a direction. Displacement, velocity,
acceleration and force are vectors. A force of 20 N is not a complete statement.
The force of 20 N pushing north is a complete statement.

| Scalar | Vector |
|---|---|
| distance | displacement |
| speed | velocity |
| mass | weight (a force) |
| energy | acceleration |
| time | force |
| temperature | momentum |
| power | impulse |

The magnitude of a vector is its size, written without a direction. The
magnitude of a velocity is a speed. The magnitude of a displacement is a
distance.

## 2. A direction is not enough on its own

Some scalar quantities have a direction in everyday language. Electric current
flows along a wire, and a temperature can be negative. Neither is a vector.

The test is how the quantities combine. Two vectors add by the **triangle rule**.
Place the tail of the second vector at the tip of the first. Draw the resultant
from the tail of the first to the tip of the second. A quantity is a vector only
if it combines in that way.

Two currents do not combine by the triangle rule. A current of 3 A and a current
of 4 A meeting at a junction give a current of 7 A, not 5 A. So current is a
scalar, even though it flows in a direction.

> **Watch out.** A sign is not the same as a direction. A temperature of
> $-5\,^\circ\text{C}$ is not a vector, and neither is a bank balance of
> $-\pounds 20$. A vector needs a direction in space, and it must add by the
> triangle rule. Check each quantity by asking: if I put two of these end to end,
> does the triangle rule give the combined effect?

## 4. Writing a vector

A vector is written as a magnitude and a direction. The direction can be given in
several ways.

- A **bearing**, measured clockwise from north. A displacement of 250 m on a
  bearing of 060° points 60° clockwise from north, which is roughly north-east.
- An angle from a stated direction. A force of 50 N at 30° above the horizontal.
- A pair of **components**. A displacement of 3.0 m east and 4.0 m north can be
  written as the vector $(3.0, 4.0)\,\text{m}$. You will use components in
  Section 2.2.

In printed work a vector is often shown in bold, such as $\mathbf{v}$, or with an
arrow, such as $\vec{v}$. Its magnitude is written $v$ or $|\mathbf{v}|$. In
handwriting an underline, $\underline{v}$, is common.

## 5. Vectors along one line

When two vectors act along the same line, choose one direction as positive. A
vector in the opposite direction then has a negative sign. Adding the signed
numbers gives the resultant, and its sign gives the direction.

**Worked example 1.** A walker walks 3.0 km east, then 1.5 km west. Find the
resultant displacement.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{take east as positive} \\
s &= (+3.0) + (-1.5) \\
  &= +1.5\,\text{km}
\end{aligned}
$$

The resultant is 1.5 km east. The distance travelled is 4.5 km, but the
displacement is only 1.5 km.

**Worked example 2.** A cyclist travels 500 m east in 20 s. Find the average
velocity.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= \frac{d}{t} \\
  &= \frac{500}{20} \\
  &= 25\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The velocity is $25\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ east. The speed is
$25\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$, with no direction stated.

**Worked example 3.** A boat has a speed through the water of 6.0 m s⁻¹ east.
The current is 2.0 m s⁻¹ east. Find the velocity over the ground when the boat
travels with the current and against it.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{with the current:}\quad v &= 6.0 + 2.0 = 8.0\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}\ \text{east} \\
\text{against the current:}\quad v &= 6.0 - 2.0 = 4.0\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}\ \text{west}
\end{aligned}
$$

The two velocities are along the same line, so the signed numbers add. With the
current the two speeds add, and against it they subtract.

## 6. Multiplying a vector by a scalar

Multiplying a vector by a positive scalar changes its magnitude and leaves its
direction unchanged. Multiplying by a negative scalar reverses the direction.

**Worked example 4.** A force of 20 N acts east. Find the force 3 times larger
in the same direction.

$$ 3 \times 20\,\text{N} = 60\,\text{N}\ \text{east} $$

Multiplying by $-1$ would give 20 N west. Doubling a displacement doubles its
length but keeps it pointing the same way.

## 7. Speed and velocity round a bend

A car on a roundabout can keep a steady speed while its velocity changes. The
speed is the magnitude of the velocity. The direction of the velocity changes as
the car turns, so the velocity vector changes even when its length does not.

On a straight runway the direction of motion stays the same, so one velocity
describes the whole run. On a curved road the direction keeps changing, so the
velocity keeps changing.
Distance and speed describe how much ground is covered. Displacement and
velocity describe the change in position, and they carry a direction.

## You should now be able to

- tell a scalar from a vector, and give an example of each;
- explain why a stated direction alone does not make a quantity a vector;
- write a vector as a magnitude and a direction, including as a bearing;
- add and subtract vectors that act along one line;
- multiply a vector by a scalar, and state the effect on the magnitude and the
  direction.
