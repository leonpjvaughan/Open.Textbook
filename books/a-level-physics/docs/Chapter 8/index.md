# Chapter 8: Quantum and nuclear physics

## The chapter problem: the hospital tracer

A hospital prepares a radioactive tracer for a scan. The physics team checks the
tracer and the equipment before the patient arrives.

These are the facts to use all the way through the chapter.

- The tracer contains **fluorine-18**. Its half-life is **110 minutes**, and it
  decays by emitting a **positron**.
- At the start of the morning the tracer has an activity of
  **8.0 × 10⁸ Bq**.
- The scan needs an activity of at least **2.0 × 10⁸ Bq** when it starts.
- Each positron meets an electron in the body. The two particles **annihilate**,
  and their mass becomes two **gamma-ray photons**. Each photon has an energy of
  **511 keV**.
- The gamma detector has a **photocathode** with a work function of **2.00 eV**.
  The team tests it with ultraviolet light of wavelength **250 nm**.
- The team also calibrates a teaching **electron gun** in the training
  laboratory. It accelerates electrons through a potential difference of
  **150 V**.
- The Planck constant is $6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$. The speed
  of light is $3.00 \times 10^{8}\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. The charge of the
  electron is $1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$. The rest energy of an electron
  is **0.511 MeV**.

By the end of the chapter you will work out the energy and frequency of the
gamma photons and check that the ultraviolet light releases electrons from the
detector. You will find the de Broglie wavelength of the electrons in the gun.
You will follow the activity of the tracer through the morning, and find the
energy released when a positron annihilates with an electron. At the end you will
decide whether the tracer is still strong enough to use, and explain your
reasoning.

> **First contact: no working yet.** Predict. The activity of the tracer halves
> every 110 minutes. The morning lasts 330 minutes. Will the activity have
> fallen to about half, about an eighth, or about a thousandth of its starting
> value? Write down your answer and one sentence of reasoning.

<summary>If you are not sure where to start</summary>

Each interval of 110 minutes halves the activity. Count how many intervals fit
into 330 minutes. Then halve the starting activity that many times.
</details>

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- describe light as a stream of photons, and use $E = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$;
- explain the photoelectric effect, and define the work function and the
  threshold frequency;
- use the photoelectric equation $hf = \phi + E_{k(\text{max})}$;
- explain why the maximum kinetic energy depends on frequency, not intensity;
- find the stopping potential from the maximum kinetic energy;
- describe the de Broglie hypothesis, and use $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p}$;
- explain how electron diffraction shows that particles have wave properties;
- explain why the wave nature of a large object is not observed;
- define activity and half-life, and use $A = \lambda N$ and
  $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$;
- find the decay constant from the half-life, and read a decay curve;
- write a balanced nuclear equation for alpha, beta and gamma decay;
- explain the random nature of radioactive decay;
- define mass defect and binding energy, and use $E = mc^2$;
- calculate the energy released in a nuclear reaction or an annihilation;
- explain why fission and fusion both release energy.

## Where it is used

A hospital uses a positron emitter for a scan, and works out the activity at the
time of the scan. A particle physicist identifies a nucleus from the energy of
the gamma rays it emits. An astronomer reads the composition of a star from its
spectrum. A power engineer follows the binding energy of a nucleus to judge
whether a reaction releases energy. An electron microscope uses the de Broglie
wavelength of electrons to see details far smaller than a light microscope can
show. The same ideas explain why a smoke alarm, a radiotherapy beam and the Sun
all work.
