# 8.1 Photons and the photoelectric effect — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check. Change nanometres to metres and electronvolts to joules before
you substitute.

## Warm-up: remember

1. A wave has a frequency of $6.0 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$. Find its
   wavelength. The speed of light is $3.00 \times 10^{8}\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.
2. Write an energy of $2.0\,\text{eV}$ in joules.
3. An electron is accelerated through a potential difference of $50\,\text{V}$.
   Find the kinetic energy it gains, in joules.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}}{6.0 \times 10^{14}} = 5.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$.
2. $E = 2.0 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.2 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$.
3. $E_k = eV = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 50 = 8.0 \times 10^{-18}\,\text{J}$.

If question 1 goes wrong, revisit the wave equation in Section 6.1. If
question 2 goes wrong, remember that one electronvolt is
$1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$. If question 3 goes wrong, the energy gained is
the charge multiplied by the potential difference.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A photon has a frequency of $5.0 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$. Find its
energy.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The energy of a photon is the Planck constant multiplied by the frequency.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $E = hf$ with $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ and
$f = 5.0 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= hf \\
  &= 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 5.0 \times 10^{14} \\
  &= 3.3 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

The photon energy is $3.3 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$, to two significant
figures.
</details>

**2.** Find the energy of a photon of wavelength 400 nm, in joules and in
electronvolts.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$. Change the wavelength from nanometres to metres
first.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $\lambda = 4.00 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}$, then divide $hc$ by it. Divide
the answer in joules by $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ to get electronvolts.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= \frac{hc}{\lambda}
   = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{4.00 \times 10^{-7}} \\
  &= 4.97 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J} \\
  &= \frac{4.97 \times 10^{-19}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.11\,\text{eV}
\end{aligned}
$$

The photon energy is $4.97 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}$, or 3.11 eV.
</details>

**3.** A metal has a work function of 2.30 eV. Light of wavelength 300 nm falls
on it. Find the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons, in eV.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the photoelectric equation. Keep both energies in electronvolts.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Find the photon energy in eV with $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, then subtract the work
function.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= \frac{hc}{\lambda}
   = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{3.00 \times 10^{-7}} \\
  &= 6.63 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J} = 4.14\,\text{eV}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$ E_{k(\text{max})} = hf - \phi = 4.14 - 2.30 = 1.84\,\text{eV} $$

The most energetic electrons leave with 1.84 eV of kinetic energy.
</details>

**4.** A metal has a work function of 2.00 eV. Find its threshold wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

At the threshold the photon energy equals the work function.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Change the work function to joules, then use $\lambda_0 = \dfrac{hc}{\phi}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi &= 2.00 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.20 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J} \\
\lambda_0 &= \frac{hc}{\phi}
   = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{3.20 \times 10^{-19}} \\
  &= 6.22 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The threshold wavelength is 622 nm.
</details>

**5.** In an experiment, the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons is
2.5 eV. Find the stopping potential.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The stopping potential is the potential difference that brings the fastest
electron to rest.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Use $eV_s = E_{k(\text{max})}$, and remember that an energy in electronvolts and
a potential difference in volts share the same number.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ eV_s = E_{k(\text{max})} = 2.5\,\text{eV} \qquad V_s = 2.5\,\text{V} $$

The stopping potential is 2.5 V.
</details>

**6.** A hydrogen atom emits a photon when an electron falls from a level at
$-1.51\,\text{eV}$ to a level at $-3.40\,\text{eV}$. Find the energy of the
photon in electronvolts, and its wavelength.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The photon energy is the difference between the two levels. Both levels are
negative, so subtract carefully.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Find the gap in eV, change it to joules, then use $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89\,\text{eV} \\
  &= 1.89 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.02 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda &= \frac{hc}{E}
   = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{3.02 \times 10^{-19}} \\
  &= 6.58 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The photon has an energy of 1.89 eV and a wavelength of 658 nm, which is red
light.
</details>

**7.** Explain why increasing the intensity of light below the threshold
frequency releases no photoelectrons, however bright the light is.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about what one photon can do to one electron.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Compare the energy of one photon with the work function. Then say what more
photons of the same energy can and cannot do.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A photon is absorbed by one electron. If the photon energy $hf$ is less than the
work function $\phi$, the electron cannot escape, even though it gains energy
for a moment. Increasing the intensity adds more photons, but each photon still
carries too little energy. The electron cannot collect energy from two photons
at once. No electrons are released.

Below the threshold, the photons are too weak on their own. The work function is
a one-photon barrier, not a total-energy barrier.
</details>
