# 8.4 Nuclear energy and E = mc² — Learn

**Prerequisites:** work, energy and the conservation of energy from Section 3.3;
the nucleus, nucleon number and isotopes from Section 8.3; the electronvolt and
the atomic mass unit from Section 1.1; photon energy from Section 8.1;
powers and scientific notation.

**By the end of this section you can:** state the mass-energy relation
$E = mc^2$; define mass defect and binding energy; use the atomic mass unit and
its energy equivalent; calculate the energy released in a nuclear reaction or an
annihilation; and explain why fission and fusion release energy.

## 1. Mass and energy

In 1905 Einstein showed that mass and energy are two forms of the same thing:

$$ E = mc^2 $$

The relation says that a mass $m$ has an **energy equivalent** $mc^2$, and that
an energy $E$ has a mass equivalent $\dfrac{E}{c^2}$. The total mass-energy of an
isolated system is conserved.

The energies in nuclear physics are large. One **atomic mass unit**,
$1\,\text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$, is equivalent to

$$ 1\,\text{u} = 931.5\,\text{MeV} $$

This number is on the data sheet. It lets you work in atomic mass units and
megaelectronvolts, which keeps the numbers manageable.

## 2. Mass defect and binding energy

If you could pull a nucleus apart into its separate protons and neutrons, you
would find a surprise. The mass of the separate nucleons is **larger** than the
mass of the nucleus they formed. The difference is the **mass defect**,
$\Delta m$.

The energy released when the nucleus formed is the **binding energy**. It is the
same as the energy needed to pull the nucleus apart again:

$$ E = \Delta m\,c^2 $$

A larger binding energy means a more tightly bound nucleus. To compare
different nuclei, divide by the number of nucleons to get the **binding energy
per nucleon**.

**Worked example 1.** A helium-4 nucleus, $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$, has a mass of
4.0015 u. A proton has a mass of 1.00728 u and a neutron a mass of 1.00867 u.
Find the mass defect and the binding energy of the helium-4 nucleus.

Find the total mass of the separate nucleons. The nucleus has 2 protons and
2 neutrons.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_{\text{separate}} &= 2(1.00728) + 2(1.00867) \\
                    &= 2.01456 + 2.01734 = 4.03190\,\text{u}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\Delta m = m_{\text{separate}} - m_{\text{nucleus}} = 4.03190 - 4.00150
         = 0.03040\,\text{u}
$$

Now use the energy equivalent of the atomic mass unit.

$$ E = 0.03040 \times 931.5 = 28.3\,\text{MeV} $$

The helium-4 nucleus has a mass defect of 0.03040 u and a binding energy of
28.3 MeV. Its binding energy per nucleon is $\dfrac{28.3}{4} = 7.08\,\text{MeV}$
per nucleon.

**Worked example 2.** A nuclear reaction releases 200 MeV. Find the mass defect
in kilograms.

Rearrange $E = \Delta m\,c^2$ and change the energy to joules.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta m &= \frac{E}{c^2}
         = \frac{200 \times 10^{6} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}}{(3.00 \times 10^{8})^2} \\
         &= \frac{3.20 \times 10^{-11}}{9.00 \times 10^{16}}
         = 3.56 \times 10^{-28}\,\text{kg}
\end{aligned}
$$

The mass defect is $3.56 \times 10^{-28}\,\text{kg}$. In atomic mass units it is
$\dfrac{200}{931.5} = 0.215\,\text{u}$.

## 3. The binding energy curve

The binding energy per nucleon is different for every nuclide. A graph of it
against nucleon number has a clear shape.

- It rises steeply for the lightest nuclei, such as hydrogen and helium.
- It reaches a broad maximum near iron-56, at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon.
- It falls slowly for the heaviest nuclei, such as uranium.

The maximum is the most stable region. A nucleus near the peak needs the most
energy per nucleon to pull it apart, so it is the hardest to break.

This curve explains where nuclear energy comes from. Both **fission** and
**fusion** move a nucleus towards the peak, and a more tightly bound nucleus has
less mass. The mass that disappears is released as energy.

- **Fission** splits a very heavy nucleus into two lighter ones. The products
  have a higher binding energy per nucleon, so energy is released.
- **Fusion** joins two light nuclei into a heavier one. The product has a higher
  binding energy per nucleon, so energy is released.

Fusion powers the Sun. Fission powers a nuclear reactor. Both are applications
of $E = mc^2$.

## 4. Annihilation

When a particle meets its **antiparticle**, the two can **annihilate**. Their
mass disappears and becomes photon energy. The electron's antiparticle is the
**positron**, which has the same mass as an electron and a positive charge.

For a positron and an electron, the total rest energy is
$2 \times 0.511 = 1.022\,\text{MeV}$. Two photons are produced, each of energy
0.511 MeV. Two photons are needed because momentum must be conserved: the
collision has almost no momentum, so the two photons must travel in opposite
directions and share the energy equally.

**Worked example 3.** In the hospital tracer, a positron annihilates with an
electron. Each particle has a rest energy of 0.511 MeV. Find the total energy
released, in joules, and the energy of each photon.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\text{total}} &= 2 \times 0.511 = 1.022\,\text{MeV} \\
                 &= 1.022 \times 10^{6} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \\
                 &= 1.64 \times 10^{-13}\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

Two photons are produced, so each carries half of the energy:
$0.511\,\text{MeV} = 8.18 \times 10^{-14}\,\text{J}$. The gamma-ray photons from
the tracer have the energy found in Section 8.1, because they come from this
annihilation.

> **Watch out.** The mass defect is not a mass that has been lost. Mass and
> energy are two forms of the same quantity, and the total is conserved. The
> mass that seems to disappear has reappeared as kinetic energy of the products
> or as photon energy. A second trap is to mix the two routes: use the energy
> equivalent of 1 u (931.5 MeV) when the mass is in atomic mass units, and use
> $E = mc^2$ when the mass is in kilograms. Mixing them gives an answer that is
> wrong by a large factor.

1. Is the binding energy per nucleon larger for helium-4 or for iron-56?
2. A uranium nucleus is near the right-hand end of the curve. Is its binding
   energy per nucleon higher or lower than iron-56?
3. Why does moving towards the peak of the curve release energy?

and then falls.

sentences. Use the idea that a higher binding energy per nucleon means a more
tightly bound nucleus with less mass.

## Progress check

Return to the hospital tracer. The positron from a fluorine-18 decay annihilates
with an electron.

1. Find the total energy released in the annihilation, in joules.
2. Find the energy of each of the two gamma photons, in joules.
3. Explain why two photons are produced rather than one.
4. The team checks the energy of the gamma photons. Each has an energy of
   511 keV. Show that this is consistent with the rest energy of an electron.

If question 1 goes wrong, use $E = mc^2$ with the combined rest mass, or add the
two rest energies. If question 2 goes wrong, the energy is shared equally. If
question 3 goes wrong, think about momentum. If question 4 goes wrong, remember
that $1\,\text{MeV} = 10^{3}\,\text{keV}$.
