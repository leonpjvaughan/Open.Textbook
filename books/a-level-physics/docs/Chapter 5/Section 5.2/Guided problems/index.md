# 5.2 Resistance and resistivity — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check. Write the unit at every step.

## Warm-up: remember

1. A component carries a current of $0.20\,\text{A}$ with a potential difference
   of $4.0\,\text{V}$ across it. Find the power.
2. Write $2.5\,\text{mm}^2$ in $\text{m}^2$.
3. Rearrange $R = \dfrac{\rho L}{A}$ to make $A$ the subject.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $P = VI = 4.0 \times 0.20 = 0.80\,\text{W}$.
2. $2.5 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$. The millimetre is squared, so the factor is
   $10^{-6}$.
3. $A = \dfrac{\rho L}{R}$.

If question 1 goes wrong, look again at Section 5.1 and the relation
$P = VI$. If question 2 goes wrong, remember that the power applies to the
prefix as well as to the unit. If question 3 goes wrong, multiply both sides by
$A$ and then divide by $R$.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A resistor has a potential difference of $9.0\,\text{V}$ across it and a
current of $0.30\,\text{A}$ through it. Find its resistance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Resistance is the potential difference divided by the current.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$ with $V = 9.0$ and $I = 0.30$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{9.0}{0.30} = 30\,\Omega $$

The resistance is $30\,\Omega$.
</details>

**2.** A wire of resistivity $1.7 \times 10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\text{m}$ has a length
of $3.0\,\text{m}$ and a cross-sectional area of $5.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2$.
Find its resistance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the resistivity equation. The length goes on the top and the area goes on the
bottom.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Substitute $\rho = 1.7 \times 10^{-8}$, $L = 3.0$ and
$A = 5.0 \times 10^{-7}$ into $R = \dfrac{\rho L}{A}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
R &= \frac{\rho L}{A} \\
  &= \frac{1.7 \times 10^{-8} \times 3.0}{5.0 \times 10^{-7}} \\
  &= 1.0 \times 10^{-1}\,\Omega
\end{aligned}
$$

The resistance is about $0.10\,\Omega$.
</details>

**3.** A wire of length $2.0\,\text{m}$ and cross-sectional area
$4.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2$ has a resistance of $6.0\,\Omega$. Find the
resistivity of the material.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Rearrange the resistivity equation to make $\rho$ the subject.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Start from $\rho = \dfrac{RA}{L}$, then substitute the three values.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho &= \frac{RA}{L} \\
     &= \frac{6.0 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-7}}{2.0} \\
     &= 1.2 \times 10^{-6}\,\Omega\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The resistivity is $1.2 \times 10^{-6}\,\Omega\,\text{m}$.
</details>

**4.** A filament lamp has a current of $0.25\,\text{A}$ when the pd across it is
$3.0\,\text{V}$, and a current of $0.40\,\text{A}$ when the pd is $6.0\,\text{V}$.
Find the resistance at each pd, and explain the change.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find each resistance with $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$, then compare them.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out $\dfrac{3.0}{0.25}$ and $\dfrac{6.0}{0.40}$, and note which is larger.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
R_1 = \frac{3.0}{0.25} = 12\,\Omega \qquad
R_2 = \frac{6.0}{0.40} = 15\,\Omega
$$

At the higher pd the filament is hotter. The ions in the metal lattice vibrate
more, so they scatter the drifting electrons more often. The current is smaller
than it would be at a constant resistance, so the resistance has risen from
$12\,\Omega$ to $15\,\Omega$.

</details>

**5.** A copper wire has a diameter of $0.50\,\text{mm}$ and a length of
$2.0\,\text{m}$. The resistivity of copper is
$1.7 \times 10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\text{m}$. Find its resistance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the cross-sectional area first. It is the area of a circle, so you need the
radius, not the diameter.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Halve the diameter to get $0.25\,\text{mm}$, change it to metres, and use
$A = \pi r^2$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= 0.25\,\text{mm} = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{m} \\
A &= \pi r^2 = \pi \times (2.5 \times 10^{-4})^2 = 1.96 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{m}^2 \\
R &= \frac{\rho L}{A} = \frac{1.7 \times 10^{-8} \times 2.0}{1.96 \times 10^{-7}} \\
  &= 0.17\,\Omega
\end{aligned}
$$

The resistance is about $0.17\,\Omega$, to two significant figures.
</details>

**6.** Two wires are made of the same metal. One has twice the length and twice
the diameter of the other. Explain what happens to the resistance of the longer,
thicker wire.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Doubling the diameter makes the area four times larger. The resistance is
proportional to the length and inversely proportional to the area.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Compare the two effects. The length doubles, so $R$ doubles. The area becomes
four times larger, so $R$ becomes one quarter. Find the combined factor.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The resistance is proportional to $\dfrac{L}{A}$. Doubling the length doubles
$R$. Doubling the diameter makes the area four times larger, so it divides $R$ by
four. The combined change is a factor of $\dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$. The
longer, thicker wire has half the resistance of the shorter, thinner wire.
</details>
