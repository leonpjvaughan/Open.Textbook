# 3.4 Momentum — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Newton's laws and $F = ma$ from Section 3.2; velocity and the
sign convention for one-dimensional motion from Section 3.1; kinetic energy from
Section 3.3.

**By the end of this section you can:** define momentum; use the
impulse–momentum relation $F\Delta t = \Delta p$; read a force–time graph; and
apply conservation of momentum to one-dimensional collisions, explosions and
recoil.

## 1. Momentum

The **momentum** of an object is its mass multiplied by its velocity.

$$ p = mv $$

Momentum is a **vector**, so it has the same direction as the velocity. Its unit
is the kilogram metre per second, $\text{kg}\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$, which is
also written as the newton second, $\text{N}\,\text{s}$.

A larger mass or a larger velocity gives more momentum. A heavy lorry moving
slowly can have the same momentum as a small car moving quickly. Stopping the
lorry takes the same total change in momentum as stopping the car.

For motion along a line, choose one direction as positive. An object moving the
other way has negative momentum. Adding the signed momenta of a system gives the
**total momentum**, and its sign gives the direction of the total.

**Worked example 1.** A car of mass 1200 kg travels at
$15\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ due east. Find its momentum.

$$ p = mv = 1200 \times 15 = 1.8 \times 10^4\,\text{kg}\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1} \ \text{east} $$

## 2. Newton's second law and impulse

Newton's second law can be written in terms of momentum. If the mass is
constant,

$$ F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} $$

The resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum. For a force that acts
for a time $\Delta t$, rearranging gives the **impulse**:

$$ F\Delta t = \Delta p $$

Impulse is measured in newton seconds. The impulse on an object equals its
change in momentum. On a **force–time graph**, the area under the graph is the
impulse, and the change in momentum.

This form of the law explains why a collision is safer when it lasts longer. For
a fixed change in momentum, a longer contact time gives a smaller force. Crumple
zones and airbags make a crash last longer, so the force on the passengers is
smaller.

**Worked example 2.** A ball of mass 0.20 kg hits a wall at
$8.0\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ and rebounds at $6.0\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. It
is in contact with the wall for 0.050 s. Find the change in momentum and the
average force on the ball.

Take the direction towards the wall as positive, so $u = +8.0$ and
$v = -6.0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta p &= m(v - u) \\
        &= 0.20(-6.0 - 8.0) \\
        &= -2.8\,\text{kg}\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1} \\
F &= \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{-2.8}{0.050} \\
  &= -56\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

The force is 56 N away from the wall. The negative sign shows that the force on
the ball points in the negative direction, away from the wall.

**Worked example 3.** A car of mass 900 kg is brought to rest from
$20\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ in 4.0 s. Find the average braking force.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta p &= 0 - 900 \times 20 = -18000\,\text{kg}\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1} \\
F &= \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{-18000}{4.0} = -4500\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

The braking force is 4500 N opposite to the motion.

## 3. Conservation of momentum

If no resultant external force acts on a system, its **total momentum is
conserved**.

$$ \sum p_{\text{before}} = \sum p_{\text{after}} $$

This applies to collisions, to explosions and to recoil. The forces between the
objects are internal, and by Newton's third law they are equal and opposite, so
they change the two momenta by equal and opposite amounts. The total is
unchanged.

**Worked example 4.** Trolley A of mass 2.0 kg moves at
$3.0\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ towards trolley B of mass 4.0 kg, which is at
rest. The trolleys collide and stick together. Find their common velocity after
the collision.

Take the initial direction of A as positive. Before the collision,

$$ p_{\text{before}} = 2.0 \times 3.0 + 4.0 \times 0 = 6.0\,\text{kg}\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1} $$

After the collision the total mass is 6.0 kg and the common velocity is $v$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
6.0 &= 6.0v \\
v &= 1.0\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The trolleys move together at $1.0\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ in the original
direction of A.

**Worked example 5.** A rifle of mass 4.0 kg fires a bullet of mass 0.020 kg at
$400\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. Find the recoil velocity of the rifle.

The rifle and bullet are at rest before firing, so the total momentum is zero.

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 &= 0.020 \times 400 + 4.0v \\
v &= -\frac{8.0}{4.0} = -2.0\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The rifle recoils at $2.0\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ in the opposite direction to
the bullet.

## 5. Collisions and kinetic energy

Momentum is conserved in every collision, but kinetic energy is not always
conserved.

- In an **elastic** collision, kinetic energy is conserved as well as momentum.
  A collision between gas molecules is close to elastic.
- In an **inelastic** collision, some kinetic energy becomes thermal energy or
  sound. Momentum is still conserved.
- In a **perfectly inelastic** collision, the objects stick together and move as
  one. This loses the most kinetic energy of any collision with the same
  momenta.

**Worked example 6.** In Worked example 4, check whether the collision is
elastic.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_k(\text{before}) &= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.0 \times 3.0^2 = 9.0\,\text{J} \\
E_k(\text{after}) &= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 6.0 \times 1.0^2 = 3.0\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

Kinetic energy has fallen from 9.0 J to 3.0 J, so the collision is inelastic. The
missing 6.0 J has been transferred to thermal stores and sound.

> **Watch out.** Momentum is a vector, so the direction signs matter. When an
> object bounces back, its velocity reverses, and the change in momentum is the
> final momentum minus the initial momentum. If you add the magnitudes instead of
> subtracting, you find the change as if the object had stopped. Also remember
> that momentum is conserved only when the **resultant external force** is zero;
> friction from the ground or a push from outside breaks the conservation.
