# Chapter 3: Mechanics

## The chapter problem: the roller coaster loop

A theme park has a roller coaster with a single vertical loop. A car is pulled to
the top of a lift hill and released. The track falls away in a steep first drop,
runs along the bottom, and rises into a circular loop. At the end of the ride,
brakes bring the car to rest.

These are the facts to use all the way through the chapter.

- The car and two riders have a total mass of **500 kg**.
- The car is released **from rest** at the top of the first drop.
- The first drop is a straight section of track at **60°** to the horizontal, and
  the car descends **45 m** vertically along it.
- The **loop** is a circle of radius **12 m**.
- During the first drop, friction and air resistance are **negligible**.
- At the end of the ride the brakes bring the car to rest from **29.7 m s⁻¹** in
  **3.0 s**.
- Take the acceleration due to gravity as **9.81 m s⁻²**.

By the end of the chapter you will work out the speed of the car at the bottom of
the first drop and its acceleration down the slope. You will follow the energy
through the ride, find the force that holds the car in the loop, and use momentum
to find the braking force. At the end you will decide how much of the loop's
height is paid for by the drop, and explain why a real loop is not a circle.

> **First contact: no working yet.** Estimate. The car is released from rest
> 45 m above the bottom. At the top of the loop it is 24 m above the bottom, so it
> has given up some height but not all of it. Is its speed at the top of the loop
> closest to 10 m s⁻¹, 20 m s⁻¹ or 30 m s⁻¹? Write down your choice and one
> sentence of reasoning.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>If you are not sure where to start</summary>

The speed at the bottom is about 30 m s⁻¹. The car loses speed as it climbs the
loop, because kinetic energy turns back into gravitational potential energy. The
top of the loop is 24 m above the bottom, so the car has climbed past half of the
first drop's height. Its speed at the top is smaller than its speed at the bottom,
but it is not zero.
</details>

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- use the equations of motion for constant acceleration, and say why each one
  applies;
- read a displacement–time graph and a velocity–time graph, using the gradient
  and the area;
- explain free fall and use $g$ in a calculation;
- state Newton's three laws of motion and apply $F = ma$ to a resultant force;
- draw a free-body diagram and find the resultant force on an object;
- analyse projectile motion as a constant horizontal velocity and a constant
  vertical acceleration;
- calculate work done by a force, including a force not along the line of motion;
- use conservation of energy, and the equations for kinetic and gravitational
  potential energy;
- calculate power from work and time, and from force and velocity;
- use the definition of momentum and the impulse–momentum relation;
- use conservation of momentum in one-dimensional collisions and explosions;
- convert between degrees and radians, and find angular velocity;
- use $v = \omega r$ and the equations for centripetal acceleration and force;
- describe simple harmonic motion, and use $a = -\omega^2 x$ and $x = A\cos\omega t$;
- calculate the period of a mass on a spring and of a simple pendulum.

## Where it is used

Mechanics is the part of physics that describes how and why things move. A
vehicle safety engineer uses momentum and impulse to design a crumple zone. A
sports scientist uses projectiles to improve a throw. A roller-coaster designer
uses energy and circular motion to keep riders safe at every point of the track.
An engineer tuning a suspension bridge uses oscillations to stop the deck
swinging in the wind.

The same ideas return throughout the book. Forces and energy carry into
materials and electricity; circular motion and oscillations carry into waves and
fields.
