# 2.2 Surds and rationalising the denominator — Practise with help

Work each one first, closed book. Before you reveal anything, write down the
square numbers up to $144$; most simplifications need one of them.

## Warm-up: remember

1. Write down the square numbers from $1^2$ to $12^2$.
2. Expand $(x + 3)(x - 3)$.
3. Simplify $\sqrt{4 \times 9}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $1$, $4$, $9$, $16$, $25$, $36$, $49$, $64$, $81$, $100$, $121$, $144$.
2. $(x + 3)(x - 3) = x^2 - 9$, a difference of two squares.
3. $\sqrt{4 \times 9} = \sqrt{36} = 6$, and also $\sqrt{4}\sqrt{9} = 2 \times 3 = 6$.

If question 2 was hard, revisit expanding brackets: the conjugate method in this
section is the same expansion with a surd in place of the letter.
</details>

:::figure warm-up-gate


## Practise with help

**1.** Simplify $\sqrt{48}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Look for the largest square number that divides $48$. The square numbers to test
are $4$, $9$, $16$, $25$, $36$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$48$ is $16 \times 3$, and $16$ is a square. Write
$\sqrt{48} = \sqrt{16 \times 3}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Split the square factor out of the root:

$$
\sqrt{48} = \sqrt{16 \times 3} = \sqrt{16}\,\sqrt{3} = 4\sqrt{3}.
$$

</details>

**2.** Simplify $\sqrt{18} + \sqrt{50}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Simplify each surd on its own first. The two simplified surds should become like
terms.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $\sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \times 2} = 3\sqrt{2}$ and do the same for $\sqrt{50}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Simplify each surd:

$$
\sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \times 2} = 3\sqrt{2}, \qquad \sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \times 2} = 5\sqrt{2}.
$$

Both are multiples of $\sqrt{2}$, so add the coefficients:

$$
3\sqrt{2} + 5\sqrt{2} = 8\sqrt{2}.
$$

</details>

**3.** Expand and simplify $\left(3 + \sqrt{2}\right)\left(1 - \sqrt{2}\right)$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Expand the brackets as usual, then use $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{2} = 2$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Multiply out to get $3 - 3\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{2} - 2$, then collect the like terms.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Expand each pair of terms:

$$
\left(3 + \sqrt{2}\right)\left(1 - \sqrt{2}\right) = 3 - 3\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{2} - \sqrt{2}\,\sqrt{2}.
$$

Use $\sqrt{2}\,\sqrt{2} = 2$:

$$
3 - 3\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{2} - 2 = 1 - 2\sqrt{2}.
$$

</details>

**4.** Write $\dfrac{8}{\sqrt{2}}$ with a rational denominator.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply the top and the bottom by $\sqrt{2}$, so the denominator becomes
$\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2} = 2$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out $\dfrac{8\sqrt{2}}{2}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Multiply the top and the bottom by $\sqrt{2}$:

$$
\frac{8}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2}} = \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{2} = 4\sqrt{2}.
$$

</details>

**5.** Write $\dfrac{2}{5 - \sqrt{3}}$ with a rational denominator.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The conjugate of $5 - \sqrt{3}$ is $5 + \sqrt{3}$. Multiply the top and the
bottom by the conjugate.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The denominator becomes $5^2 - 3$. Expand the numerator as
$2\left(5 + \sqrt{3}\right)$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Multiply the top and the bottom by the conjugate:

$$
\frac{2}{5 - \sqrt{3}} = \frac{2\left(5 + \sqrt{3}\right)}{\left(5 - \sqrt{3}\right)\left(5 + \sqrt{3}\right)}.
$$

The denominator is a difference of two squares:

$$
\left(5 - \sqrt{3}\right)\left(5 + \sqrt{3}\right) = 25 - 3 = 22.
$$

So

$$
\frac{2}{5 - \sqrt{3}} = \frac{2\left(5 + \sqrt{3}\right)}{22} = \frac{5 + \sqrt{3}}{11}.
$$

</details>

**6.** A rectangle has length $\left(4 + \sqrt{5}\right)$ cm and width
$\left(4 - \sqrt{5}\right)$ cm. Show that its area is a whole number, and work
out the area.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The area is the product of the two lengths. The two factors are a conjugate
pair, so the product is a difference of two squares.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write the area as $\left(4 + \sqrt{5}\right)\left(4 - \sqrt{5}\right)$ and apply
$x^2 - y^2$ with $x = 4$ and $y = \sqrt{5}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The area is

$$
\left(4 + \sqrt{5}\right)\left(4 - \sqrt{5}\right) = 4^2 - \left(\sqrt{5}\right)^2 = 16 - 5 = 11.
$$

The area is exactly $11$ cm$^2$, a whole number. The surds cancel because the
lengths form a conjugate pair.
</details>
