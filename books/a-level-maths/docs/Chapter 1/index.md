# Chapter 1: Proof and mathematical argument

## The chapter problem: which claims can we trust?

Three claims are on the table.

- **Claim A.** For every whole number $n$, the value of $n^2 + n$ is even.
- **Claim B.** For every whole number $n$, the value of $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime.
- **Claim C.** There is a largest prime number.

By the end of the chapter you will decide whether each claim is true or false,
and you will prove your decision. A guess is not a proof: each answer needs an
argument that covers every case at once.

> **First contact — no proof yet.** Work out $n^2 + n + 41$ for $n = 1$, $n = 2$
> and $n = 3$. All three values are prime. Would you bet that Claim B is true?
> Write down one sentence saying what would make you doubt it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>If you are not sure where to start</summary>

Substitute each value of $n$ into the expression. For $n = 2$ the value is
$2^2 + 2 + 41 = 4 + 2 + 41 = 47$. Now test Claim A for $n = 3$ and $n = 4$, and
write down the first six prime numbers. Does the list of primes look as if it
stops?
</details>

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- say what a statement, an assumption and a conclusion are;
- tell the difference between "for all" and "there is";
- prove a general statement by **deduction**;
- prove a statement about a finite list of cases by **exhaustion**;
- disprove a statement by finding one **counter example**;
- prove a statement by **contradiction**, including the fact that $\sqrt{2}$ is
  irrational.

## Where it is used

Proof is the reason mathematics works. A calculation answers one question; a
proof answers every version of it at once. Every later chapter in this book
leans on the ideas here: when you show that a formula holds for all $x$, or that
a result cannot happen, you are writing a proof. At A level, argument and proof
carry a quarter of the marks, across all three papers.

## Your turn to connect it

Think of a rule that seemed true in every example you tried, but turned out to
have an exception. It could be a rule of thumb about money, travel, games or
sport. Write two examples: one from outside mathematics, and one from
mathematics.
