# Chapter 11: Statistical sampling and data presentation

## The chapter problem: is the bus company's claim believable?

A bus company says its 07:30 service from the estate to the town centre takes
25 minutes on average. A passenger group does not trust the claim. Over one
week, the group records the time and the distance of 30 journeys on that
service.

Here are the 30 journey times, in minutes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&12,\ 15,\ 16,\ 17,\ 18,\ 18,\ 19,\ 20,\ 20,\ 21,\ 21,\ 22,\ 22,\ 23,\ 23,\\
&23,\ 24,\ 24,\ 25,\ 25,\ 26,\ 27,\ 28,\ 29,\ 30,\ 32,\ 34,\ 36,\ 41,\ 58.
\end{aligned}
$$

The group also records how far each journey went. The distances rise from about
$2$ km to about $9.5$ km as the journey times rise.

The group cannot time every journey for a year. It has a sample of 30, and it
must say what that sample suggests about all journeys on the service. The
company says the mean is 25 minutes. By the end of the chapter you will decide
whether the sample supports that claim. You will also see what one unusual
value does to the picture.

> **First contact — no working needed yet.** Read the 30 times without adding
> them up. Predict whether the mean of the sample is above 25 minutes, below
> 25 minutes, or very close to 25 minutes. Write your prediction down.
>
> Then count how many of the 30 journeys took longer than 30 minutes. Predict
> that number before you count. Keep both predictions for the Review.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>If you are not sure where to start</summary>

Look at the middle of the list, not at the ends. About half the values sit
below 23 and half sit above 23, so the centre is near 23. The value 58 is much
larger than the rest, and a value like that pulls the mean upwards. A quick
estimate is useful here: it does not have to be exact.

For the count, look at the last eight numbers. Only a few of them are larger
than 30.
</details>

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- explain the difference between a population and a sample, and why a sample is
  often the only practical choice;
- define a sampling frame and use simple random, systematic, stratified and
  opportunity sampling;
- choose or criticise a sampling technique for a real problem, and explain why
  two samples can lead to different conclusions;
- read and draw diagrams for single-variable data, and explain why the **area**
  of a histogram bar represents frequency;
- work out a frequency density and use it to find a frequency or a probability;
- read a scatter diagram, describe the correlation and interpret a regression
  line, including why correlation does not imply causation;
- calculate the mean, median, mode, range and interquartile range;
- calculate the standard deviation, including from summary statistics, and
  compare distributions using it;
- recognise an outlier, decide whether to keep, correct or remove it, and clean
  a data set;
- criticise a statistical diagram or a published claim;
- work with a large data set, using a spreadsheet or a calculator.

## Where it is used

News reports quote averages, surveys and pass rates every day, and each number
rests on a sample. A hospital measures waiting times to see whether a target is
met. A factory tests part of a batch, because testing all of it would destroy
it. A market researcher asks a few hundred people what a whole town thinks.
Sport, weather forecasting, medicine and government all turn a sample into a
claim about a much larger group. Knowing how the sample was chosen, and how
much it can vary, is what separates a careful claim from a careless one.
