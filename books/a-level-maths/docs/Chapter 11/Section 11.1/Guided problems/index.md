# 11.1 Population, sample and informal inference — Practise with help

Work each question closed book first. Write down whether your answer describes
the sample or the population.

## Warm-up: remember

1. Find the mean of $4, 7, 9, 12, 13$.
2. Write $18$ out of $60$ as a fraction in its simplest form.
3. Work out $15\%$ of $240$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\dfrac{4 + 7 + 9 + 12 + 13}{5} = \dfrac{45}{5} = 9$.
2. $\dfrac{18}{60} = \dfrac{3}{10}$.
3. $0.15 \times 240 = 36$.

If question 2 was hard, revise simplifying fractions before you start.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A council wants to know the mean recycling weight of households in a
town. It weighs the recycling from 250 households. Name the population and the
sample.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The population is the whole group the council wants to describe. The sample is
the 250 households it actually weighed.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write one sentence for each. Start with "The population is every household
in…" and "The sample is the 250 households that…".
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The population is every household in the town. The sample is the 250 households
whose recycling was weighed. The mean weight of the sample is an estimate of the
mean weight of the population.
</details>

**2.** A sample of 50 batteries has a mean life of 12 hours. Estimate the total
life, in hours, of 800 batteries of the same type.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The estimate of a total is the sample mean multiplied by the population size.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Multiply $12$ by $800$. The sample mean is in hours, so the total is in
hours.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\text{estimated total} = 12 \times 800 = 9600 \text{ hours}.
$$

This is an estimate. The 800 batteries were not all tested.
</details>

**3.** In a sample of 150 light bulbs, 18 are faulty. Estimate the number of
faulty bulbs in a batch of 2000.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

First find the sample proportion. It is the number faulty divided by the sample
size.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The sample proportion is $\dfrac{18}{150} = 0.12$. Multiply it by $2000$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\text{estimated number} = \frac{18}{150} \times 2000 = 0.12 \times 2000 = 240.
$$

About 240 bulbs in the batch are faulty.
</details>

**4.** In a sample of 500 people, 85 support a new park. Estimate the percentage
of the population who support the park.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A percentage is a proportion multiplied by 100.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out $\dfrac{85}{500}$, then multiply by $100$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\frac{85}{500} = 0.17,
\qquad
0.17 \times 100 = 17\%.
$$

About $17\%$ of the population support the park. This is a conclusion about the
population, drawn from one sample.
</details>

**5.** Two samples of size 40 give means of $3.1$ and $3.5$. Estimate the
population mean and state one reason why the two samples disagree.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Both samples are the same size, so their means carry the same weight.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Find the midpoint of $3.1$ and $3.5$: add them and divide by 2.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\text{estimate} = \frac{3.1 + 3.5}{2} = 3.3.
$$

The samples disagree because of sampling variability: each sample contains
different members of the population.
</details>

**6.** A sample of 20 students gives a mean screen time of 4 hours. Would you
expect a sample of 200 students to give exactly the same mean? Explain your
answer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about what happens when more students are included. The sample still does
not contain everyone.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Compare the two sample sizes, then say whether a larger sample removes
sampling variability completely.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

No. A larger sample usually gives a mean closer to the population mean, and its
sample-to-sample variation is usually smaller. It does not give the population
mean exactly, because it is still only part of the population. The estimate from
the sample of 200 is likely to be more reliable than the estimate from the
sample of 20.
</details>
