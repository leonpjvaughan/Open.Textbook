# Chapter 5: Trigonometry

## The chapter problem: the beacon and the rising tide

A coastguard station $S$ and a lighthouse $L$ stand $10$ km apart on a straight
coast. A distress beacon $B$ is at sea. From $S$, the angle between the line to
$L$ and the line to $B$ is $30^\circ$. From $L$, the angle between the line to
$S$ and the line to $B$ is $105^\circ$. A rescue boat waits at $S$, and the crew
must know how far the beacon is from each station.

The harbour beside $S$ is a sector of a circle. Its two straight walls are
$4$ km long and meet at $S$ at an angle of $0.9$ radians. The harbourmaster
needs the length of the curved sea wall and the area of water the harbour holds.

The sea level at the harbour rises and falls with the tide. After $t$ hours the
height $h$ metres of the water above mean sea level is modelled by

$$
h = 12\cos\theta + 5\sin\theta,
$$

where $\theta$ is measured in radians and turns through a full circle once every
$12$ hours. The harbour gate closes when the water reaches $10$ m. The crew want
to know the greatest height and the times when the gate closes.

By the end of the chapter you will answer all of this exactly. You will find both
distances from the survey, and the area of the triangle. You will find the length
and the area of the harbour sector. You will rewrite the tide model as one wave,
find its greatest height, and solve for the times when $h = 10$.

> **First contact — no working needed yet.** The beacon is closer to $L$ than
> to $S$, and the largest angle is at $L$. Look at the triangle $SLB$ on rough
> paper. Which side is longest, and which is shortest? Estimate the distance
> $SB$. Then read the tide model: guess its greatest value before you do any
> algebra. Write both estimates down.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>If you are not sure where to start</summary>

The side opposite the largest angle is always the longest. The angle at $L$ is
$105^\circ$, so the side opposite it, which is $SB$, is the longest side. The
angle at $B$ is $180^\circ - 30^\circ - 105^\circ = 45^\circ$. The side opposite
$30^\circ$ is $LB$, so $LB$ is the shortest.

For the tide, the two numbers $12$ and $5$ add to at most $12 + 5 = 17$ only
when both are at their peak, and they do not peak together. A reasonable guess is
between $12$ and $13$. Section 5.9 turns the guess into an exact value.
</details>

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- use the sine rule and the cosine rule to find sides and angles in any
  triangle;
- find the area of a triangle from two sides and the included angle;
- measure an angle in radians, and convert between radians and degrees;
- find the length of an arc and the area of a sector;
- sketch and read the graphs of sine, cosine and tangent, and describe their
  periodicity;
- state and use the exact values of sine, cosine and tangent at the special
  angles;
- use small-angle approximations for sine, cosine and tangent;
- work with secant, cosecant and cotangent, and with the inverse trigonometric
  functions;
- use the trigonometric identities, including the addition and double-angle
  formulae;
- write $a\cos\theta + b\sin\theta$ as a single sine or cosine;
- solve trigonometric equations in a given interval without losing solutions;
- prove a trigonometric identity;
- use trigonometry with vectors, kinematics and forces.

## Where it is used

Trigonometry is the mathematics of anything that repeats, turns or leans. Waves,
tides, alternating current and sound are all sine curves. Forces and velocities
are resolved into components with sine and cosine, which is how mechanics adds
them. Radians are the natural unit for angles in calculus: the derivative of
$\sin x$ is $\cos x$ only when $x$ is in radians. The identities in this chapter
turn complicated expressions into simple ones, and the single-wave form is how
engineers describe any oscillation that is a sum of two.
