# Chapter 15: Quantities, units and kinematics

## The chapter problem: does the free kick go over the crossbar?

A free kick is taken when the ball is on the ground. The ball starts $40$ m from
the goal line, straight in front of the goal. The player strikes the ball so that
it leaves the ground at $20$ m/s at $45^\circ$ to the horizontal.

The crossbar is $2.44$ m above the ground. Model the ball as a particle that
moves under gravity alone. Ignore air resistance. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$.

By the end of the chapter you will work out how high the ball is when it reaches
the goal line. Then you will decide whether it passes under the crossbar or over
it.

> **First contact — no working needed yet.** Sketch the path of the ball. Mark
> the goal line and a line for the crossbar. Predict whether the ball passes
> under the bar or over it. Write one sentence to give your prediction, and one
> reason for it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>If you are not sure where to start</summary>

The ball leaves the ground at $45^\circ$, so it rises and then falls. The kick
has two parts to follow: how far the ball travels across each second, and how
its height changes each second. A ball kicked at $20$ m/s travels about $14$ m
across each second. Work out how many seconds the ball takes to cover $40$ m.
Then decide how high it is at that moment.
</details>

## By the end of this chapter you will be able to

- name the fundamental SI quantities and their units, and build derived units
  from them;
- convert between units, including km/h and m/s;
- use the language of kinematics: position, displacement, distance travelled,
  speed, velocity and acceleration;
- read the gradient of a displacement–time graph and the gradient and area of a
  velocity–time graph;
- choose and use the constant-acceleration formulae;
- use calculus to move between displacement, velocity and acceleration;
- model motion under gravity in a vertical plane;
- resolve a velocity into components and solve a projectile problem with those
  components.

## Where it is used

Mechanics starts with measurement. Before you can describe a moving object, you
need to say what you are measuring and in what unit. Velocity and acceleration
are built from length and time, and force is built from mass as well. The same
language then describes a car on a road, a lift in a shaft, a ball in flight and
a planet in orbit. Projectile motion is the first model that puts a horizontal
and a vertical motion together. The same idea returns in Chapter 16, where forces
cause the acceleration, and in the study of physics.
