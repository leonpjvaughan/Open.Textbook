# 16.2 Newton's second law and F = ma — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Newton's first law and resultant forces (Section 16.1);
rearranging a formula; the equations of constant acceleration (Section 15.4).

**By the end of this section you can:** state Newton's second law; use $F = ma$
along the line of motion; find an acceleration, a resultant force or a mass;
decide the direction of the acceleration; and read a simple modelling
assumption from a question.

## 1. Newton's second law

**Newton's second law** links the resultant force on a body to the way its
velocity changes. For a body of constant mass $m$, moving in a straight line
with acceleration $a$, and with resultant force $F$ along that line:

$$
F = ma.
$$

The force is measured in newtons, the mass in kilograms, and the acceleration
in metres per second squared. One newton is the force that gives a mass of
$1$ kg an acceleration of $1$ m s$^{-2}$.

The law has three consequences worth stating plainly.

- The acceleration is in the **same direction** as the resultant force.
- A zero resultant force gives a zero acceleration, which is Newton's first law
  again.
- For a fixed force, a larger mass gives a smaller acceleration.

The symbol $F$ in $F = ma$ is the **resultant** force, not one of the forces.
Add all the forces along the line first, then use the total.

## 2. Using F = ma

Choose the direction of motion as positive, then write down the resultant force
along that direction. A force that opposes the motion is negative.

Three rearrangements are useful.

$$
a = \frac{F}{m}, \qquad m = \frac{F}{a}, \qquad F = ma.
$$

**Example 1.** A crate of mass $45$ kg is pushed along a horizontal floor. The
two workers push with horizontal forces of $120$ N and $95$ N in the same
direction. A constant resistance of $65$ N opposes the motion. Find the
acceleration of the crate.

Take the direction of motion as positive. The resultant force along the floor is

$$
120 + 95 - 65 = 150 \text{ N}.
$$

Now use $F = ma$ with $F = 150$ N and $m = 45$ kg:

$$
a = \frac{150}{45} = \frac{10}{3} \approx 3.33 \text{ m s}^{-2}.
$$

The crate accelerates at about $3.33$ m s$^{-2}$ in the direction of the push.

**Example 2.** A car of mass $1200$ kg travels along a straight road. The
driving force is $2500$ N and the total resistance is $700$ N. Find the
acceleration.

Take the direction of motion as positive. The resultant force is

$$
2500 - 700 = 1800 \text{ N}.
$$

Then

$$
a = \frac{1800}{1200} = 1.5 \text{ m s}^{-2}.
$$

**Example 3.** A body of mass $8$ kg has an acceleration of $2.5$ m s$^{-2}$.
Find the resultant force on it.

Use $F = ma$ directly:

$$
F = 8 \times 2.5 = 20 \text{ N}.
$$

The force is in the direction of the acceleration.

**Example 4.** A trolley of mass $6$ kg accelerates at $1.5$ m s$^{-2}$ against
a resistance of $5$ N. Find the driving force.

First find the resultant force needed:

$$
F = ma = 6 \times 1.5 = 9 \text{ N}.
$$

The driving force must give this resultant and also overcome the resistance, so

$$
\text{driving force} = 9 + 5 = 14 \text{ N}.
$$

## 3. Reading a modelling question

Mechanics questions use a short list of modelling words. Each one removes a
complication.

- **Particle** means the body is treated as a single point, so its size and any
  turning effect are ignored.
- **Smooth** means there is no friction.
- **Rough** means friction is present, and a coefficient of friction is given.
- **Light** means a string or rod has negligible mass.
- **Inextensible** means a string does not stretch, so two bodies joined by it
  have accelerations of the same size.
- **Air resistance is negligible** means drag is left out of the calculation.

> **Watch out.** Three slips are common.
>
> **Using one force instead of the resultant.** In Example 1, $120$ N is not the
> force in $F = ma$. The resultant is $150$ N, after the second push and the
> resistance. Use the total along the chosen direction.
>
> **Forgetting the direction.** If the acceleration is negative, the body is
> slowing down. Write the sign down with the number and say what it means.
> **Re-check:** a car braking has a forward driving force and a larger backward
> braking force, so the resultant points backwards.
>
> **Mixing up mass and weight.** The $m$ in $F = ma$ is the mass in kilograms,
> not the weight in newtons. A mass of $45$ kg has a weight of about $441$ N.
> Put the mass in the formula, and Section 16.3 explains the difference.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem. The two workers push the $45$ kg crate along the
horizontal floor with forces of $120$ N and $95$ N. A resistance of $65$ N
opposes the motion.

1. Find the resultant force along the floor.
2. Find the acceleration of the crate.
3. The resistance doubles to $130$ N while the push stays the same. Find the
   new acceleration, and say what happens to the motion.

## You should now be able to

- state Newton's second law and write it as $F = ma$;
- use the resultant force along the chosen direction;
- find an acceleration, a resultant force or a mass;
- say which way the acceleration points;
- read the modelling words *particle*, *smooth*, *rough*, *light*,
  *inextensible* and *air resistance is negligible*.
