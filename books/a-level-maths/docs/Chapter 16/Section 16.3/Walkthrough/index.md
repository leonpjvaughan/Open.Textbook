# 16.3 Weight and motion under gravity — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Newton's second law, $F = ma$ (Section 16.2); rearranging a
formula.

**By the end of this section you can:** explain the difference between mass and
weight; use $W = mg$; describe free fall and terminal velocity; and find the
normal reaction on a body in a lift that accelerates.

## 1. Mass and weight

**Mass** is the amount of matter in a body. It is measured in kilograms and it
does not change when the body moves. Mass is a scalar.

**Weight** is the force of gravity on a body. It is measured in newtons, and it
acts vertically downwards. Weight is a vector.

The link between them is

$$
W = mg,
$$

where $m$ is the mass in kilograms and $g$ is the **acceleration due to
gravity**. Near the Earth's surface, $g = 9.8$ m s$^{-2}$. The value of $g$ is
smaller on the Moon and on other planets, so a body's weight changes from place
to place while its mass stays the same.

A body of mass $1$ kg has a weight of $9.8$ N on the Earth. That is why a
kilogram of sugar feels heavy but has no weight at all in deep space.

## 2. Motion under gravity

When the only force on a falling body is its weight, the body is in **free
fall**. The resultant force is $mg$, so by $F = ma$:

$$
mg = ma, \qquad a = g.
$$

So every freely falling body accelerates at $g$ downwards, whatever its mass.

In real motion, air resistance $R$ acts upwards and opposes the fall. Newton's
second law along the vertical then gives

$$
mg - R = ma.
$$

As the body speeds up, the air resistance grows. When $R$ reaches $mg$, the
resultant force is zero and the acceleration is zero. The body now falls at a
constant velocity called the **terminal velocity**.

**Example 1.** Find the weight of a crate of mass $45$ kg. Take
$g = 9.8$ m s$^{-2}$.

Use $W = mg$:

$$
W = 45 \times 9.8 = 441 \text{ N}.
$$

The weight acts downwards. The mass is still $45$ kg.

**Example 2.** A body has a weight of $588$ N. Find its mass. Take
$g = 9.8$ m s$^{-2}$.

Rearrange $W = mg$ to make the mass the subject:

$$
m = \frac{W}{g} = \frac{588}{9.8} = 60 \text{ kg}.
$$

**Example 3.** A skydiver of mass $60$ kg falls with an air resistance of
$200$ N. Find the acceleration. Take $g = 9.8$ m s$^{-2}$.

Take downwards as positive. The weight is

$$
W = 60 \times 9.8 = 588 \text{ N}.
$$

The resultant force downwards is

$$
588 - 200 = 388 \text{ N}.
$$

Then

$$
a = \frac{388}{60} \approx 6.47 \text{ m s}^{-2}.
$$

The air resistance makes the acceleration less than $g$.

**Example 4.** The same skydiver continues to fall. Explain what happens to the
acceleration and to the velocity.

As the skydiver speeds up, the air resistance increases. The resultant force
$588 - R$ therefore decreases, so the acceleration decreases. When $R$ reaches
$588$ N the resultant force is zero and the acceleration is zero. The skydiver
then falls at a constant terminal velocity.

## 3. Apparent weight in a lift

A person standing in a lift feels the normal reaction $R$ from the floor, not
the weight. That reaction is the **apparent weight**.

Take upwards as positive. The resultant force is $R - mg$, so

$$
R - mg = ma, \qquad R = m(g + a).
$$

- If the lift accelerates **upwards**, $R$ is larger than $mg$, and the person
  feels heavier.
- If the lift accelerates **downwards**, $a$ is negative, so $R$ is smaller than
  $mg$, and the person feels lighter.
- If the lift moves at a **constant velocity**, $a = 0$ and $R = mg$.

**Example 5.** A person of mass $70$ kg stands in a lift that accelerates
upwards at $1.5$ m s$^{-2}$. Find the normal reaction from the floor. Take
$g = 9.8$ m s$^{-2}$.

Use $R = m(g + a)$:

$$
R = 70(9.8 + 1.5) = 70 \times 11.3 = 791 \text{ N}.
$$

The reaction is $791$ N, which is larger than the weight of $686$ N. The person
feels heavier during the upward acceleration.

> **Watch out.** Three mistakes are common.
>
> **Calling weight a mass in kilograms.** Weight is a force in newtons. In
> Example 1 the crate has mass $45$ kg and weight $441$ N. Writing "$45$ kg"
> where a force is wanted breaks every later step.
>
> **Assuming $R = mg$ in a lift.** That is true only when the acceleration is
> zero. While the lift accelerates, use $R = m(g + a)$ with the sign of $a$.
> **Re-check:** if the lift accelerates downwards, the person should feel
> lighter, so $R$ must come out less than $mg$.
>
> **Using $g$ far from the Earth.** The value $9.8$ m s$^{-2}$ belongs to the
> Earth's surface. A question set on the Moon gives its own value of $g$, and
> the mass stays the same.

## You should now be able to

- say what mass is and what weight is, and give the unit of each;
- use $W = mg$ in both directions;
- describe free fall and explain why all bodies fall with the same
  acceleration when air resistance is negligible;
- explain terminal velocity;
- find the normal reaction on a body in a lift that accelerates up, accelerates
  down, or moves at a constant velocity.
