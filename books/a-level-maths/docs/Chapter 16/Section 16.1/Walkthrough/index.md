# 16.1 Force and Newton's first law — Learn

**Prerequisites:** adding and subtracting vectors and finding a resultant
(Section 10.3); Pythagoras' theorem; the tangent ratio.

**By the end of this section you can:** list the forces that act in common
situations; draw a clear force diagram for a body; state Newton's first law; use
a zero resultant to find an unknown force; and explain what a resultant force
does to a moving body.

## 1. What a force is

A **force** is a push or a pull. It is a vector, so it has a size and a
direction. The unit of force is the **newton** (N). One newton is the force that
gives a mass of one kilogram an acceleration of one metre per second squared.

Forces come in two broad groups.

- **Contact forces** act where two surfaces or bodies touch. Examples are the
  normal reaction from a floor, tension in a rope, thrust from an engine,
  friction and air resistance.
- **Non-contact forces** act at a distance. Weight is the most important one
  here, and it acts on a body from the Earth without touching it.

Some forces have standard names. The **normal reaction** is the push from a
surface at right angles to that surface. **Tension** is the pull in a string or
rope. **Weight** is the force of gravity on a body. **Friction** opposes sliding
along a surface. **Air resistance**, or drag, opposes motion through air.

## 2. Force diagrams

A **force diagram**, also called a free-body diagram, shows one chosen body on
its own with every force that acts on it.

- Draw one arrow for each force.
- Start each arrow on the body.
- Point the arrow the way the force acts.
- Label each arrow with the force and its size.
- Do not draw a force that acts on a different body.

With one body on a table, the weight acts downwards and the normal reaction acts
upwards at right angles to the table.

## 3. Newton's first law

**Newton's first law** says that a body stays at rest, or keeps moving with a
constant velocity, unless a resultant force acts on it.

Two useful ways to read the law:

- If the resultant force on a body is zero, its velocity does not change. It
  stays still, or it keeps the same speed in the same straight line.
- If the resultant force is not zero, the velocity changes. The body speeds up,
  slows down, or changes direction.

The tendency of a body to keep its velocity is called its **inertia**. A body
with more mass has more inertia, so a larger force is needed to change its
velocity by the same amount.

**Example 1.** A book rests on a table. Draw a force diagram and explain why the
book stays at rest.

Two forces act on the book:

- its weight $W$, pulling downwards;
- the normal reaction $R$ from the table, pushing upwards.

The book stays at rest, so by Newton's first law the resultant force is zero.
The two forces have equal sizes and opposite directions, so $R = W$.

**Example 2.** Forces of $5$ N and $12$ N act at right angles at a point. Find
the resultant force.

Add the two perpendicular forces as vectors. The resultant has size

$$
\sqrt{5^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{169} = 13 \text{ N}.
$$

The direction is $\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{5}{12}\right) = 22.6^\circ$ from the
$12$ N force, towards the $5$ N force.

**Example 3.** A particle is in equilibrium under three forces. Two of them are
$7$ N to the right and $4$ N upwards. Find the third force.

The word **equilibrium** means the resultant force is zero. The three forces are
coplanar, meaning they act in one plane. For zero resultant, the third force
must cancel the other two. Write the two known forces as a column vector:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} \text{ N}.
$$

The third force is

$$
-\begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix} -7 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix} \text{ N}.
$$

It has size $\sqrt{7^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{65} \approx 8.06$ N and points to the
left and downwards.

**Example 4.** A cyclist moves at a constant speed of $6$ m/s in a straight
line. The total driving force is $120$ N. Find the total resistance.

A constant velocity means the resultant force is zero. The resistance must
balance the driving force, so the resistance is $120$ N backwards.

## 4. Try it: the resultant force explorer

**Predict first.** Two forces act at a point. Before you move anything, predict
what the resultant is when force B is the exact opposite of force A. Write down
the size you expect for the resultant.

:::figure ch16-force-balance

**Test.** Change the size and the direction of each force. Watch the resultant
arrow. Then make force B opposite to force A and watch the resultant shrink.
Change the size of force B only and watch what the resultant does.

**Explain.** Write two sentences. The first says what has to be true about two
forces for the point to be in equilibrium. The second says what a non-zero
resultant does to the velocity of the point.

> **Watch out.** Three mistakes are common on this page.
>
> **Thinking that motion needs a force.** A body moving at a constant velocity
> has a zero resultant force. The engine's thrust balances the resistance
> exactly. A resultant force changes the velocity, and it does not keep the
> velocity steady.
>
> **Adding sizes that are not in the same direction.** A $6$ N force and an
> $8$ N force at right angles do **not** give $14$ N. Add them as vectors, using
> Pythagoras, to get $10$ N. **Re-check:** if the two forces point along the
> same line, the sizes add or subtract; if not, they do not.
>
> **Leaving a force off a diagram.** A body at rest on a table has two forces
> on it, not one. If you show only the weight, the diagram says the book should
> fall. Every force that acts on the chosen body belongs on the diagram.

## Progress check

Return to the chapter problem. The $45$ kg crate rests on the warehouse floor
before anyone touches it. The floor is horizontal.

1. Draw the crate on its own. Add an arrow for every force that acts on the
   crate.
2. Say why the crate stays at rest, using Newton's first law.
3. The weight of the crate turns out to be $441$ N. What is the normal reaction
   from the floor, and why?

## You should now be able to

- say what a force is, and give its unit;
- name the common contact and non-contact forces;
- draw a labelled force diagram for one chosen body;
- state Newton's first law and use it in words;
- decide what a zero resultant means for a body at rest and for a body moving;
- add perpendicular forces to find a resultant, and its direction;
- use equilibrium to find a missing third force.
