# 16.1 Force and Newton's first law — Practise with help

Work each one first, closed book. Decide whether the question wants a list of
forces, a resultant, a size or a direction. Draw a quick sketch before you
calculate.

## Warm-up: remember

1. Add the vectors $(2, 3)$ and $(5, -1)$.
2. Find the size of the vector $6\mathbf{i} + 8\mathbf{j}$.
3. Work out $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $(2 + 5, 3 - 1) = (7, 2)$.
2. $\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10$.
3. $\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.

If question 2 was hard, revise the size of a vector in Section 10.2 before you
continue.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A crate is at rest on a horizontal floor. Name the forces that act on the
crate, and say what Newton's first law tells you about them.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One force comes from the Earth without touching the crate. One force comes from
the floor and acts where they touch.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The crate stays at rest, so the resultant force is zero. Write down what that
says about the sizes and the directions of the two forces.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Two forces act on the crate:

- its weight, acting downwards;
- the normal reaction from the floor, acting upwards.

The crate is at rest, so by Newton's first law the resultant force is zero. The
two forces have equal sizes and opposite directions, so the normal reaction
equals the weight.
</details>

**2.** A force of $14$ N acts due east and a force of $6$ N acts due west on a
particle. Find the resultant force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The two forces act along the same straight line, so the sizes combine by
addition or subtraction.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The larger force points east, so take $14$ subtract $6$ and keep the east
direction.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The forces are opposite, so subtract:

$$
14 - 6 = 8.
$$

The larger force points east, so the resultant is $8$ N due east.
</details>

**3.** Forces of $9$ N and $12$ N act at right angles at a point. Find the size
of the resultant and the angle it makes with the $12$ N force.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Two perpendicular forces make a right-angled triangle. The resultant is the
hypotenuse.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out $\sqrt{9^2 + 12^2}$ first. Then use $\tan^{-1}$ of the $9$ N force over
the $12$ N force.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\text{size} = \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{225} = 15 \text{ N}.
$$

$$
\text{angle} = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{9}{12}\right) = 36.9^\circ
$$

to $1$ d.p., measured from the $12$ N force towards the $9$ N force.
</details>

**4.** A particle is in equilibrium under three forces. Two of them are $10$ N
due east and $24$ N due north. Find the third force as a column vector, and
find its size.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Equilibrium means the resultant is zero, so the three force arrows form a closed
triangle. The third force cancels the other two.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write the two known forces as $(10, 24)$ N. The third force is the negative of
that vector.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The two known forces are $(10, 24)$ N. The third force is

$$
-(10, 24) = (-10, -24) \text{ N}.
$$

Its size is

$$
\sqrt{10^2 + 24^2} = \sqrt{676} = 26 \text{ N}.
$$
</details>

**5.** A car travels at a constant velocity of $20$ m/s along a straight road.
The driving force is $600$ N. Find the total resistance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A constant velocity is the second case in Newton's first law.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The resultant force is zero, so the resistance balances the driving force.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The velocity is constant, so the resultant force is zero. The driving force and
the resistance are equal and opposite, so the total resistance is $600$ N
backwards.
</details>

**6.** Forces $(3, 4)$ N and $(5, -2)$ N act on a particle. Find the resultant
force and its size.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add the matching components first.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The resultant is $(3 + 5, 4 - 2)$ N. Then take the square root of the sum of the
squares of the two components.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
(3, 4) + (5, -2) = (8, 2) \text{ N}.
$$

$$
\text{size} = \sqrt{8^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{68} = 2\sqrt{17} \approx 8.25 \text{ N}.
$$
</details>

**7.** Forces $(2, 5)$ N, $(-4, 1)$ N and $(1, -3)$ N act at a point. Find the
resultant force, and state whether the particle is in equilibrium.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Add all three vectors component by component.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

The $x$-component is $2 - 4 + 1$ and the $y$-component is $5 + 1 - 3$.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
(2 - 4 + 1)\mathbf{i} + (5 + 1 - 3)\mathbf{j} = -\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j} \text{ N}.
$$

The resultant is not the zero vector, so the particle is **not** in equilibrium.
It accelerates in the direction of $-\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j}$.
</details>

**8.** A box is at rest on a horizontal table. A student says the only force on
the box is its weight, so the box should accelerate downwards. Explain what is
missing from this reasoning.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The box touches the table. Think about the force the table exerts where they
touch.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

If the box is at rest, the resultant force on it must be zero. Compare the size
of the weight with the size of the missing force.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The student has left out the normal reaction from the table. The table pushes up
on the box at the place where they touch. The box is at rest, so the resultant
force is zero, which means the normal reaction equals the weight. The two forces
cancel, so the box does not accelerate.
</details>
