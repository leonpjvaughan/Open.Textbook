# 10.1 The solar system and orbits — Learn

**Prerequisites:** from earlier science, that gravity is a non-contact force that
pulls masses together; from Chapter 3, that velocity is speed in a given
direction; from Chapter 9, that nuclear fusion joins light nuclei and releases
energy; and writing very large numbers in standard form.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe the main features of the solar
system; compare the planets, their moons and artificial satellites; explain how
gravity keeps a planet or a satellite in a circular orbit; explain how the Sun
formed from a cloud of dust and gas; and explain why fusion reactions keep the
Sun stable.

## 1. What is in the solar system

The **solar system** is the Sun and everything that orbits it. It has one star,
the Sun. Eight **planets** orbit the Sun, and **dwarf planets** such as Pluto
orbit it too. Smaller bodies called asteroids and comets also orbit the Sun.

A **moon** is a natural satellite. It orbits a planet. Some planets have many
moons, and some have none. An **artificial satellite** is built by people and put
into orbit. Hundreds of artificial satellites orbit the Earth.

The Sun is one of billions of stars in the **Milky Way** galaxy. The solar
system is a very small part of the Milky Way.

The planets are spread out over a huge range of distances. This table gives the
distance of each planet from the Sun, and the time it takes to orbit once.

| Planet | Distance from the Sun (million km) | Time for one orbit (Earth years) |
|---|---|---|
| Mercury | 58 | 0.24 |
| Venus | 108 | 0.62 |
| Earth | 150 | 1.0 |
| Mars | 228 | 1.9 |
| Jupiter | 780 | 12 |
| Saturn | 1400 | 29 |
| Uranus | 2900 | 84 |
| Neptune | 4500 | 165 |

Two patterns stand out. The planets further from the Sun are much further apart
than the inner planets. They also take much longer to go round once. Neptune is
30 times further from the Sun than the Earth, and its orbit takes 165 times
longer.

**Worked example 1.** Describe the main features of our solar system.

- There is one star, the Sun.
- Eight planets orbit the Sun, and dwarf planets orbit it too.
- Natural satellites, called moons, orbit the planets.
- Artificial satellites built by people also orbit the Earth and other bodies.
- Asteroids and comets orbit the Sun.
- The solar system is a small part of the Milky Way galaxy.

## 2. Planets, moons and artificial satellites

Planets, moons and artificial satellites are all held in orbit by gravity. That
is what they have in common. They differ in where they orbit and in how they
were made.

| Body | What it orbits | Made by |
|---|---|---|
| Planet | A star, such as the Sun | Nature |
| Moon | A planet | Nature |
| Artificial satellite | The Earth or another body | People |

A planet is large and roughly spherical. A moon is smaller than the planet it
orbits. An artificial satellite can be any size and shape, because engineers
build it for a job. A communications satellite carries aerials. A weather
satellite carries cameras.

**Worked example 2.** Give one similarity and one distinction between a moon and
an artificial satellite.

One similarity is that both orbit a larger body, and gravity keeps both of them
in orbit. One distinction is that a moon is natural and always orbits a planet.
An artificial satellite is built by people and can orbit a planet or another
body.

## 3. Gravity and circular orbits

**Gravity** is a force of attraction between any two masses. It acts across
empty space. Every object with mass pulls on every other object with mass.

Gravity provides the force that keeps a planet or a satellite in a circular
orbit. The force on the orbiting body is directed towards the centre of the body
it orbits. This force bends the path of the object. Without the force, the object
would travel in a straight line.

The telescope in the chapter problem is 540 km above the Earth. The Earth still
pulls on it. The pull bends its path into a circle, so it falls around the Earth
instead of into it.

> **Watch out.** A pupil may say that the telescope floats because there is no
> gravity in space, or because it is above the air. That is a trap. Gravity acts
> between all masses, so the Earth still pulls on the telescope. The force is
> weaker far from the Earth, but it is not zero. Re-check by asking what force
> keeps the Moon in orbit 384 000 km away.

**Worked example 3.** Explain what keeps a planet in a circular orbit around the
Sun.

Gravity provides a force of attraction between the planet and the Sun. The force
on the planet is directed towards the Sun, the centre of the orbit. The force
changes the direction of the planet, so its path curves into a circle instead of
going in a straight line.

**Worked example 4.** The telescope orbits 540 km above the Earth. A radio signal
from the telescope travels at $3.0 \times 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$. Calculate the time
for the signal to reach the ground.

$$
\begin{aligned}
t &= \frac{d}{v} \\
  &= \frac{5.4 \times 10^{5}}{3.0 \times 10^{8}} \\
  &= 1.8 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

The signal travels at a steady speed, so use $\text{time} =
\dfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{speed}}$. First change the height to metres, so
$540\,\text{km} = 5.4 \times 10^{5}\,\text{m}$. The answer is
$1.8 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{s}$, which is 1.8 ms.

## 4. Velocity and a stable orbit

Velocity is speed in a given direction. A body in a circular orbit moves at a
constant speed, but its direction changes all the time. Its speed stays the same,
yet its velocity changes, because the direction is part of the velocity.

A stable circular orbit needs a speed that matches its radius. If the speed of
the body changes, it can no longer stay in a stable orbit at the same radius. The
radius must change until the speed and the radius match again. The exact rule for
this is studied later; at GCSE you explain it in words.

**Worked example 5.** The telescope moves at a constant speed in its orbit, but
its velocity changes. Explain how this is possible.

Velocity is speed in a given direction. As the telescope goes round its circular
orbit, the direction of its motion changes all the time. Its speed stays the
same, but the changing direction means the velocity changes.

## 5. How the Sun formed and why it is stable

The Sun was formed about 4600 million years ago. It began as a cloud of dust and
gas, called a **nebula**. Gravity pulled the dust and gas together. As the cloud
shrank, it became hotter and denser.

When the centre became hot enough, hydrogen nuclei began to join together to form
helium. This process is **nuclear fusion**. Fusion releases a large amount of
energy. A star is born when fusion starts in its core.

Fusion pushes outwards and tends to make the star expand. Gravity pulls the
material inwards and tends to make the star collapse. In a stable star, these two
are equal. The star is in **equilibrium**, so it keeps the same size and shines
at a steady rate.

**Worked example 6.** Explain why the Sun stays the same size.

Fusion reactions in the core release energy. This pushes outwards and tends to
make the Sun expand. Gravity pulls the material of the Sun inwards and tends to
make it collapse. The outward push and the inward pull are equal, so the Sun is
in equilibrium and stays the same size.

## 7. The life cycle of a star

A star goes through a life cycle. The path it takes depends on its mass.

A star about the size of the Sun follows this path.

1. A cloud of dust and gas is pulled together by gravity.
2. Fusion starts, and the star becomes a **main-sequence** star like the Sun.
3. The star expands into a **red giant**.
4. It then cools and collapses into a **white dwarf**.

A star much more massive than the Sun follows a different path.

1. It forms from a nebula and becomes a main-sequence star.
2. It expands into a **red supergiant**.
3. It explodes as a **supernova**.
4. The core is left behind as a **neutron star**, or as a **black hole** if the
   star was massive enough.

Fusion in stars makes the naturally occurring elements up to iron. Elements
heavier than iron are made in a supernova. The explosion spreads those elements
through space, and they become part of new stars and planets.
