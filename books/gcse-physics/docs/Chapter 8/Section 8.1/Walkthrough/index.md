# 8.1 Changes of state and the particle model — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 2.1, especially the idea that matter is made of
particles; Chapter 1, especially energy stores and energy transfers.

**By the end of this section you can:** use the particle model to describe a
solid, a liquid and a gas; calculate density from mass and volume; describe how
to measure the density of a solid or a liquid; list the changes of state; and
explain why mass is conserved when a substance changes state.

## 1. The three states of matter

Everything around you is made of tiny particles: atoms, or groups of atoms called
molecules. The **particle model** describes how these particles are arranged and
how they move. The model explains the three states of matter.

- In a **solid**, the particles are close together in a regular pattern. They
  vibrate about fixed positions, so a solid keeps its own shape.
- In a **liquid**, the particles are still close together, but they are not in a
  fixed pattern. They move around each other, so a liquid flows and takes the
  shape of its container.
- In a **gas**, the particles are far apart. They move quickly and randomly, so a
  gas fills any container.

The particles themselves do not change during a change of state. Only their
arrangement and their energy change.

**Worked example 1.** Describe the difference between a solid and a gas using the
particle model.

A model answer is:

- In a solid, the particles are close together and held in a regular pattern.
  They can only vibrate about fixed positions.
- In a gas, the particles are far apart and move quickly in random directions.
  The forces between them are very weak.

## 2. Density

**Density** tells you how much mass there is in each unit of volume. It is
defined by this equation.

$$ \rho = \frac{m}{V} $$

where $\rho$ is the density in kilograms per metre cubed
($\text{kg/m}^3$), $m$ is the mass in kilograms and $V$ is the volume in metres
cubed. The Greek letter $\rho$ is called rho.

Density is a property of a material. A large block of ice and a small block of
ice have the same density, because both the mass and the volume grow together.

**Worked example 2.** The block of ice in the chapter problem has a volume of
$2.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^3$. The density of ice is $920\,\text{kg/m}^3$.
Calculate the mass of the block.

We need the mass, so we rearrange $\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}$ to make $m$ the subject.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= \rho V \\
  &= 920 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-3} \\
  &= 2.3\,\text{kg}
\end{aligned}
$$

The block has a mass of $2.3\,\text{kg}$.

**Worked example 3.** A piece of metal has a mass of $67.5\,\text{g}$ and a
volume of $25\,\text{cm}^3$. Calculate its density in $\text{g/cm}^3$ and in
$\text{kg/m}^3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho &= \frac{m}{V} \\
     &= \frac{67.5}{25} \\
     &= 2.7\,\text{g/cm}^3
\end{aligned}
$$

To change $\text{g/cm}^3$ into $\text{kg/m}^3$, multiply by 1000, because there
are 1000 g in a kilogram and $10^6\,\text{cm}^3$ in a cubic metre.

$$ 2.7 \times 1000 = 2700\,\text{kg/m}^3 $$

## 3. Measuring density

You can find the density of a material in the laboratory. The method depends on
the shape of the object.

- **A regular solid**, such as a block or a cylinder. Measure its length, width
  and height with a ruler. Calculate the volume, then use $\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}$.
- **An irregular solid**, such as a stone. Find its volume by displacement. Lower
  it into water in a measuring cylinder and read how much the water level rises.
  The rise is the volume of the solid.
- **A liquid.** Measure the mass of an empty container, then the mass of the
  container with the liquid. The difference is the mass of the liquid. Measure
  the volume with a measuring cylinder.

**Worked example 4.** An empty beaker has a mass of $120\,\text{g}$. When
$100\,\text{cm}^3$ of a liquid is poured in, the total mass is $204\,\text{g}$.
Calculate the density of the liquid.

The mass of the liquid is the total mass minus the mass of the beaker.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= 204 - 120 = 84\,\text{g} \\
\rho &= \frac{m}{V} \\
     &= \frac{84}{100} \\
     &= 0.84\,\text{g/cm}^3
\end{aligned}
$$

The liquid has a density of $0.84\,\text{g/cm}^3$, which is
$840\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

## 4. Why the states have different densities

The particle model explains the densities of the three states.

In a solid and a liquid, the particles are packed closely together, so the
density is high. Solids and liquids usually have similar densities.

In a gas, the particles are far apart, with large empty spaces between them. The
same mass of gas therefore fills a much larger volume, so a gas has a much lower
density than a solid or a liquid.

Water is unusual in one way. Ice is **less** dense than liquid water. When water
freezes, the particles are held in an open pattern that takes up more space. That
is why ice floats on water, and why water expands when it freezes.

## 5. Changes of state

A substance can change from one state to another. Each change has its own name.

| Change | Name |
|---|---|
| Solid to liquid | Melting |
| Liquid to solid | Freezing |
| Liquid to gas | Boiling or evaporating |
| Gas to liquid | Condensing |
| Solid to gas, or gas to solid | Sublimating |

A change of state is a **physical change**. The particles stay the same, and no
new substance is made. If you reverse the change, the material gets back its
original properties. This makes a change of state different from a chemical
change, where a new substance forms.

**Mass is conserved** during a change of state. When ice melts, the mass of the
water equals the mass of the ice. The particles have not been created or
destroyed; they have only moved further apart.

**Worked example 5.** A $2.3\,\text{kg}$ block of ice melts completely. Explain
what happens to the mass and to the volume, and why.

A model answer is:

- The mass stays at $2.3\,\text{kg}$. The same particles are present before and
  after melting, so no mass is lost.
- The volume decreases. In liquid water the particles are packed a little more
  closely than in the open ice pattern.
