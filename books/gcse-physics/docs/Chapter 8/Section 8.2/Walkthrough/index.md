# 8.2 Internal energy, energy transfers and particle motions — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 8.1, especially the particle model and the changes of
state; Chapter 1, especially energy stores, energy transfers and power.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe the internal energy of a system;
explain what heating does to the particles; calculate an energy transfer with
$\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ and with $E = mL$; tell specific heat capacity and
specific latent heat apart; and interpret a heating or cooling graph.

## 1. Internal energy

Every particle in a substance has energy. It has kinetic energy because it is
moving or vibrating, and it has potential energy because of the forces between it
and the particles around it.

The **internal energy** of a system is the total kinetic energy and the total
potential energy of all the particles in the system.

Internal energy is not the same as temperature. Temperature measures the
**average** kinetic energy of the particles. A small cup of water at
$80\,^\circ\text{C}$ is at a higher temperature than a full bath at
$40\,^\circ\text{C}$, but the bath holds much more internal energy, because it
contains far more particles.

## 2. Heating a system

**Heating** is an energy transfer to a system, from a hotter place to a cooler
one. Heating increases the internal energy of the system. The extra energy can do
one of two things.

- It can raise the temperature. The particles move faster, so their average
  kinetic energy increases. The temperature rises.
- It can produce a change of state. The energy breaks the forces between the
  particles, so their potential energy increases. The temperature does not
  change while this happens.

The size of the temperature rise depends on three things: the mass of the
substance heated, the material it is made of, and the energy supplied to it.

**Worked example 1.** A student heats $2.0\,\text{kg}$ of water from
$20\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $50\,^\circ\text{C}$. The specific heat capacity of water
is $4200\,\text{J/kg}\,^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the energy transferred to the
water.

The temperature change is

$$ \Delta\theta = 50 - 20 = 30\,^\circ\text{C} $$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta E &= mc\Delta\theta \\
         &= 2.0 \times 4200 \times 30 \\
         &= 252\,000\,\text{J} \\
         &= 2.5 \times 10^5\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

The water gains $2.5 \times 10^5\,\text{J}$.

## 3. Specific heat capacity

The **specific heat capacity** of a substance is the energy needed to raise the
temperature of one kilogram of the substance by one degree Celsius. Its unit is
$\text{J/kg}\,^\circ\text{C}$.

The equation for a temperature change is

$$ \Delta E = mc\Delta\theta $$

where $\Delta E$ is the change in thermal energy in joules, $m$ is the mass in
kilograms, $c$ is the specific heat capacity and $\Delta\theta$ is the
temperature change in degrees Celsius.

A material with a large specific heat capacity needs a lot of energy for a small
temperature rise. Water has a large value, $4200\,\text{J/kg}\,^\circ\text{C}$.
That is why water is used to store thermal energy in a central heating system.

**Worked example 2.** A block of metal of mass $2.0\,\text{kg}$ gains
$46\,200\,\text{J}$ when its temperature rises by $55\,^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate
the specific heat capacity of the metal.

Make $c$ the subject of $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{\Delta E}{m\Delta\theta} \\
  &= \frac{46\,200}{2.0 \times 55} \\
  &= 420\,\text{J/kg}\,^\circ\text{C}
\end{aligned}
$$

The metal has a specific heat capacity of $420\,\text{J/kg}\,^\circ\text{C}$.

## 4. Specific latent heat

Energy is also needed to change the state of a substance. The energy needed to
change state is called **latent heat**. During a change of state the energy
supplied increases the internal energy, but the temperature does not change.

The **specific latent heat** of a substance is the energy needed to change the
state of one kilogram of the substance with no change in temperature. Its unit is
$\text{J/kg}$.

The equation for a change of state is

$$ E = mL $$

where $E$ is the energy in joules, $m$ is the mass in kilograms and $L$ is the
specific latent heat in $\text{J/kg}$.

There are two kinds of specific latent heat.

- **Specific latent heat of fusion** is the energy to change from solid to
  liquid. For ice, $L = 3.3 \times 10^5\,\text{J/kg}$.
- **Specific latent heat of vaporisation** is the energy to change from liquid to
  vapour. For water, $L = 2.3 \times 10^6\,\text{J/kg}$.

**Worked example 3.** Calculate the energy needed to melt $0.50\,\text{kg}$ of
ice at $0\,^\circ\text{C}$. The specific latent heat of fusion of ice is
$3.3 \times 10^5\,\text{J/kg}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= mL \\
  &= 0.50 \times 3.3 \times 10^5 \\
  &= 165\,000\,\text{J} \\
  &= 1.65 \times 10^5\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

To two significant figures the energy is $1.7 \times 10^5\,\text{J}$.

**Worked example 4.** Calculate the energy needed to boil away
$0.20\,\text{kg}$ of water at $100\,^\circ\text{C}$. The specific latent heat of
vaporisation of water is $2.3 \times 10^6\,\text{J/kg}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= mL \\
  &= 0.20 \times 2.3 \times 10^6 \\
  &= 460\,000\,\text{J} \\
  &= 4.6 \times 10^5\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 5. Heating and cooling graphs

A heating graph shows temperature on the vertical axis and energy or time on the
horizontal axis. It has two kinds of part.

- A **sloped** part, where the temperature is changing. Here the substance is
  being heated without changing state, and $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ applies.
- A **flat** part, where the temperature does not change. Here the substance is
  changing state, and $E = mL$ applies.

A cooling graph is the same shape read from right to left. The flat parts are
still the changes of state.

**Worked example 5.** A heating graph for a pure substance has a flat part at
$0\,^\circ\text{C}$ and another at $100\,^\circ\text{C}$. Explain what is
happening on each flat part.

A model answer is:

- On the flat part at $0\,^\circ\text{C}$, the substance is melting. The energy
  supplied breaks the forces between the particles, so the state changes but the
  temperature stays the same.
- On the flat part at $100\,^\circ\text{C}$, the substance is boiling. The energy
  supplied separates the particles completely, so the state changes again, and
  the temperature stays the same.

**Explain.** The energy needed to melt the ice is the same whatever the power of
the heater. Explain why a higher power makes the flat part shorter in time but
does not change the energy needed. Write your answer in two or three sentences.

> **Watch out.** Specific heat capacity and specific latent heat are not the same
> thing. Specific heat capacity is used when the **temperature changes**, and its
> unit is $\text{J/kg}\,^\circ\text{C}$. Specific latent heat is used when the
> temperature is **constant during a change of state**, and its unit is
> $\text{J/kg}$. A flat part of a heating graph means a change of state, so use
> $E = mL$ there, not $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$. Re-check which part of the
> graph the question is about before you choose an equation.

1. Calculate the energy needed to melt the ice. Use

## You should now be able to

- describe internal energy as the total kinetic and potential energy of the
  particles in a system;
- explain how heating can raise the temperature or change the state;
- relate the temperature of a substance to the average kinetic energy of its
  particles;
- define specific heat capacity and use $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$;
- define specific latent heat and use $E = mL$;
- distinguish between specific heat capacity and specific latent heat;
- interpret a heating or cooling graph, including its flat parts.
