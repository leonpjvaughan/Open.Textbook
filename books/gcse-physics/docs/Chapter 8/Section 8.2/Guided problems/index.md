# 8.2 Internal energy, energy transfers and particle motions — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check.

## Warm-up: remember

Answer these from memory. They use ideas from Section 8.1 and Chapter 1.

1. A block has a mass of $1.2\,\text{kg}$ and a volume of
   $1.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^3$. Calculate its density.
2. A heater transfers $30\,000\,\text{J}$ of energy in $20\,\text{s}$. Calculate
   its power.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\rho = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{1.2}{1.5 \times 10^{-3}} = 800\,\text{kg/m}^3$.
2. $P = \dfrac{E}{t} = \dfrac{30\,000}{20} = 1500\,\text{W}$.

If question 1 went wrong, look again at Section 8.1: divide the mass by the
volume. If question 2 went wrong, remember that power is the rate of energy
transfer, so divide the energy by the time.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A block of copper of mass $0.50\,\text{kg}$ is heated through
$80\,^\circ\text{C}$. The specific heat capacity of copper is
$390\,\text{J/kg}\,^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the energy transferred to the
copper.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The temperature changes, so use the equation with specific heat capacity. You
know the mass, the specific heat capacity and the temperature change.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ and put in $m = 0.50$, $c = 390$ and
$\Delta\theta = 80$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta E &= mc\Delta\theta \\
         &= 0.50 \times 390 \times 80 \\
         &= 15\,600\,\text{J} \\
         &= 1.56 \times 10^4\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$
</details>

**2.** A liquid of mass $1.5\,\text{kg}$ gains $13\,500\,\text{J}$ when its
temperature rises by $20\,^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the specific heat capacity
of the liquid.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Start from $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ and make $c$ the subject.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $c = \dfrac{\Delta E}{m\Delta\theta}$, then divide $13\,500$ by
$1.5 \times 20$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{\Delta E}{m\Delta\theta} \\
  &= \frac{13\,500}{1.5 \times 20} \\
  &= 450\,\text{J/kg}\,^\circ\text{C}
\end{aligned}
$$
</details>

**3.** Calculate the energy needed to melt $0.80\,\text{kg}$ of ice at
$0\,^\circ\text{C}$. The specific latent heat of fusion of ice is
$3.3 \times 10^5\,\text{J/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The ice is at its melting point, so the temperature does not change. Use the
latent heat equation.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $E = mL$ and multiply $0.80\,\text{kg}$ by
$3.3 \times 10^5\,\text{J/kg}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= mL \\
  &= 0.80 \times 3.3 \times 10^5 \\
  &= 264\,000\,\text{J} \\
  &= 2.64 \times 10^5\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$
</details>

**4.** Calculate the energy needed to boil away $0.050\,\text{kg}$ of water at
$100\,^\circ\text{C}$. The specific latent heat of vaporisation of water is
$2.3 \times 10^6\,\text{J/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Boiling is a change of state, so this is a latent heat question. The specific
latent heat of vaporisation is much larger than the value for fusion.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write $E = mL$ and multiply $0.050\,\text{kg}$ by
$2.3 \times 10^6\,\text{J/kg}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= mL \\
  &= 0.050 \times 2.3 \times 10^6 \\
  &= 115\,000\,\text{J} \\
  &= 1.15 \times 10^5\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$
</details>

**5.** Two students heat $1.0\,\text{kg}$ of ice at $0\,^\circ\text{C}$. One
student melts the ice completely. The other student warms $1.0\,\text{kg}$ of
water from $0\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $45\,^\circ\text{C}$. Which student transfers
more energy? Show your working.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One change is a change of state, so it uses latent heat. The other is a
temperature change, so it uses specific heat capacity. Calculate both energies.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

For melting, use $E = mL$ with $L = 3.3 \times 10^5\,\text{J/kg}$. For warming,
use $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ with $c = 4200\,\text{J/kg}\,^\circ\text{C}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Melting:

$$ E = mL = 1.0 \times 3.3 \times 10^5 = 3.3 \times 10^5\,\text{J} $$

Warming:

$$ \Delta E = mc\Delta\theta = 1.0 \times 4200 \times 45 = 1.89 \times 10^5\,\text{J} $$

Melting needs more energy, $3.3 \times 10^5\,\text{J}$ compared with
$1.89 \times 10^5\,\text{J}$.
</details>

**6.** A heating graph for a pure substance has a flat part. Explain what is
happening to the particles during this flat part, and what happens to the
temperature.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A flat part means the energy supplied is not raising the temperature. Think about
what else the energy can do.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say that the substance is changing state, then say what the energy does to the
forces between the particles.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

On the flat part the substance is changing state. The energy supplied does work
against the forces between the particles, so the potential energy of the
particles increases and their spacing changes. The average kinetic energy of the
particles does not change, so the temperature stays constant.

This answer is a model answer for you to compare with your own wording.
</details>

**7.** Say which relationships you will use, then calculate the total energy
needed to warm $0.20\,\text{kg}$ of water from $20\,^\circ\text{C}$ to
$100\,^\circ\text{C}$ and then boil it all away. Use
$c = 4200\,\text{J/kg}\,^\circ\text{C}$ and
$L = 2.3 \times 10^6\,\text{J/kg}$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

There are two stages. One warms the water and changes the temperature. The other
boils the water and changes the state.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

For the warming stage use $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ with
$\Delta\theta = 80\,^\circ\text{C}$. For the boiling stage use $E = mL$. Add the
two energies.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Warming from $20\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\,^\circ\text{C}$:

$$ \Delta E = mc\Delta\theta = 0.20 \times 4200 \times 80 = 67\,200\,\text{J} $$

Boiling at $100\,^\circ\text{C}$:

$$ E = mL = 0.20 \times 2.3 \times 10^6 = 460\,000\,\text{J} $$

Total energy:

$$ 67\,200 + 460\,000 = 527\,200\,\text{J} = 5.3 \times 10^5\,\text{J} $$

The total is $5.3 \times 10^5\,\text{J}$ to two significant figures.
</details>
