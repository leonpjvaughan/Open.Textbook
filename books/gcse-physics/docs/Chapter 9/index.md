# Chapter 9: Atomic structure

## The chapter problem: the hospital scanning unit

A hospital uses radioactive isotopes to look inside the body and to treat some
illnesses. One isotope is a tracer. It is injected into a patient, and a detector
outside the body follows where it goes.

These are the facts to use all the way through the chapter.

- Each nucleus of the tracer has **43 protons** and **56 neutrons**.
- The tracer emits **gamma** radiation.
- The half-life of the tracer is **6.0 hours**.
- A freshly prepared sample has a count rate of **800 counts per second**.
- The background count rate in the scanning room is **20 counts per second**.
- The hospital also uses a source that destroys unwanted tissue.
- The hospital's electricity is generated by a nuclear **fission** reactor.

By the end of the chapter you will work out how many protons, neutrons and
electrons the tracer has. You will find how its count rate falls during a day.
You will explain why the tracer emits gamma radiation, why its half-life is
chosen carefully, and how the hospital's reactor makes electricity. You will
also decide whether a tracer scan is worth its small risk.

> **First contact: no working yet.** Predict. A sample of the tracer is prepared
> at 08:00 and used at 20:00. By then, how much of the original count rate is
> left: none, one half, one quarter, or one sixteenth? Write your prediction
> down, with a reason.

<summary>If you are not sure where to start</summary>

The half-life is 6.0 hours. Count how many 6.0-hour intervals fit between 08:00
and 20:00. In each interval, the count rate halves.
</details>

## By the end of this chapter

You will be able to:

- describe the structure of an atom, and name the particles inside it;
- state the atomic number, the mass number and the number of electrons of an
  atom or an ion;
- explain what isotopes are, and describe how the model of the atom changed;
- describe alpha, beta and gamma radiation, and write balanced decay equations;
- explain what half-life means, and use it to find a count rate or an activity;
- compare contamination with irradiation, and describe the hazard of each;
- describe background radiation, and explain why the dose a person receives
  varies;
- describe and evaluate the medical uses of nuclear radiation;
- describe nuclear fission and nuclear fusion, and explain where each happens.

## Where it is used

Radioactive isotopes are used in medicine to image organs and to treat some
cancers. They are used in industry to measure the thickness of paper and metal.
A smoke alarm uses a tiny alpha source. Nuclear reactors make electricity without
releasing carbon dioxide while they run. The same ideas also explain the
radiation that reaches us from rocks, from space and from the air.
