# 9.1 Nuclear atom and isotopes — Learn

**Prerequisites:** the idea from earlier science that all matter is made of
atoms; writing very small numbers in standard form; using a table to read
values.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe the structure of an atom;
state the atomic number, the mass number and the number of electrons; explain
what an isotope is; and describe how the model of the atom changed.

## 1. Atoms are mostly empty space

An atom is the smallest part of an element. It is far too small to see. The
radius of an atom is about $1 \times 10^{-10}\,\text{m}$.

Almost all of the mass of an atom sits in a tiny central **nucleus**. The radius
of the nucleus is less than $\dfrac{1}{10\,000}$ of the radius of the atom. If
the atom were the size of a sports hall, the nucleus would be a grain of sand at
its centre.

## 2. The particles inside an atom

An atom is built from three particles.

| Particle | Relative charge | Where it is |
|---|---|---|
| Proton | $+1$ | In the nucleus |
| Neutron | $0$ | In the nucleus |
| Electron | $-1$ | In energy levels around the nucleus |

The nucleus is **positively charged**, because the protons in it are positive
and the neutrons are neutral. The electrons are **negatively charged**, and they
are arranged at different distances from the nucleus. Each distance is an
**energy level**.

An atom has no overall charge, because the number of electrons equals the number
of protons.

## 3. Atomic number and mass number

Two numbers describe the nucleus of an atom.

- The **atomic number** is the number of protons in the nucleus. Every atom of
  the same element has the same atomic number.
- The **mass number** is the total number of protons and neutrons in the
  nucleus.

An atom is written with these numbers beside its symbol.

$$ ^{A}_{Z}\text{X} $$

Here $Z$ is the atomic number, $A$ is the mass number and $\text{X}$ is the
element symbol. So $^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$
has 11 protons and $23 - 11 = 12$ neutrons.

**Worked example 1.** An atom has 11 protons and 12 neutrons. State its atomic
number, its mass number and the number of electrons in the neutral atom.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{atomic number} &= 11 \\
\text{mass number}   &= 11 + 12 = 23 \\
\text{electrons}     &= 11
\end{aligned}
$$

The atomic number is the number of protons. The mass number counts protons and
neutrons together. A neutral atom has the same number of electrons as protons.

## 4. Isotopes

Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons. These atoms
are called **isotopes** of that element.

- **Carbon-12** has 6 protons and 6 neutrons, so $A = 12$.
- **Carbon-14** has 6 protons and 8 neutrons, so $A = 14$.

Both are carbon, because both have 6 protons. Their mass numbers are different,
because their numbers of neutrons are different. Isotopes of an element have the
same chemical behaviour, because the number of electrons is the same.

**Worked example 2.** Write the symbols for carbon-12 and carbon-14, and explain
why they are isotopes.

Carbon-12 is $^{12}_{6}\text{C}$ and carbon-14 is $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$. Both have
6 protons, so both are atoms of carbon. They have 6 and 8 neutrons, so their mass
numbers are 12 and 14. Atoms with the same number of protons but different
numbers of neutrons are isotopes.

## 5. Ions

An atom can lose or gain outer electrons. It then becomes a charged particle
called an **ion**.

- An atom that **loses** one or more electrons has more protons than electrons,
  so it becomes a **positive** ion.
- An atom that **gains** electrons has more electrons than protons, so it becomes
  a **negative** ion.

For example, a magnesium atom can lose two outer electrons. It then has 12
protons and 10 electrons, so its charge is $+2$.

## 6. Electron energy levels

An electron can move between energy levels. It does this by absorbing or
emitting electromagnetic radiation.

- When an atom **absorbs** electromagnetic radiation, an electron moves to a
  higher energy level, further from the nucleus.
- When an atom **emits** electromagnetic radiation, an electron moves to a lower
  energy level, closer to the nucleus.

The radiation absorbed or emitted has an energy that matches the gap between the
two levels.

> **Watch out.** The mass number is not the number of neutrons. It counts the
> protons and the neutrons together. A carbon-14 atom has mass number 14, but it
> has only 8 neutrons. Also take care not to change the proton number when you
> write an isotope: isotopes of carbon always have 6 protons. Re-check by adding
> the protons and neutrons to see whether you get the mass number.

## 7. How the model of the atom changed

Scientific models change when new evidence does not fit them. The model of the
atom changed several times.

1. **A solid sphere.** Before the electron was discovered, atoms were thought to
   be tiny spheres that could not be divided.
2. **The plum pudding model.** The discovery of the electron led to a model in
   which the atom was a ball of positive charge with negative electrons spread
   through it.
3. **The nuclear model.** In the alpha particle scattering experiment, most
   alpha particles passed straight through a thin gold foil, but a few were
   repelled back. This showed that the mass and the positive charge are
   concentrated in a tiny nucleus. The nuclear model replaced the plum pudding
   model.
4. **Electron shells.** Niels Bohr suggested that electrons orbit the nucleus at
   specific distances, and his calculations agreed with experiments.
5. **The proton.** Later experiments showed that the positive charge of the
   nucleus comes in whole numbers of a smaller particle, which was named the
   proton.
6. **The neutron.** About 20 years later, James Chadwick found evidence for the
   neutron in the nucleus.

**Worked example 3.** Explain how the alpha particle scattering experiment
changed the model of the atom.

Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil, so most of the atom is
empty space. A small number were repelled back, so the positive charge is
concentrated in a very small region. This evidence produced the nuclear model:
a tiny, positively charged nucleus holding almost all of the mass, with the
electrons around the outside.

**Worked example 4.** The radius of a nucleus is about
$1 \times 10^{-14}\,\text{m}$. The radius of an atom is about
$1 \times 10^{-10}\,\text{m}$. Work out how many times smaller the nucleus is
than the atom.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1 \times 10^{-10}}{1 \times 10^{-14}} &= 1 \times 10^{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

The atom is about $10\,000$ times wider than its nucleus.

- state the relative charge and the position of a proton, a neutron and an
