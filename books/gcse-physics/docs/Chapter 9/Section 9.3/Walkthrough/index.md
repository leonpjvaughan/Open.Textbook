# 9.3 Hazards and uses of radioactive emissions and background radiation — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 9.2, especially the three types of radiation, their
penetration and ionising power, and half-life.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe background radiation and where
it comes from; explain why the dose a person receives varies; describe the
hazards of ionising radiation; and describe and evaluate the medical uses of
nuclear radiation.

## 1. Background radiation

**Background radiation** is the ionising radiation that is around us all the
time. It comes from several sources.

- **Natural sources.** Rocks and soil contain radioactive atoms, and cosmic rays
  reach the Earth from space.
- **Man-made sources.** Fallout from nuclear weapon tests, and releases from
  nuclear accidents, add to the background.

The level of background radiation, and the **radiation dose** a person receives,
depend on where they live and on their job. A radiographer works near sources, so
receives a larger dose than an office worker. A person who lives in an area with
more radon in the rocks also receives a larger dose. A person who lives high
above sea level receives more cosmic rays.

Radiation dose is measured in **sieverts** (Sv). A thousand millisieverts (mSv)
make one sievert.

## 2. Measuring a count rate

A detector measures a count rate in counts per second. The detector also records
background radiation, so to find the count rate from a source alone you subtract
the background count rate.

**Worked example 1.** A detector measures 260 counts per minute near a source.
The background count rate is 20 counts per minute. Calculate the corrected count
rate from the source.

$$ 260 - 20 = 240 \ \text{counts per minute} $$

## 3. The hazards of ionising radiation

Ionising radiation can knock electrons away from the atoms it passes through.
Inside a living cell, this can damage the DNA. Damaged cells may die, or they may
grow in an uncontrolled way and start a cancer.

The size of the hazard depends on two things: how ionising the radiation is, and
whether the source is inside or outside the body.

- **Alpha radiation** is the most strongly ionising, but it is stopped by paper
  or by the outer layer of skin. Outside the body it is a small hazard. Inside
  the body, it transfers a large amount of energy to a short range of living cells, so it
  is a serious hazard.
- **Beta radiation** penetrates further and is less ionising. It can pass into
  the body and can burn the skin.
- **Gamma radiation** is the least ionising, but it passes through the body. A
  strong gamma source outside the body is the main external hazard.

The **half-life** also matters. A source with a long half-life stays radioactive
for a long time, so it remains a hazard in the environment or in the body. A
source with a short half-life becomes less active quickly, which can make it
easier to work with and to dispose of.

## 4. Using radiation in medicine: exploring the body

A **tracer** is a small amount of a radioactive isotope that is taken into the
body. It moves through the body in the same way as the substance it is attached
to. A detector outside the body follows the radiation and builds up an image of
an internal organ.

A tracer should:

- emit **gamma** radiation, because gamma rays pass out through the body and can
  be detected outside it;
- have a **short half-life**, so that the activity falls to a low level soon
  after the scan and the patient receives a small dose.

An alpha or beta emitter would be stopped inside the body, so its radiation
could not reach a detector outside.

## 5. Using radiation in medicine: treating tissue

A strong beam of radiation can also be used to **control or destroy unwanted
tissue**, such as a tumour. The radiation damages the cells in the target tissue
and stops them dividing.

The beam is aimed carefully from several directions, so that the target tissue
receives a large dose while the healthy tissue around it receives as small a
dose as possible. This use needs a larger dose than a tracer scan, because the
aim is to damage the cells.

## 6. Choosing the right source

The best source for a job depends on what the radiation has to pass through and
what the detector has to measure.

| Use | Best radiation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Tracer to image an organ | Gamma | Passes out through the body to a detector |
| Treatment of a tumour | Gamma or beta | Strongly ionising, so it damages the target cells |

> **Watch out.** Irradiation does not make an object radioactive. An irradiated
> object only receives radiation; when the source is taken away, it is not
> radioactive itself. Contamination is different, because radioactive atoms are
> left on or in the material. Check by asking: has any radioactive material been
> left behind, or has radiation only passed through?

## 8. Weighing risk against benefit

A medical use of radiation gives a patient a dose, which carries a small risk.
The benefit is a diagnosis or a treatment that can be much more valuable. When
you evaluate a use, give both sides.

- **Benefit:** the scan can find a problem early, or the treatment can destroy
  unwanted tissue.
- **Risk:** the dose can damage healthy cells, and in the long term it can cause
  a cancer.
- **Judgement:** describe how the dose is kept small, using a short half-life or
  a carefully aimed beam. Then say whether the benefit is worth the risk, giving
  a reason.

## You should now be able to

- name natural and man-made sources of background radiation;
- describe how location and occupation change the radiation dose a person
  receives;
- correct a measured count rate for background radiation;
- describe the hazards of alpha, beta and gamma radiation, inside and outside the
  body;
- explain how half-life affects the hazard of a source;
- describe how tracers and beams of radiation are used in medicine;
- evaluate the risks and the benefits of a medical use of radiation.
