# 9.3 Hazards and uses of radioactive emissions and background radiation — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check.

## Warm-up: remember

Answer these from memory. They use ideas from Section 9.2.

1. After one half-life, what fraction of the original count rate remains?
2. Which type of radiation is stopped by a sheet of paper?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. One half of the original count rate remains.
2. Alpha radiation is stopped by paper.

If question 1 went wrong, look again at the definition of half-life in Section
9.2. If question 2 went wrong, look at the table of penetration in Section 9.2.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Name two natural sources and two man-made sources of background radiation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Natural sources have always been present. Man-made sources began in the last
century.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

For the natural pair, name one source from the ground and one from space. For the
man-made pair, name one from weapon tests and one from an accident.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Natural sources: radioactive rocks and soil, and cosmic rays from space.
Man-made sources: fallout from nuclear weapon tests, and releases from nuclear
accidents.
</details>

**2.** Explain how a person's location and occupation can change the radiation
dose they receive in a year.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Location affects the rocks and the height above sea level. Occupation affects how
close a person works to a source.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Give one example of a place that gives a larger dose, and one job that gives a
larger dose.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Some rocks contain more radioactive atoms than others. A person who lives in an
area with more radon receives a larger dose. A person who lives high above sea
level receives more cosmic rays. For occupation, a radiographer or a nuclear
worker spends time near sources, so receives a larger dose than an office worker.
</details>

**3.** A detector measures 95 counts per minute near a source. The background
count rate is 15 counts per minute. Calculate the corrected count rate from the
source.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The detector measures the source and the background together.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Subtract the background count rate from the measured count rate.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ 95 - 15 = 80 \ \text{counts per minute} $$
</details>

**4.** Compare the hazard of an alpha source inside the body with the hazard of a
gamma source outside the body.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about how far each type of radiation travels, and how strongly it ionises
the material it passes through.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say which one is more strongly ionising, then say where it would need to be to
become a serious hazard.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Alpha radiation is strongly ionising but has a short range. Outside the body it
is stopped by the skin, so it is a small hazard. Inside the body it transfers a
lot of energy to a short range of living cells, so it is a serious hazard.

Gamma radiation is weakly ionising but has a long range. Outside the body it can
pass through the body and reach living cells, so a strong gamma source is a
serious external hazard.
</details>

**5.** A hospital must choose between two isotopes for a tracer. Isotope A emits
gamma radiation and has a half-life of 6 hours. Isotope B emits alpha radiation
and has a half-life of 20 years. State which isotope is suitable, and give two
reasons.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The radiation must reach a detector outside the body. The patient should not stay
radioactive for a long time.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Choose the isotope whose radiation escapes the body, then say why its half-life
is suitable.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Isotope A is suitable. Its gamma radiation passes out through the body, so a
detector outside the body can measure it. Isotope B emits alpha radiation, which
would be stopped inside the body. Isotope A also has a short half-life of a few
hours, so its activity falls quickly after the scan and the patient receives a
small dose. Isotope B would leave the patient radioactive for many years.
</details>

**6.** Explain why a radioactive source with a long half-life is a greater
contamination hazard than one with a short half-life.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about how long the contaminating atoms keep decaying.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say what happens to the activity of the short-half-life source after a few
half-lives, then compare it with the long-half-life source.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

A source with a long half-life keeps decaying for a long time, so the
contamination stays radioactive for years. A source with a short half-life falls
to a very low activity after a few half-lives, so the hazard does not last as
long.
</details>

**7.** A patient is offered a scan with a radioactive tracer. Describe the benefit
and the risk of the scan, and say whether the benefit is worth the risk.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The benefit is what the scan finds. The risk comes from the dose of ionising
radiation.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write one sentence for the benefit, one for the risk, and one judgement that
gives a reason.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Benefit: the scan can show how an organ is working, and can find a problem that
other tests would miss, so the patient can be treated sooner. Risk: the tracer
gives the patient a dose of ionising radiation, which can damage cells and, in
the long term, cause a cancer. Judgement: the dose is kept small by choosing a
short half-life and a small amount of the isotope. For most patients the benefit
of finding a serious problem is much greater than the small risk, so the scan is
worth having.
</details>
