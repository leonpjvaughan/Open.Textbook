# Chapter 9: Review

## The chapter problem, solved

The hospital scanning unit uses a tracer with 43 protons and 56 neutrons, a
half-life of 6.0 hours and a starting count rate of 800 counts per second.

**1. The particles in the tracer atom.** The atomic number is the number of
protons.

$$ Z = 43 $$

The mass number counts protons and neutrons together.

$$ A = 43 + 56 = 99 $$

A neutral atom has the same number of electrons as protons, so it has 43
electrons.

**2. The count rate after 12 hours.** A sample is prepared at 08:00 and used at
20:00, which is 12 hours later.

$$ \frac{12}{6.0} = 2 \ \text{half-lives} $$

After two half-lives the count rate is

$$ \frac{800}{2^2} = 200 \ \text{counts per second} $$

**3. The count rate after 24 hours.** The stored sample is now four half-lives
old.

$$ \frac{24}{6.0} = 4 \ \text{half-lives} $$

$$ \frac{800}{2^4} = 50 \ \text{counts per second} $$

**4. The background correction.** A detector near the stored sample reads 70
counts per second. The background count rate is 20 counts per second, so the
count rate from the tracer alone is

$$ 70 - 20 = 50 \ \text{counts per second} $$

This agrees with the value predicted in part 3.

<!-- tier:h -->
**5. The net decline.** After four half-lives the fraction remaining is

$$ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \frac{1}{16} $$

The net decline is

$$ 1 - \frac{1}{16} = \frac{15}{16} $$

so the net decline is a ratio of $15:16$ of the original emission.
<!-- /tier:h -->

**6. Why the tracer is a gamma emitter with a short half-life.** Gamma radiation
passes out through the body, so a detector outside can measure it. An alpha or
beta emitter would be stopped before the radiation reached the detector. A
half-life of 6.0 hours is long enough to carry out the scan, but short enough
that the activity falls to a low level soon afterwards, so the patient receives
only a small dose.

**7. The hospital's electricity.** The reactor uses nuclear fission. A
uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei,
releasing two or three neutrons, gamma rays and energy. The chain reaction is
controlled so that, on average, only one neutron from each fission causes the
next, so the energy is released at a steady rate.

**8. Is the scan worth its risk?** The scan can find a problem that other tests
would miss, so the patient can be treated sooner. The tracer gives a dose of
ionising radiation, which carries a small risk of damaging cells. The dose is kept
small by choosing a short half-life and a small amount of the isotope. This final
judgement is a reasoned answer. A computer cannot check it, so compare your
reasoning with the evidence above.

## What you can now do

- describe the structure of an atom and the particles inside it;
- state the atomic number, the mass number and the number of electrons of an atom
  or an ion;
- explain what isotopes are, and describe how the model of the atom changed;
- describe alpha, beta and gamma radiation, and write balanced decay equations;
- explain what half-life means, and use it to find a count rate or an activity;
- compare contamination with irradiation, and describe the hazard of each;
- describe background radiation, and explain why the dose a person receives
  varies;
- describe and evaluate the medical uses of nuclear radiation;
- describe nuclear fission and nuclear fusion, and explain where each happens.
