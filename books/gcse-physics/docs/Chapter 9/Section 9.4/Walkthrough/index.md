# 9.4 Nuclear fission and fusion — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 9.2, especially the nucleus and the neutron.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe nuclear fission and the chain
reaction it can start; explain how the chain reaction is controlled in a reactor;
describe nuclear fusion; and compare fission with fusion.

## 1. Nuclear fission

**Nuclear fission** is the splitting of a large and unstable nucleus, such as a
nucleus of uranium or plutonium.

Spontaneous fission is rare. Usually, for fission to happen, the unstable nucleus
must first absorb a **neutron**. The nucleus then splits into two smaller nuclei
that are roughly equal in size. The fission releases:

- two or three neutrons;
- gamma rays;
- a large amount of energy.

The two smaller nuclei and the neutrons move away at high speed. They carry away
the released energy in their kinetic store.

The equation below shows one fission of uranium-235.

$$ ^{235}_{92}\text{U} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow {}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} $$

**Worked example 1.** Check that the mass numbers and the atomic numbers balance
in the fission equation above.

Mass numbers: on the left, $235 + 1 = 236$. On the right,
$141 + 92 + 3 = 236$.

Atomic numbers: on the left, 92. On the right, $56 + 36 = 92$.

Both balance, so the equation is correct.

## 2. A chain reaction

Each fission releases two or three neutrons. Those neutrons can be absorbed by
other uranium-235 nuclei, and each of those nuclei can fission in turn. This
keeps the reaction going and is called a **chain reaction**.

If each fission releases three neutrons and every neutron causes one more
fission, then the number of fissions grows quickly.

| Generation after the first fission | Neutrons released |
|---|---|
| First generation | 3 |
| Second generation | 9 |
| Third generation | 27 |

**Worked example 2.** Each fission releases 3 neutrons. Calculate the number of
neutrons released in the third generation after the first fission.

The first fission releases 3 neutrons. Each neutron causes one more fission, so
the next generation releases $3 \times 3 = 9$ neutrons. The third generation
releases

$$ 3^3 = 27 \ \text{neutrons} $$

## 3. Controlling the chain reaction

If every neutron caused another fission, the number of fissions would double or
triple at every step. The energy released would grow out of control in a fraction
of a second. This is what happens in a nuclear weapon.

In a **nuclear reactor**, the chain reaction is controlled. On average, only one
neutron from each fission goes on to cause the next fission. The energy is then
released at a steady rate that can be used to make electricity.

> **Watch out.** Fission and fusion are opposite processes, and their names sound
> alike. Fission **splits** a large nucleus. Fusion **joins** two light nuclei.
> A nuclear power station uses fission, not fusion. Check by asking whether the
> nucleus at the start is large and split, or small and joined.

## 4. Nuclear fusion

**Nuclear fusion** is the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus.
In the process, some of the mass of the nuclei is converted into the energy of
radiation.

Fusion happens naturally in the core of a star, including the Sun. The very high
temperature gives the light nuclei enough energy to move fast and join, even
though their positive charges make them repel each other. It is difficult to
reach and to hold such a high temperature on the Earth, so fusion is not yet used
to generate electricity.

**Worked example 3.** Compare nuclear fission and nuclear fusion. Give one thing
they have in common and one important difference.

Both reactions release a large amount of energy from the nucleus. In fission a
large nucleus splits into smaller nuclei. In fusion two light nuclei join to form
a heavier nucleus.

## 5. Fission and fusion side by side

| | Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | A large nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei | Two light nuclei join into one heavier nucleus |
| What starts it | Absorbing a neutron (usually) | Very high temperature and pressure |
| Energy | Released | Released |
| Where it is used or found | A nuclear reactor in a power station | The core of a star |
