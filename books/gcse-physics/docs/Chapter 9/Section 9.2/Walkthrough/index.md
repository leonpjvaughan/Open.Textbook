# 9.2 Atoms and nuclear radiation — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 9.1, especially the particles in the nucleus and the
meaning of the mass number and the atomic number.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe radioactive decay and the
radiation it emits; write balanced nuclear equations; explain what half-life
means and use it to find a count rate; and compare contamination with
irradiation.

## 1. Unstable nuclei and radioactive decay

Some nuclei are unstable. An unstable nucleus gives out radiation as it changes
to become more stable. This is called **radioactive decay**.

Radioactive decay is a **random** process. You cannot predict which nucleus will
decay next, or when it will happen. The decay of one nucleus does not change the
chance that another nucleus will decay.

## 2. Activity and count rate

**Activity** is the rate at which the unstable nuclei in a source decay. It is
measured in **becquerels** (Bq), where one becquerel is one decay each second.

A detector, such as a Geiger-Muller tube, records a **count rate**: the number of
decays it detects each second. Some radiation is absorbed before it reaches the
detector, so the count rate is usually smaller than the activity.

## 3. The radiation a nucleus can emit

A nucleus can emit four kinds of nuclear radiation.

| Radiation | What it is | Stopped by | Range in air | Ionising power |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha ($\alpha$) | Two protons and two neutrons; the same as a helium nucleus | Paper or skin | A few centimetres | Strongest |
| Beta ($\beta$) | A fast electron ejected from the nucleus | A few millimetres of aluminium | About a metre | Weaker than alpha |
| Gamma ($\gamma$) | Electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus | Thick lead or concrete | Very long; passes through the body | Weakest |

A nucleus can also emit a **neutron**. You will meet neutrons again in Section
9.4, where they keep a chain reaction going.

The ionising power matters. Ionising radiation can knock electrons away from the
atoms it passes through, which can damage living cells. Alpha particles are the
most strongly ionising, but they are stopped quickly. Gamma rays are the least
ionising, but they penetrate the furthest.

## 4. Nuclear equations

A nuclear equation shows the change in the nucleus. Two rules always hold:

- the **mass numbers** on the two sides must balance;
- the **atomic numbers** on the two sides must balance.

An alpha particle is written $^{4}_{2}\alpha$, and a beta particle is written
$^{0}_{-1}\beta$.

In **alpha decay**, the nucleus loses two protons and two neutrons, so the mass
number falls by 4 and the atomic number falls by 2. In **beta decay**, a neutron
turns into a proton, so the mass number does not change and the atomic number
goes up by 1. A gamma ray has no mass and no charge, so it changes neither
number.

**Worked example 1.** Radon-222 decays by alpha emission. Write the balanced
nuclear equation.

Radon-222 is $^{222}_{86}\text{Rn}$. The mass number falls by 4 to give 218, and
the atomic number falls by 2 to give 84.

$$ ^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} \rightarrow {}^{218}_{84}\text{Po} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha $$

**Worked example 2.** Carbon-14 decays by beta emission. Write the balanced
nuclear equation.

Carbon-14 is $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$. The mass number does not change, and the atomic
number goes up by 1 to give 7.

$$ ^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\beta $$

## 5. Half-life

The **half-life** of a radioactive isotope is the time it takes for:

- the number of nuclei of the isotope in a sample to halve; or
- the count rate from a sample to fall to half of its starting level.

**Worked example 3.** A source has a count rate of 400 counts per second. The
count rate is measured every 2 minutes: 400, 200, 100, 50. Work out the
half-life.

The count rate halves from 400 to 200 in 2 minutes, and then from 200 to 100 in
the next 2 minutes. The half-life is 2 minutes.

The half-life is a statistical average. It describes a very large number of
nuclei. A single measurement will not fall to exactly half, because the decay is
random.

**Worked example 4.** A tracer has a half-life of 6.0 hours and an initial count
rate of 800 counts per second. Calculate the count rate after 12 hours.

$$ \frac{12}{6.0} = 2 \ \text{half-lives} $$

After one half-life the count rate is 400 counts per second. After two
half-lives it is 200 counts per second.

$$ \text{count rate} = \frac{800}{2^2} = 200 \ \text{counts per second} $$

## 6. Net decline after a number of half-lives

Sometimes a question gives the number of half-lives and asks for the **net
decline** in the emission, written as a ratio.

After $n$ half-lives, the fraction of the original activity that remains is

$$ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n} $$

so the net decline is $1 - \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}$.

**Worked example 5.** After three half-lives, calculate the net decline in the
emission as a ratio.

After three half-lives the fraction remaining is
$\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \dfrac{1}{8}$.

$$ \text{net decline} = 1 - \frac{1}{8} = \frac{7}{8} $$

The net decline is a ratio of $7:8$ of the original emission.

## 7. Contamination and irradiation

Two words describe two different hazards.

- **Contamination** is the unwanted presence of radioactive atoms on or in
  another material. The contaminating atoms keep decaying, so the material
  itself gives out radiation.
- **Irradiation** is exposing an object to nuclear radiation. The irradiated
  object does **not** become radioactive, because the radiation passes through
  it or is absorbed.

Alpha contamination inside the body is very serious, because alpha particles
have a short range and are strongly ionising. In that short range, they transfer
a large amount of energy to living cells. Gamma irradiation from a source outside the
body is the opposite case: gamma rays penetrate the body, so they can reach
living cells from outside.

> **Watch out.** After two half-lives the source has not gone. One half-life
> leaves one half of the activity, two half-lives leave one quarter, and three
> half-lives leave one eighth. The activity keeps halving and never reaches zero
> in a fixed time. Re-check by halving the starting value once for each
> half-life, rather than stopping early.
