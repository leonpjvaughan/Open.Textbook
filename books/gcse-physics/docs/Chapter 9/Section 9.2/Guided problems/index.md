# 9.2 Atoms and nuclear radiation — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check.

## Warm-up: remember

Answer these from memory. They use ideas from Section 9.1 and earlier work on
powers and decimals.

1. How many protons are in an atom with atomic number 20?
2. Work out $2^4$.
3. Write $\tfrac{1}{8}$ as a decimal.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. 20 protons.
2. $2^4 = 16$.
3. $0.125$.

If question 1 went wrong, look again at the atomic number in Section 9.1. If
question 2 went wrong, remember that a power tells you how many copies to
multiply. If question 3 went wrong, divide 1 by 8.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** Define activity, and state the unit in which it is measured.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Activity is a rate: it counts decays in each second.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say what is decaying, and how often. Then name the unit and what one unit means.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Activity is the rate at which the unstable nuclei in a source decay. It is
measured in becquerels (Bq). One becquerel is one decay each second.
</details>

**2.** Describe an alpha particle, a beta particle and a gamma ray, and say what
stops each one.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One of the three has no mass and no charge at all.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say what each particle is made of, then match it to the material that stops it:
paper, aluminium or thick lead.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

An alpha particle is two protons and two neutrons, the same as a helium nucleus.
It is stopped by paper or skin. A beta particle is a fast electron ejected from
the nucleus. It is stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. A gamma ray is
electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus, with no mass and no charge. It is
reduced by thick lead or concrete.
</details>

**3.** A nucleus of radon-222, $^{222}_{86}\text{Rn}$, emits an alpha particle.
Write the balanced nuclear equation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

An alpha particle is $^{4}_{2}\alpha$, so the mass number falls by 4 and the
atomic number falls by 2.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out the new mass number $222 - 4$ and the new atomic number $86 - 2$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ ^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} \rightarrow {}^{218}_{84}\text{Po} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha $$

The mass number is $222 - 4 = 218$, and the atomic number is $86 - 2 = 84$.
</details>

**4.** A nucleus of carbon-14, $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$, emits a beta particle. Write
the balanced nuclear equation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A beta particle is $^{0}_{-1}\beta$, so the mass number does not change.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Keep the mass number at 14, and work out the new atomic number $6 + 1$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$ ^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\beta $$

The mass number stays at 14, and the atomic number goes up by 1 to give 7.
</details>

**5.** A source has a count rate of 320 counts per second. After 12 minutes the
count rate is 40 counts per second. Work out the half-life.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Count how many times the count rate must halve to get from 320 to 40.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Halve step by step: 320, then 160, then 80, then 40. Count the steps.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$320 \rightarrow 160 \rightarrow 80 \rightarrow 40$ is three halvings, so three
half-lives have passed in 12 minutes. The half-life is
$\dfrac{12}{3} = 4.0$ minutes.
</details>

**6.** Explain why the count rate from a source is only roughly halved after one
half-life.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about whether you can say which nucleus decays next.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say what kind of process decay is, then say what the half-life describes.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Radioactive decay is random. You cannot predict which nucleus will decay next.
The half-life is the average time for half of the nuclei in a large sample to
decay, so a real measurement only follows this average roughly.
</details>

<!-- tier:h -->
**7.** After four half-lives, calculate the net decline in the emission as a
ratio.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Work out the fraction that remains after four halvings, then subtract it from 1.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Write the fraction remaining as $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^4$, then work out
$1 - \dfrac{1}{16}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The fraction remaining is $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \dfrac{1}{16}$. The net
decline is

$$ 1 - \frac{1}{16} = \frac{15}{16} $$

so the net decline is a ratio of $15:16$ of the original emission.
</details>
