# 2.4 Pressure and pressure differences in fluids — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 2.1, especially forces and the unit newton; Section
2.3, work done; the area of a rectangle; changing between square centimetres and
square metres.

**By the end of this section you can:** explain what pressure means in a fluid;
calculate pressure, force or area; describe atmospheric pressure and why it
changes with height; and, at Higher tier, calculate the pressure at a depth in a
liquid and explain upthrust.

## 1. Pressure in a fluid

A **fluid** is a liquid or a gas. The particles of a fluid move around and
collide with anything in contact with the fluid. The pressure in a fluid causes a
force **normal** to any surface, which means at right angles to the surface.

**Pressure** is the force normal to a surface divided by the area of that
surface.

$$ p = \frac{F}{A} $$

where $p$ is the pressure in pascals, $F$ is the force normal to the surface in
newtons, and $A$ is the area of the surface in square metres.

One pascal is one newton per square metre.

$$ 1\,\text{Pa} = 1\,\text{N/m}^2 $$

A large pressure means a large force on a small area. This is why a sharp knife
cuts well: the same push acts on a very small area.

**Worked example 1.** A force of 300 N acts on an area of 0.015 m². Calculate the
pressure.

$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= \frac{F}{A} \\
  &= \frac{300}{0.015} \\
  &= 20000\,\text{Pa} \\
  &= 2.0 \times 10^4\,\text{Pa}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 2.** A pressure of 8000 Pa acts on an area of 0.25 m².
Calculate the force normal to the surface.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= p A \\
  &= 8000 \times 0.25 \\
  &= 2000\,\text{N} \\
  &= 2.0 \times 10^3\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 3.** A force of 900 N produces a pressure of 30000 Pa.
Calculate the area.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{F}{p} \\
  &= \frac{900}{30000} \\
  &= 0.030\,\text{m}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Watch out.** The area must be in **square metres** before you use $p = F/A$.
> One square metre is $10000\,\text{cm}^2$, so a contact area of $12\,\text{cm}^2$
> is $12 \div 10000 = 0.0012\,\text{m}^2$. Dividing by 100 instead of 10000 gives
> a pressure 100 times too large. Check that your area is much smaller than a
> square metre before you divide.

## 2. Atmospheric pressure

The **atmosphere** is a thin layer of air around the Earth. The air is made of
molecules that move around and collide with surfaces. Each collision pushes, and
the total push is the **atmospheric pressure**.

The atmosphere gets less dense as you go higher, and there is less air above a
high surface. So the number of molecules above a surface, and the weight of the
air above it, both decrease as the height increases. Fewer collisions give a
smaller pressure, so atmospheric pressure **decreases** as you go higher.

## 3. Pressure at a depth in a liquid

The pressure due to a column of liquid depends on how deep you are and how dense
the liquid is.

$$ p = h \rho g $$

where $h$ is the height of the column in metres, $\rho$ (rho) is the **density**
of the liquid in kilograms per cubic metre, and $g$ is the gravitational field
strength in newtons per kilogram. Density is the mass of one cubic metre of the
substance.

**Worked example 4.** Calculate the pressure due to a column of water 5.0 m high.
The density of water is 1000 kg/m³ and $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= h \rho g \\
  &= 5.0 \times 1000 \times 9.8 \\
  &= 49000\,\text{Pa} \\
  &= 4.9 \times 10^4\,\text{Pa}
\end{aligned}
$$

The pressure at a point in a liquid increases with the height of liquid above
that point, and with the density of the liquid. To find the **difference** in
pressure between two depths, use the difference in depth as $h$.

**Worked example 5.** Calculate the pressure difference between depths of 1.0 m
and 3.0 m in water of density 1000 kg/m³. Take $g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
h &= 3.0 - 1.0 = 2.0\,\text{m} \\
p &= h \rho g = 2.0 \times 1000 \times 9.8 \\
  &= 19600\,\text{Pa} \\
  &= 2.0 \times 10^4\,\text{Pa}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Upthrust and floating

A submerged object has a greater pressure on its **bottom** surface than on its
**top** surface, because the bottom is deeper. The upward force on the bottom is
therefore larger than the downward force on the top. The resultant upward force
is called **upthrust**.

An object floats if its average density is less than the density of the liquid,
and sinks if its average density is greater. A block of wood averages less than
water, so it floats. A block of steel averages more than water, so it sinks.
