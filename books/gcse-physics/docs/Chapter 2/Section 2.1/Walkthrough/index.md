# 2.1 Forces and their interactions — Learn

**Prerequisites:** multiplying and dividing numbers; substituting numbers into a
formula; the units kilogram, metre and newton; the idea of a gravitational store
from Chapter 1.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe a force as an interaction
between two objects; sort forces into contact and non-contact forces; represent
a force as a vector; calculate weight from mass; and find the resultant of two
forces that act in a straight line.

## 1. A force is a push or a pull

A **force** is a push or a pull that acts on an object because of an interaction
with another object. Forces come in pairs. If you push a wall, the wall pushes
back on you.

A force is measured in **newtons** (N). You measure a force with a
**newtonmeter**, which is a spring balance with a scale.

## 2. Contact and non-contact forces

Forces are sorted into two groups.

- **Contact forces** act when the objects touch.
- **Non-contact forces** act when the objects are apart.

| Contact force | What it does |
|---|---|
| Friction | Opposes sliding between two surfaces. |
| Air resistance | Opposes motion through the air. |
| Tension | A pull along a rope, cable or string. |
| Normal contact force | A push at right angles to a surface. |

| Non-contact force | What it does |
|---|---|
| Gravitational force | Pulls objects towards each other. |
| Electrostatic force | Acts between electric charges. |
| Magnetic force | Acts between magnets, or between a magnet and a magnetic material. |

## 3. Forces are vectors

A **vector** is a quantity that has both a size and a direction. Force is a
vector. Mass and speed have a size only, so they are **scalars**.

We draw a force as an arrow. The length of the arrow shows the size of the
force, and the arrow points in the direction of the force.

## 4. Mass and weight

**Mass** is the amount of matter in an object. It is measured in kilograms (kg)
and does not change when the object moves to another place.

**Weight** is the force of gravity on an object. It is a force, so it is measured
in newtons (N). The weight of an object depends on the **gravitational field
strength**, $g$, at that place. On the Earth, $g$ is about $9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

$$ W = m g $$

where $W$ is the weight in newtons, $m$ is the mass in kilograms and $g$ is the
gravitational field strength in newtons per kilogram.

Weight is measured with a newtonmeter. The weight of an object acts at a single
point, called the **centre of mass**. The weight and the mass of an object are
directly proportional, so a graph of weight against mass is a straight line
through the origin.

**Worked example 1.** A pupil of mass 70 kg stands on the ground. Take
$g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$. Calculate the weight of the pupil.

$$
\begin{aligned}
W &= m g \\
  &= 70 \times 9.8 \\
  &= 686\,\text{N} \\
  &= 690\,\text{N} \quad (2\ \text{significant figures})
\end{aligned}
$$

## 5. Resultant forces

Several forces can act on the same object. A single force that has the same
effect as all the others together is called the **resultant force**.

When the forces act in a straight line, you can combine them.

- Forces in the **same direction** add together.
- Forces in **opposite directions** subtract.
- If the forces are equal and opposite, the resultant force is **zero**. The
  forces are balanced.

**Worked example 2.** A force of 250 N acts to the right and a force of 100 N
acts to the left. Calculate the resultant force.

The forces act in opposite directions, so subtract.

$$ 250 - 100 = 150\,\text{N} $$

The resultant force is 150 N to the right.

**Worked example 3.** Two forces of 35 N and 50 N act in the same direction.
Calculate the resultant force.

The forces act in the same direction, so add.

$$ 35 + 50 = 85\,\text{N} $$

> **Watch out.** You can add and subtract forces only when they act along the
> same straight line. Two forces at an angle do not add in the same way. A force
> of 3 N and a force of 4 N at right angles give a resultant of 5 N, not 7 N. Use a
> vector diagram, and see the Higher tier part below.
> **Re-check:** draw the two forces tip to tail and measure the resultant on the diagram.

## Resolving a force into two components

A single force can be split into two components that act at right angles. The
two components together have the same effect as the single force.

To find the components of a force $F$ at an angle $\theta$ to the horizontal:

$$ F_{\text{horizontal}} = F \cos\theta \qquad F_{\text{vertical}} = F \sin\theta $$

**Worked example 4.** A force of 50 N acts at $30^\circ$ to the horizontal.
Resolve the force into a horizontal component and a vertical component.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{\text{horizontal}} &= 50 \cos 30^\circ = 43.3\,\text{N} \\
F_{\text{vertical}}   &= 50 \sin 30^\circ = 25.0\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Free body diagrams

A **free body diagram** shows one object on its own, with arrows for every force
that acts on it. The arrows show the direction and the relative size of each
force.

For an object that is not moving, or is moving at a constant speed, the forces
are balanced, so the resultant force is zero. A lift hanging still has its weight
pulling down and the tension in the cable pulling up. The two forces are equal
and opposite.

### Vector diagrams

A **vector diagram** is a scale drawing of two or more forces. Draw the arrows
tip to tail, keeping their directions. The arrow from the start of the first
force to the tip of the last force is the resultant. You can use a vector diagram
to find the resultant of two forces that are not in a straight line, and to find
a missing force when three forces are in equilibrium.

**Worked example 5.** Two forces of 30 N and 40 N act at right angles. Calculate
the magnitude and direction of the resultant force.

$$
\begin{aligned}
R &= \sqrt{30^2 + 40^2} = 50\,\text{N} \\
\tan\theta &= \frac{40}{30} \qquad \theta = 53^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

The resultant is 50 N, at $53^\circ$ to the 30 N force.
