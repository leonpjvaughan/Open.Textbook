# 2.2 Forces and elasticity — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 2.1, especially forces, newtonmeters and the unit
newton; substituting numbers into a formula.

**By the end of this section you can:** explain why more than one force is needed
to change the shape of an object; describe elastic and inelastic deformation; use
the force-extension relationship for a spring; and calculate the energy stored in
a stretched spring.

## 1. Changing the shape of an object

To stretch, bend or squeeze an object, you must apply **more than one force**.
For a stationary object the forces are equal and opposite, so they squash or
stretch the object instead of moving it. A spring stretched by your hands has a
pull at each end. A sponge squeezed by your fingers has a push at each side.

## 2. Elastic and inelastic deformation

When the forces are removed, the object may or may not go back to its original
shape.

- **Elastic deformation** happens when the object returns to its original shape
  and size.
- **Inelastic deformation** happens when the object stays changed. It does not
  return to its original shape.

A rubber band stretched a little springs back, so the change is elastic. A paper
clip bent out of shape stays bent, so the change is inelastic.

## 3. Force and extension

The **extension** of a spring is how much longer it becomes. It is measured in
metres. Push a spring instead and it gets shorter; the **compression** is how
much shorter it becomes.

For a spring, the extension is directly proportional to the force applied, as
long as the spring is not stretched too far. Directly proportional means that
doubling the force doubles the extension.

$$ F = k e $$

where $F$ is the force in newtons, $k$ is the **spring constant** in newtons per
metre, and $e$ is the extension in metres. The spring constant tells you how
stiff the spring is. A larger spring constant means a stiffer spring.

**Worked example 1.** A spring has a spring constant of 320 N/m and is stretched
by 0.15 m. Calculate the force applied.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= k e \\
  &= 320 \times 0.15 \\
  &= 48\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 2.** A force of 20 N stretches a spring by 0.080 m. Calculate
the spring constant.

$$
\begin{aligned}
k &= \frac{F}{e} \\
  &= \frac{20}{0.080} \\
  &= 250\,\text{N/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 3.** A spring has a spring constant of 120 N/m. A force of 36 N
is applied. Calculate the extension.

$$
\begin{aligned}
e &= \frac{F}{k} \\
  &= \frac{36}{120} \\
  &= 0.30\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 4. The limit of proportionality

The straight-line relationship between force and extension holds only up to the
**limit of proportionality**. Beyond that point, the line curves. The extension
is then no longer proportional to the force, and the spring is inelastically
deformed.

A graph of force against extension shows the pattern. Up to the limit of
proportionality it is a straight line through the origin. Beyond the limit the
line curves upwards, because each extra newton gives a larger extra extension.

> **Watch out.** The extension is the **increase in length**, not the total
> length. A spring that is 12 cm long and stretches to 15 cm has an extension of
> 3 cm, which is 0.030 m. Put every length in metres before you use $F = ke$, and
> do not add the natural length to the extension.

## 5. Energy in a stretched spring

A force that stretches a spring does work on it. If the spring is not
inelastically deformed, all of that work is stored in the **elastic potential
store**. The energy stored is

$$ E_e = \tfrac{1}{2} k e^2 $$

where $k$ is the spring constant in newtons per metre and $e$ is the extension in
metres. The extension is squared, so doubling the extension gives **four times**
the energy.

**Worked example 4.** A spring has a spring constant of 200 N/m and is stretched
by 0.20 m. Calculate the energy stored in the elastic potential store.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_e &= \tfrac{1}{2} k e^2 \\
    &= 0.5 \times 200 \times 0.20^2 \\
    &= 4.0\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

The same expression gives the work done in stretching the spring, as long as the
spring is not stretched past its limit of proportionality.
