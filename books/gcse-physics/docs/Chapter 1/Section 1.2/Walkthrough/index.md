# 1.2 Conservation, dissipation and energy resources — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 1.1, especially energy stores, power and the idea that
energy is transferred rather than used up.

**By the end of this section you can:** state the principle of conservation of
energy for a closed system; explain what dissipation means; describe how to
reduce unwanted energy transfer; calculate the efficiency of an energy transfer;
and compare the energy resources used on the Earth.

## 1. Conservation of energy

A **closed system** is a system that no energy can enter or leave. In a closed
system, the total energy stored never changes. This is the **principle of
conservation of energy**.

Energy is transferred between stores inside the system, but the total stays the
same. A ball dropped in a vacuum, with no air resistance, loses gravitational
potential energy at exactly the rate at which it gains kinetic energy.

**Worked example 1.** A ball falls from a ledge. At the ledge it has 24 J in its
gravitational potential store. Air resistance is ignored. State the energy in its
kinetic store just before it lands, and explain your answer.

The total energy is unchanged, so the 24 J that leaves the gravitational
potential store enters the kinetic store. Just before landing,

$$ E_k = 24\,\text{J} $$

In a real room there is air resistance. The ball then has a little less kinetic
energy, because some energy has been transferred to the thermal store of the air.

## 2. Dissipation

In every real change, some energy is transferred to the surroundings in a way
that is not useful. This is **dissipation**. Dissipated energy is often stored in
the thermal store of the surroundings, and it spreads out.

Dissipated energy has not been destroyed. It is still there, but it is stored in
a less useful way. Friction, air resistance and the heating of a wire are all
ways in which energy is dissipated.

**Worked example 2.** A machine is supplied with 2000 J of energy. It transfers
800 J usefully. How much energy is dissipated, and where does it go?

$$
\text{dissipated} = 2000 - 800 = 1200\,\text{J}
$$

The 1200 J is transferred to the thermal store of the machine and the air. It
spreads out and becomes hard to use.

## 3. Reducing unwanted energy transfer

You can reduce unwanted transfer in two common ways.

- **Lubrication.** Oil between moving surfaces reduces friction, so less energy
  is transferred to the thermal store.
- **Thermal insulation.** A layer of trapped air or foam slows the transfer of
  energy from a warm object to its cooler surroundings.

For a building, the rate at which it cools depends on its walls. A **thicker**
wall transfers energy more slowly, so the building cools more slowly. A wall made
of a material with a **higher thermal conductivity** transfers energy faster, so
the building cools faster.

## 4. Efficiency

The **efficiency** of an energy transfer compares the useful output with the
total input.

$$ \text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output energy transfer}}{\text{total input energy transfer}} $$

Efficiency has no unit. It can be written as a decimal or as a percentage. A
transfer can never be more than 100% efficient, because the useful output cannot
be larger than the total input.

**Worked example 3.** A motor is supplied with 500 J of energy and transfers
350 J usefully. Calculate its efficiency.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{efficiency} &= \frac{350}{500} \\
                  &= 0.70 \\
                  &= 70\%
\end{aligned}
$$

The other 150 J is dissipated to the thermal store of the motor and the air.

**Worked example 4.** A machine is 40% efficient. Its useful output is 800 J.
Calculate the total input.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{efficiency} &= \frac{\text{useful output}}{\text{total input}} \\
0.40 &= \frac{800}{\text{total input}} \\
\text{total input} &= \frac{800}{0.40} \\
                   &= 2000\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<!-- tier:h -->
The efficiency of a transfer can be increased by reducing the energy that is
dissipated. A machine with moving parts can be lubricated. A hot pipe can be
wrapped in insulation. A device can be designed so that less energy is
transferred to the surroundings. Each of these makes the useful output a larger
share of the same input.
<!-- /tier:h -->

> **Watch out.** Efficiency compares the useful output with the **total input**,
> not with the waste. A transfer that gives 350 J usefully from 500 J has an
> efficiency of $350 \div 500 = 0.70$, not $350 \div 150$. As a percentage it is
> 70%, not 0.70%. If you work out a value above 1, or above 100%, you have
> divided by the wrong number. Re-check by comparing your useful output with the
> total input.

## 5. Energy resources

The main energy resources available on the Earth are:

| Resource | Renewable? |
|---|---|
| Fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas) | No |
| Nuclear fuel | No |
| Bio-fuel | Yes |
| Wind | Yes |
| Hydroelectricity | Yes |
| Geothermal | Yes |
| The tides | Yes |
| The Sun | Yes |
| Water waves | Yes |

A **renewable** resource is one that is replaced as it is used, so it will not
run out. A **non-renewable** resource is used up faster than it is replaced.

Energy resources are used for transport, for generating electricity and for
heating. Different resources suit different uses. Oil suits transport because it
can be carried in a tank and stored until it is needed. The Sun and wind suit
electricity generation because the fuel costs nothing.

Resources differ in reliability. Wind does not always blow, and the Sun does not
always shine, so these resources are not always available. Fossil fuels and
nuclear fuel can be used at any time, so they are more reliable. Resources also
differ in their effect on the environment: burning fossil fuels adds carbon
dioxide to the air, and wind farms change the look of a landscape.

The mix of resources used has changed over time. In many countries the share
from renewable resources has grown, and the share from coal has fallen.
Renewable resources do not run out, and they produce less carbon dioxide when
they generate electricity.
