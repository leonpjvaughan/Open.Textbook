# 1.1 Energy stores and transfers — Learn

**Prerequisites:** multiplying and dividing numbers; using a formula by
substitution; the units kilogram, metre, second, joule and watt.

**By the end of this section you can:** name the energy stores; describe how
energy moves between them; and calculate the energy in the kinetic,
gravitational potential, elastic potential and thermal stores, plus the power of
an energy transfer.

## 1. Systems and energy stores

A **system** is an object, or a group of objects, that we choose to study. A
falling ball is a system. So is a kettle and the water inside it.

Energy is stored in a system in several ways. Each way is called an **energy
store**. These are the stores you need to know.

| Store | What it is |
|---|---|
| Kinetic | The energy of a moving object. |
| Gravitational potential | The energy an object has because of its height above the ground. |
| Elastic potential | The energy stored in a stretched or squashed spring. |
| Thermal | The energy of the particles in a warm object. |
| Chemical | The energy stored in food, fuel and batteries. |
| Nuclear | The energy stored inside the nucleus of an atom. |
| Magnetic | The energy stored between magnets. |
| Electrostatic | The energy stored between electric charges. |

Energy is **transferred** from one store to another. It is never created and it
is never destroyed. This is the **principle of conservation of energy**. You will
use it in Section 1.2.

## 2. How energy is transferred

There are three transfers you need to describe:

- **by heating**, from a hotter object to a cooler one;
- **by work done by forces**, when a force moves an object;
- **by work done when a current flows**, in an electric circuit.

A transfer is not a store. When a battery lights a lamp, chemical energy is
transferred electrically to the thermal store of the lamp and its surroundings.

## 3. Describing a change in a system

To describe a change, name the store that changes, then say what it changes
into. Here are the examples the specification asks for.

- **An object projected upwards.** Chemical energy in the muscles is transferred
  to the kinetic store. As it rises, the kinetic store empties and the
  gravitational potential store fills.
- **A moving object hitting an obstacle.** The kinetic store empties. Energy is
  transferred to the thermal store of the object and the obstacle.
- **An object accelerated by a constant force.** Energy is transferred by the
  force to the kinetic store, which fills.
- **A vehicle slowing down.** The kinetic store empties. Brakes transfer the
  energy to the thermal store of the brakes and the air.
- **Bringing water to a boil in an electric kettle.** Energy is transferred
  electrically to the thermal store of the water and the kettle.

## 4. Gravitational potential energy

The energy in the gravitational potential store of an object raised above the
ground is

$$ E_p = m g h $$

where $m$ is the mass in kilograms, $g$ is the gravitational field strength in
newtons per kilogram, and $h$ is the height in metres. On the Earth, $g$ is about
$9.8\,\text{N/kg}$.

**Worked example 1.** A pupil of mass 65 kg climbs 4.0 m up a rope. Take
$g = 9.8\,\text{N/kg}$. Calculate the energy gained by the gravitational
potential store.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_p &= m g h \\
    &= 65 \times 9.8 \times 4.0 \\
    &= 2548\,\text{J} \\
    &= 2500\,\text{J} \quad (2\ \text{significant figures})
\end{aligned}
$$

## 5. Kinetic energy

The energy in the kinetic store of a moving object is

$$ E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 $$

where $m$ is the mass in kilograms and $v$ is the speed in metres per second.
The speed is **squared**.

**Worked example 2.** A car of mass 1200 kg travels at 15 m/s. Calculate the
energy in its kinetic store.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_k &= \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 \\
    &= 0.5 \times 1200 \times 15^2 \\
    &= 0.5 \times 1200 \times 225 \\
    &= 135000\,\text{J} \\
    &= 1.4 \times 10^5\,\text{J} \quad (2\ \text{significant figures})
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Watch out.** The speed is squared, so a car at 30 m/s has **four times** the
> kinetic energy of the same car at 15 m/s, not twice. Check the mass first:
> 500 g is 0.5 kg, not 500 kg. Re-check by asking whether the answer is bigger
> or smaller than the object's mass suggests.

## 6. Elastic potential energy

The energy stored in a stretched spring is

$$ E_e = \tfrac{1}{2} k e^2 $$

where $k$ is the spring constant in newtons per metre and $e$ is the extension
in metres. This equation holds while the spring is not stretched past its
**limit of proportionality**, the point beyond which the stretch is no longer
proportional to the force.

**Worked example 3.** A spring with spring constant 250 N/m is stretched by
0.080 m. Calculate the energy in the elastic potential store.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_e &= \tfrac{1}{2} k e^2 \\
    &= 0.5 \times 250 \times 0.080^2 \\
    &= 0.5 \times 250 \times 0.0064 \\
    &= 0.8\,\text{J} \\
    &= 0.80\,\text{J} \quad (2\ \text{significant figures})
\end{aligned}
$$

## 7. Energy transferred by heating

When a substance is heated, its thermal store fills. The energy transferred is

$$ \Delta E = m c \Delta\theta $$

where $m$ is the mass in kilograms, $c$ is the **specific heat capacity** and
$\Delta\theta$ is the temperature change in degrees Celsius.

The specific heat capacity of a substance is the energy needed to raise the
temperature of one kilogram of it by one degree Celsius. For water,
$c = 4200\,\text{J/kg°C}$. A large value means the substance needs a large amount of
energy to warm up.

**Worked example 4.** A 2.0 kg block of aluminium is heated from 18 °C to 68 °C.
The specific heat capacity of aluminium is 900 J/kg°C. Calculate the energy
transferred to the block.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta E &= m c \Delta\theta \\
         &= 2.0 \times 900 \times (68 - 18) \\
         &= 2.0 \times 900 \times 50 \\
         &= 90000\,\text{J} \\
         &= 9.0 \times 10^4\,\text{J} \quad (2\ \text{significant figures})
\end{aligned}
$$

## 8. Power

**Power** is the rate at which energy is transferred. It is measured in watts,
where one watt is one joule per second.

$$ P = \frac{E}{t} $$

where $P$ is the power in watts, $E$ is the energy transferred in joules and $t$
is the time in seconds.

**Worked example 5.** A heater transfers 90000 J in 45 s. Calculate its power.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P &= \frac{E}{t} \\
  &= \frac{90000}{45} \\
  &= 2000\,\text{W} \\
  &= 2.0\,\text{kW}
\end{aligned}
$$

A powerful appliance transfers the same energy in less time. A 2500 W kettle
boils the water faster than a 2000 W kettle, and both change the same thermal
store.

**Worked example 6.** A 2500 W kettle is switched on for 3.0 minutes. Calculate
the energy transferred to the water.

$$
\begin{aligned}
t &= 3.0 \times 60 = 180\,\text{s} \\
E &= P t \\
  &= 2500 \times 180 \\
  &= 450000\,\text{J} \\
  &= 4.5 \times 10^5\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is how a current transfers energy: the power of the appliance tells you the
energy transferred each second. The same idea gives the energy used by any
appliance on the mains.

## 9. Energy transferred by work

Work is another way to transfer energy. When a force moves an object along the
line of the force, the work done is

$$ W = F s $$

where $F$ is the force in newtons and $s$ is the distance moved in metres. The
energy transferred equals the work done, so one newton-metre is one joule.

**Worked example 7.** A force of 120 N moves a trolley 15 m along the line of the
force. Calculate the work done.

$$
\begin{aligned}
W &= F s \\
  &= 120 \times 15 \\
  &= 1800\,\text{J}
\end{aligned}
$$
