# 4.2 Sound waves and waves for detection and exploration — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 4.1, especially the wave equation and longitudinal
waves; Section 3.1 for speed as distance divided by time; powers of ten and
prefixes.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe how sound travels through
solids and how the ear detects it; explain why hearing covers only a limited
range of frequencies; calculate a distance from an echo; describe how ultrasound
is used for medical and industrial imaging; and describe how seismic waves give
evidence about the structure of the Earth.

## 1. Sound needs a medium

A sound wave is a longitudinal wave. It travels as a series of compressions and
rarefactions, so it needs particles to vibrate. A bell inside a sealed jar rings
loudly at first. When the air is pumped out of the jar, the ringing fades away,
even though the hammer still strikes the bell. The sound has no particles to
travel through.

Sound travels at different speeds in different media. The speed depends on the
medium, not on the sound. In air at room temperature the speed of sound is about
330 m/s. In water it is about 1500 m/s. In steel it is about 5000 m/s. Sound
travels fastest in solids, because the particles are closer together.

## 2. Sound waves, solids and hearing

Sound waves can travel through a solid and make the solid vibrate. When you tap
a table, you can sometimes feel the vibration through the wood.

Inside the ear, sound waves make the ear drum and other parts of the ear
vibrate. This vibration causes the sensation of sound. The conversion of a sound
wave into a vibration of a solid works only over a limited range of frequencies,
because the parts of the ear cannot vibrate at every frequency. That limited
range sets the limits of normal human hearing.

Most people can hear frequencies from about **20 Hz to 20 kHz**. Below 20 Hz is
**infrasound**. Above 20 kHz is **ultrasound**. Both are real waves, but you
cannot hear them.

**Worked example 1.** The lowest frequency a healthy young person can hear is
20 Hz. Calculate the period of this sound wave.

$$
\begin{aligned}
T &= \frac{1}{f} \\
  &= \frac{1}{20} \\
  &= 0.050\,\text{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 3. Using an echo to find a distance

When a wave meets the boundary between two different media, part of the wave is
reflected. The reflection is called an **echo**. The time taken for the echo to
return to a detector can tell you how far away the boundary is.

The pulse travels to the boundary and back, so the distance it covers is twice
the distance to the boundary:

$$ \text{distance to boundary} = \frac{\text{speed} \times \text{time}}{2} $$

**Worked example 2.** A pulse of ultrasound travels through tissue at 1500 m/s.
The reflection returns to the detector after 0.00012 s. Calculate the distance
to the boundary.

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= \frac{v t}{2} \\
  &= \frac{1500 \times 0.00012}{2} \\
  &= \frac{0.18}{2} \\
  &= 0.090\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 3.** In the chapter problem the echo sounder sends a pulse
through sea water at 1500 m/s. The reflection returns after 0.020 s. Calculate
the depth of the sea floor.

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= \frac{v t}{2} \\
  &= \frac{1500 \times 0.020}{2} \\
  &= \frac{30}{2} \\
  &= 15\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 4. Ultrasound and echo sounding

**Ultrasound** is a sound wave with a frequency higher than 20 kHz. Ultrasound
waves are partially reflected when they meet a boundary between two different
media. The time taken for the reflections to reach a detector shows how far away
the boundary is. This is how ultrasound is used for medical and industrial
imaging.

**Echo sounding** uses high-frequency sound to detect objects in deep water and
to measure the depth of the water. The survey boat in the chapter problem uses
echo sounding.

**Worked example 4.** A medical scanner uses ultrasound at a frequency of
2.0 MHz. The speed of sound in tissue is 1500 m/s. Calculate the wavelength.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2.0\,\text{MHz} &= 2.0 \times 10^6\,\text{Hz} \\
v &= f \lambda \\
\lambda &= \frac{v}{f} \\
  &= \frac{1500}{2.0 \times 10^6} \\
  &= 7.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is 0.75 mm. A shorter wavelength can show smaller details.

## 5. Seismic waves

An earthquake produces **seismic waves** that travel through the Earth. There
are two important types.

- **P-waves** are longitudinal. They travel at different speeds through solids
  and liquids.
- **S-waves** are transverse. They cannot travel through a liquid.

The two types travel at different speeds, so they arrive at a distant detector
at different times. This gives evidence about the inside of the Earth.

S-waves do not pass through the liquid outer core, so a region on the far side of
the Earth receives no S-waves. P-waves do pass through, but they are bent at the
boundary. From this evidence, scientists worked out the size and structure of the
Earth's core. The core cannot be seen directly, so the waves are the evidence.

**Worked example 5.** A seismic P-wave travels 60 km in 12 s. Calculate its
speed.

$$
\begin{aligned}
60\,\text{km} &= 60000\,\text{m} \\
v &= \frac{s}{t} \\
  &= \frac{60000}{12} \\
  &= 5000\,\text{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 6. Comparing waves in solids and liquids

Different types of wave travel at different speeds, are absorbed differently and
are reflected differently in solids and liquids. These differences can be used
to detect and to explore structures that cannot be seen directly, in the body,
in the Earth's core and in deep water.

- An ultrasound pulse sends a reflection back from a boundary inside the body, so
  a doctor can build a picture of an organ.
- A seismic wave changes speed and direction as it crosses the boundary of the
  core, so a scientist can work out where that boundary is.
- An echo sounder detects the sea floor or a shoal of fish from the reflection
  of a high-frequency sound pulse.

> **Watch out.** The echo time is the time for the pulse to travel to the
> boundary **and back**. A common error is to use the full time as if it were a
> one-way trip, which makes the distance twice as large. Always halve the time
> before you multiply by the speed. Also keep every quantity in SI units: convert
> kilometres to metres and megahertz to hertz before you calculate.
> **Re-check:** halve the echo time before multiplying by the speed, and confirm that your units are SI.
