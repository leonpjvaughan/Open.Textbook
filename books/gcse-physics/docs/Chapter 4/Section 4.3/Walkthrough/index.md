# 4.3 Waves at material interfaces — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 4.1, especially the wave equation and the difference
between a transverse wave and a longitudinal wave; Section 3.1 for speed as
distance divided by time; measuring an angle with a protractor.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe what happens to a wave at the
boundary between two materials; state the law of reflection; draw a ray diagram
to show reflection; and explain what happens to the speed, frequency and
wavelength when a wave is transmitted into a new material.

## 1. A boundary between two materials

The boundary between two different materials is called a **material interface**.
When a wave reaches an interface, three things can happen to it.

- It can be **reflected**: the wave bounces back from the boundary.
- It can be **transmitted**: the wave passes through the boundary into the second
  material.
- It can be **absorbed**: the energy of the wave is transferred to the material.

Usually all three happen at once. Some of the wave is reflected, some passes
through, and some is absorbed. How much of each depends on the two materials and
on the type of wave.

## 2. Reflection

Reflection happens when a wave bounces off a surface. To describe it, we draw a
**normal**: the line at right angles to the surface at the point where the wave
hits.

- The ray arriving at the surface is the **incident ray**.
- The ray leaving the surface is the **reflected ray**.
- The angle of incidence is the angle between the incident ray and the normal.
- The angle of reflection is the angle between the reflected ray and the normal.

The **law of reflection** states that the angle of incidence equals the angle of
reflection.

An echo is a reflected sound wave. Light reflecting from a mirror is a
reflection of a light wave, and a water ripple reflecting from the side of a tank
is a reflection of a water wave.

**Worked example 1.** A ray of light meets a mirror at an angle of incidence of
35 degrees. State the angle of reflection, and say what line the angles are
measured from.

The angle of reflection is 35 degrees, because the angle of reflection equals the
angle of incidence. Both angles are measured from the normal, which is the line
at right angles to the mirror at the point where the ray hits it.

## 3. Transmission and the speed of a wave

When a wave is transmitted into a new material, its speed can change. The
frequency does not change, because the frequency is set by the source. The wave
equation $v = f\lambda$ then tells you what happens to the wavelength.

- If the wave speeds up, the wavelength gets longer.
- If the wave slows down, the wavelength gets shorter.

This is why a wave can change direction when it crosses a boundary at an angle.
The change of direction is called **refraction**. You will meet refraction again
when you study lenses.

**Worked example 2.** A sound wave of frequency 500 Hz passes from air into
water. The speed of sound is 330 m/s in air and 1500 m/s in water. Calculate the
wavelength in each material.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{in air:}\quad \lambda &= \frac{v}{f} = \frac{330}{500} = 0.66\,\text{m} \\
\text{in water:}\quad \lambda &= \frac{v}{f} = \frac{1500}{500} = 3.0\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The frequency stays at 500 Hz. The higher speed in water gives the longer
wavelength.

**Worked example 3.** When a wave meets a boundary, 15% of its energy is
reflected and 25% is absorbed. Calculate the percentage of the energy that is
transmitted.

$$ 100\% - 15\% - 25\% = 60\% $$

## 4. Absorption

**Absorption** happens when a material takes in the energy of a wave. The energy
is transferred to the material and usually warms it a little.

Soft, rough materials absorb sound well. That is why a room lined with thick
curtains or carpets has a quieter echo than a room with bare walls. Dark, matt
surfaces absorb light well, which is why they look black.

**Worked example 4.** A water wave travels at 4.0 m/s and reflects from a wall
30 m away. Calculate the time taken for the wave to travel to the wall and back.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{total distance} &= 2 \times 30 = 60\,\text{m} \\
t &= \frac{s}{v} \\
  &= \frac{60}{4.0} \\
  &= 15\,\text{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 5.** A ripple tank contains water to a depth of 25 cm. Convert
this depth to metres.

$$ 25\,\text{cm} = 0.25\,\text{m} $$

**Worked example 6.** A person claps and hears the echo from a wall after
0.80 s. The speed of sound in air is 330 m/s. Calculate the distance from the
person to the wall.

$$
\begin{aligned}
d &= \frac{v t}{2} \\
  &= \frac{330 \times 0.80}{2} \\
  &= \frac{264}{2} \\
  &= 132\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 5. Using boundaries to explore hidden structures

Different materials reflect, transmit and absorb waves by different amounts, and
they can change the speed of a wave by different amounts. Scientists and
engineers use these differences to explore structures that cannot be seen
directly.

- An ultrasound pulse sends back a reflection from a boundary inside the body,
  such as the edge of an organ.
- A seismic wave from an earthquake changes speed and direction as it crosses
  the boundary of the Earth's core.
- An echo sounder detects the sea floor or a shoal of fish from the reflection
  of a high-frequency sound pulse.

The reflection tells you where the boundary is, and the change in speed tells you
something about the material on each side.
<!-- /tier:h -->

> **Watch out.** The angle of incidence and the angle of reflection are measured
> from the **normal**, not from the surface. A common error is to measure the
> angle between the ray and the surface, which gives a different number. The
> normal is the dashed line at right angles to the surface. Also remember that
> the frequency does not change when a wave crosses a boundary: only the speed
> and the wavelength can change.
> **Re-check:** draw the normal and measure both angles from it, not from the surface.
