# 4.1 Waves in air, fluids and solids — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 3.1, especially speed as distance divided by time;
reading a graph; multiplying and dividing decimals; using a formula and
rearranging it.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe a wave in terms of amplitude,
wavelength, frequency and period; use the wave equation; use the period
equation; describe the difference between a transverse wave and a longitudinal
wave; and describe a method to measure the speed of sound in air and the speed
of ripples on a water surface.

## 1. What a wave is

A **wave** transfers energy from one place to another. The material the wave
passes through is called the **medium**. The wave does not carry the medium with
it.

Water ripples spread across a pond. Sound travels from a speaker to your ear.
Light travels from a lamp to a book. In every case, energy moves from one place
to another, but the water, the air and the light path stay where they are.

A wave made in one short push is called a **pulse**. A wave that repeats is
described by four quantities: amplitude, wavelength, frequency and period.

## 2. Amplitude, wavelength, frequency and period

The **amplitude** of a wave is the maximum displacement of a point on the wave
from its undisturbed position. A large amplitude means a large vibration.

The **wavelength** is the distance from a point on one wave to the equivalent
point on the next wave. It is measured in metres. The symbol for wavelength is
the Greek letter lambda, $\lambda$.

The **frequency** is the number of waves that pass a point each second. It is
measured in hertz (Hz). One hertz is one wave per second.

The **period** is the time for one complete wave to pass a point. It is measured
in seconds. The period and the frequency are linked:

$$ \text{period} = \frac{1}{\text{frequency}} \qquad T = \frac{1}{f} $$

**Worked example 1.** A wave has a frequency of 4.0 Hz. Calculate its period.

$$
\begin{aligned}
T &= \frac{1}{f} \\
  &= \frac{1}{4.0} \\
  &= 0.25\,\text{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

The wave equation links the speed of a wave to its frequency and wavelength:

$$ \text{wave speed} = \text{frequency} \times \text{wavelength} \qquad v = f \lambda $$

where $v$ is the wave speed in metres per second, $f$ is the frequency in hertz,
and $\lambda$ is the wavelength in metres. The equation is given on the
equations sheet, so you choose it and use it.

**Worked example 2.** A ripple tank makes water waves with a frequency of
2.5 Hz and a wavelength of 0.20 m. Calculate the wave speed.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= f \lambda \\
  &= 2.5 \times 0.20 \\
  &= 0.50\,\text{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 3.** A sound wave in air has a frequency of 440 Hz. The speed
of sound in air is 330 m/s. Calculate the wavelength.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= f \lambda \\
\lambda &= \frac{v}{f} \\
  &= \frac{330}{440} \\
  &= 0.75\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 4.** A wave travels along a spring at 6.0 m/s. Its wavelength
is 0.40 m. Calculate the frequency.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= f \lambda \\
f &= \frac{v}{\lambda} \\
  &= \frac{6.0}{0.40} \\
  &= 15\,\text{Hz}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 5.** In the chapter problem the ripple tank makes waves with a
frequency of 5.0 Hz and a wavelength of 0.12 m. Calculate the wave speed.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= f \lambda \\
  &= 5.0 \times 0.12 \\
  &= 0.60\,\text{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 6.** A sound wave travels through a steel rail at 5000 m/s. Its
frequency is 1000 Hz. Calculate the wavelength.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= f \lambda \\
\lambda &= \frac{v}{f} \\
  &= \frac{5000}{1000} \\
  &= 5.0\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 3. The wave travels, not the medium

Put a cork on a pond and watch a ripple pass. The cork moves up and down, but it
does not travel across the pond with the wave. The cork returns to the same
place. This is evidence that the wave carries energy through the water, and the
water itself does not travel along with it.

The same is true of sound. When you speak, the air molecules near your mouth
vibrate back and forth. They push the neighbouring molecules, and a compression
travels to the listener. The air molecules do not travel from your mouth to the
listener.

## 4. Transverse and longitudinal waves

Waves are grouped by how the vibration compares with the direction of travel.

In a **transverse wave**, the vibration is at right angles to the direction in
which the wave travels. Ripples on a water surface are transverse waves.

In a **longitudinal wave**, the vibration is along the same line as the
direction in which the wave travels. A longitudinal wave has areas of
**compression**, where the particles are close together, and **rarefaction**,
where the particles are spread out. Sound waves travelling through air are
longitudinal waves.

You can make both kinds with a spring. Shake a spring up and down, and a
transverse wave travels along it. Push and pull the end of the spring along its
length, and a longitudinal wave travels along it.

## 5. Measuring the speed of a wave

To find a wave speed, measure a distance and the time the wave takes to travel
it, and use $v = \dfrac{s}{t}$.

To measure the speed of ripples on a water surface, make ripples in a ripple
tank at a steady frequency. Measure the wavelength from a photograph or a
stroboscope, and use $v = f\lambda$.

To measure the speed of sound in air, stand a measured distance from a large
wall or building. Make a short, loud sound, such as a clap, and measure the time
until you hear the echo. The sound travels to the wall and back, so the distance
is twice the measured distance.

> **Watch out.** Frequency and wavelength are not the same thing, and one is not
> the other rearranged. Frequency is how many waves pass each second, measured
> in hertz. Wavelength is the length of one wave, measured in metres. When the
> wave speed is fixed, doubling the frequency halves the wavelength, because
> $v = f\lambda$. Also, a wave carries energy and not the medium, so the water
> in a ripple tank does not travel across the tank with the ripples.
> **Re-check:** use $v=f\lambda$ to test whether your frequency and wavelength give the stated speed.
