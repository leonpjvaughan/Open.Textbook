# 5.5 Black body radiation — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check.

## Warm-up: remember

Answer these from memory. They use ideas from Sections 5.1 and 5.2.

1. Name the kind of radiation emitted by every warm object.
2. Work out $\dfrac{3.0 \times 10^8}{1.0 \times 10^{-5}}$.
3. Which has the longer wavelength, infrared radiation or visible light?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Infrared radiation.
2. $3.0 \times 10^{13}\,\text{Hz}$.
3. Infrared radiation has the longer wavelength.

If question 1 went wrong, look again at the order of the spectrum. If question 2
went wrong, take care with the powers of ten when you divide.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** A metal block is heated until it glows. Explain how the radiation it emits
changes as its temperature rises.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Two things change: how much radiation is emitted, and which wavelength is the
most intense.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say what happens to the total intensity. Then say what happens to the peak
wavelength, and link this to the colour you see.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

As the block gets hotter it emits more radiation at every wavelength, so the
total intensity rises. The wavelength of the most intense radiation becomes
shorter. The block first glows red, because red is the longest visible
wavelength. As it gets hotter the peak moves to shorter wavelengths, so the
colour changes through orange towards white.
</details>

**2.** A warm object emits most of its radiation at a wavelength of 10 μm.
Calculate the frequency of this radiation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use the wave equation as $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$, where
$c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\text{m/s}$ is the speed of light in a vacuum. Convert
micrometres to metres first.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$10\,\mu\text{m} = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{m}$. Put this into
$f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
f &= \frac{c}{\lambda} \\
  &= \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{1.0 \times 10^{-5}} \\
  &= 3.0 \times 10^{13}\,\text{Hz}
\end{aligned}
$$
</details>

**3.** A hotter object emits most of its radiation at a wavelength of 2.0 μm.
Work out the frequency of the radiation from the hotter object.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

This is the same calculation as problem 2, with a shorter wavelength. Check that
your frequency comes out higher.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

$2.0\,\mu\text{m} = 2.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$. Then divide the speed by this
wavelength.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
f &= \frac{c}{\lambda} \\
  &= \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{2.0 \times 10^{-6}} \\
  &= 1.5 \times 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}
\end{aligned}
$$

The shorter wavelength gives the higher frequency.
</details>

**4.** A cup of hot tea cools to room temperature and then stays there. Explain
why its temperature becomes constant.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The cup is always both absorbing and emitting radiation. Compare the two rates
while the tea is hot, and again at room temperature.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

First say why the tea is losing energy while it is hotter than the room. Then say
what must be true about the two rates when the temperature stops changing.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

While the tea is hotter than the room, it emits more infrared radiation than it
absorbs, so its thermal store empties and it cools. Once the tea is at room
temperature, it emits and absorbs radiation at the same rate. The net energy
transfer is zero, so the temperature stays constant.

This answer is a model answer for you to compare with your own wording.
</details>

**5.** A black surface and a shiny surface are at the same temperature. Compare
how much radiation each surface emits and absorbs.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

A good absorber is also a good emitter. Think about what each surface does with
infrared radiation.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Start with the black surface: decide whether it absorbs a lot or a little, then
say what that means for how much it emits.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The black surface absorbs a large amount of infrared radiation, and it also
emits a large amount.
The shiny surface absorbs very little, because it reflects most of the radiation,
and it also emits very little. The black surface is the better absorber and the
better emitter.
</details>
