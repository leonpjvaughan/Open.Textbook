# 5.3 Lenses and ray diagrams — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 5.2, especially refraction and the normal; measuring
lengths in millimetres, centimetres and metres.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe the action of a convex lens and
a concave lens; draw a ray diagram to find the image formed by a convex lens; and
calculate the magnification of a lens.

## 1. Convex and concave lenses

A **lens** is a piece of transparent material that refracts light. The refraction
happens at the two curved surfaces, so a lens can bring light to a point or
spread it out.

- A **convex lens** is thicker in the middle than at the edges. It brings
  parallel rays of light together. It is a **converging lens**.
- A **concave lens** is thinner in the middle than at the edges. It spreads
  parallel rays of light apart. It is a **diverging lens**.

The point where a convex lens brings parallel rays together is the **principal
focus**. The distance from the centre of the lens to the principal focus is the
**focal length**. A lens with a short focal length bends the light more strongly.

## 2. Ray diagrams

A **ray diagram** shows how light from an object forms an image. For a convex
lens you draw three things.

1. The **principal axis**, a straight line through the centre of the lens.
2. The lens, as a vertical line across the axis, with a principal focus marked on
   each side.
3. Two rays from the top of the object.

There are two rules for the rays.

- A ray that travels **parallel to the principal axis** is refracted through the
  principal focus on the other side.
- A ray that passes through the **centre of the lens** continues in a straight
  line.

The two rays meet at the top of the image. The image of a distant object is
**real** and **inverted**. A real image can be put on a screen, because the rays
really meet there.

If the object is closer to the lens than the principal focus, the rays do not
meet on the far side. They only appear to come from a point on the same side as
the object. This image is **virtual**. It is upright, and it is what a magnifying
glass produces. A virtual image cannot be put on a screen.

## 3. Magnification

The size of an image is described by the **magnification**:

$$ \text{magnification} = \frac{\text{image height}}{\text{object height}} $$

Magnification is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no unit. A magnification
greater than 1 means the image is larger than the object. A magnification
between 0 and 1 means the image is smaller.

**Worked example 1.** A lens projects a picture 2.0 cm tall onto a wall. The
image is 0.30 m tall. Calculate the magnification.

First change the units so that both heights are the same:

$$ 0.30\,\text{m} = 30\,\text{cm} $$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{magnification} &= \frac{30\,\text{cm}}{2.0\,\text{cm}} \\
                     &= 15
\end{aligned}
$$

The image is 15 times taller than the object.

**Worked example 2.** A lens forms an image of a coin. The coin is 3.0 mm across,
and the image is 15 mm across. Calculate the magnification.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{magnification} &= \frac{15\,\text{mm}}{3.0\,\text{mm}} \\
                     &= 5.0
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 3.** A lens has a magnification of 3.0. The object is 8.0 mm
tall. Calculate the height of the image.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{image height} &= \text{magnification} \times \text{object height} \\
                    &= 3.0 \times 8.0 \\
                    &= 24\,\text{mm}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 4. Changing the object distance

far the object is from the lens.

**Predict first.** Do not move the slider yet. Write down your answers.

1. As the object moves closer to the lens, does the image get bigger or smaller?
2. Does the image stay the same way up, or turn upside down?

**Test.** Move the slider and watch the image. Stop with the object twice as far
from the lens as the principal focus, and read the magnification.

**Explain.** The rays still obey the two ray rules at every setting. Why does
moving the object change the size of the image? Write your answer in two or three
sentences.

> **Watch out.** A real image from a convex lens is **inverted**, so it is upside
> down compared with the object. A virtual image from a magnifying glass is
> upright. Also check the units before you divide: a height in metres must be
> changed to centimetres or millimetres so that it matches the other height.
> Magnification has no unit, so an answer in centimetres is wrong.

## You should now be able to

- describe the action of a convex lens and a concave lens;
- explain what the principal focus and the focal length of a lens are;
- draw a ray diagram for a convex lens, using the two ray rules;
- describe a real image and a virtual image, and say how they differ;
- calculate the magnification of a lens;
- find an image height or an object height from the magnification.
