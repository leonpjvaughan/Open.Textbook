# 6.5 Static electricity and electric fields — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 6.1, especially electric charge and $Q = It$; the
idea that a force can act without contact, from Chapter 2.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe how static electricity is
produced by rubbing insulating materials; explain the transfer of electrons;
describe the forces between charged objects; and explain the non-contact force
using an electric field.

## 1. Charging by rubbing

When two different insulating materials are rubbed together, they become
**electrically charged**. Electrons are rubbed off one material and on to the
other.

- The material that **gains** electrons becomes **negatively** charged.
- The material that **loses** electrons is left with an equal **positive**
  charge.

This is why the charge is called **static**. The charge stays on the material
and does not flow away, because the material is an insulator. A dry cloth and a
plastic window are both insulators, so rubbing them together leaves charge on
both.

## 2. Forces between charged objects

When two charged objects are brought close together, they exert a force on each
other.

- Two objects with the **same** type of charge **repel** each other.
- Two objects with **different** types of charge **attract** each other.

You can show this with two polythene rods. Rub both with a cloth, then bring
them together. They push apart. Bring a rubbed polythene rod near a rubbed
perspex rod, and they pull together. The polythene has gained electrons and the
perspex has lost them, so the two rods carry opposite charges.

Attraction and repulsion between two charged objects are examples of a
**non-contact force**. The force acts even when the objects do not touch.

## 3. Electric fields

A charged object creates an **electric field** around itself. The field is the
region where the object can exert a force on another charged object.

The electric field is strongest close to the charged object. The further away
from the object, the weaker the field. A second charged object placed in the
field experiences a force, and that force gets stronger as the distance between
the objects decreases.

The electric field around an isolated charged sphere can be drawn as lines
radiating from the surface. The lines point away from a positive sphere and
towards a negative sphere. The lines are closer together near the surface, which
shows that the field is stronger there.

## 4. Sparking

A strong electric field can cause a **spark**. When charge builds up on an
object, the field near it becomes very strong. If the field becomes strong
enough, it can ionise the air, which means it removes electrons from the air
molecules. The ionised air can conduct a current, so charge jumps across the gap
as a spark.

You may have felt a small spark when you touch a car door after walking on a dry
carpet. Charge built up on your body, and it jumped to the metal as you touched
it.

**Worked example 1.** A spark transfers a charge of 3.0 μC in a time of 1.5 μs.
Calculate the average current.

$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \frac{Q}{t} \\
  &= \frac{3.0 \times 10^{-6}}{1.5 \times 10^{-6}} \\
  &= 2.0\,\text{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $I$ is the current, $Q$ is the charge transferred and $t$ is the time in
seconds.

The average current is 2.0 A. The spark lasts for a very short time, so the
current is large even though the charge is small.

> **Watch out.** Static charge does not stay on every material. Charge flows
