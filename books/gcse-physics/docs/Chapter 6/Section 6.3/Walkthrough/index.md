# 6.3 Domestic uses and safety — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 6.1 and Section 6.2, especially current as a flow of
charge, potential difference, and how components are connected. Chapter 1 for
energy transfers.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe the difference between direct
and alternating potential difference; recall the frequency and potential
difference of the UK domestic supply; describe the live, neutral and earth
wires; and explain the dangers of the live wire.

## 1. Direct and alternating potential difference

There are two kinds of supply.

- A **direct current** (d.c.) flows in one direction only. The potential
  difference stays the same way round. A battery supplies direct current.
- An **alternating current** (a.c.) keeps changing direction. The potential
  difference swaps ends many times each second. The mains supply is alternating.

The number of complete changes each second is the **frequency**, measured in
hertz. The UK domestic supply has a frequency of **50 Hz** and a potential
difference of about **230 V**. The current reverses direction 100 times each
second, because it changes direction twice in every cycle.

A graph of potential difference against time is a flat horizontal line for a
direct supply. For an alternating supply it is a wave.

## 2. The three wires

Most appliances are connected to the mains with three-core cable. Each wire has
a job and its own insulation colour.

| Wire | Colour | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Live | Brown | Carries the alternating potential difference from the supply |
| Neutral | Blue | Completes the circuit |
| Earth | Green and yellow stripes | A safety wire, to stop the appliance becoming live |

The potential difference between the live wire and earth is about 230 V. The
neutral wire is at, or close to, earth potential, which is 0 V. The earth wire
is also at 0 V. In normal use the earth wire carries no current. It carries a
current only if there is a fault.

## 3. Why the live wire is dangerous

The live wire is at about 230 V. If you touch it, a current can flow through
your body to the ground. Even a small current through the body can be fatal.

The live wire can be dangerous even when a switch in the mains circuit is open.
The switch may be in the neutral wire, or between the appliance and the live
wire. Either way, the wire that goes back to the supply is still live.

A second danger is a connection between the live wire and earth. The earth wire
and the metal case of an appliance are joined. If the live wire comes loose and
touches the metal case, the case would become live. Because the case is earthed,
there is instead a low-resistance path to earth, so a large current flows to
earth and the case does not stay at 230 V. This is why the metal case of an
appliance is always connected to the earth wire.

## 5. Worked example

**Worked example 1.** A mains appliance takes a current of 5.0 A for 2.0 minutes.
Calculate the charge that flows through it.

$$
\begin{aligned}
t &= 2.0 \times 60 = 120\,\text{s} \\
Q &= It \\
  &= 5.0 \times 120 \\
  &= 600\,\text{C}
\end{aligned}
$$

The same 600 C flows through the live wire and back through the neutral wire.
The current is not used up by the appliance.

## 6. Comparing direct and alternating

The table compares the two supplies you are most likely to meet.

| | Battery | UK mains |
|---|---|---|
| Kind of current | Direct (d.c.) | Alternating (a.c.) |
| Direction | One direction only | Changes direction |
| Typical potential difference | 1.5 V to 12 V | About 230 V |
| Frequency | 0 Hz | 50 Hz |

> **Watch out.** The neutral wire is at about 0 V, but it is not the same wire as
> the earth wire, and it is not safe to touch. It is part of the working circuit,
> so it can carry a current. The earth wire carries a current only in a fault.
> Also remember that the live wire is dangerous even when an appliance is
> switched off. If the switch is in the neutral wire, the live wire is still
> connected to the supply. Re-check which wire carries the current in normal use
> before you describe a circuit.
