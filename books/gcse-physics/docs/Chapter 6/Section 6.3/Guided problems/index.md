# 6.3 Domestic uses and safety — Practise with help

Work each problem first. Open a hint only if you are stuck, and the full working
only to check.

## Warm-up: remember

Answer these from memory. They use ideas from Section 6.1 and Section 6.2.

1. Use $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$ to find the current when 300 C flows in 60 s.
2. Two resistors of 5.0 $\Omega$ and 7.0 $\Omega$ are connected in series.
   Find the total resistance.
3. Name the instrument connected in parallel to measure potential difference.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. $I = \dfrac{300}{60} = 5.0\,\text{A}$.
2. $R_{total} = 5.0 + 7.0 = 12\,\Omega$.
3. A voltmeter.

If question 1 went wrong, check that you divided the charge by the time. If
question 2 went wrong, remember that resistances add in series.
</details>

## Practise with help

**1.** State the frequency and the potential difference of the UK domestic
supply, and describe the difference between direct and alternating current.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about the direction in which the charge flows in each kind of supply.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Give the two numbers for the UK supply first. Then say which kind of supply
changes direction and which one does not.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The UK domestic supply has a frequency of 50 Hz and a potential difference of
about 230 V.

A direct current flows in one direction only, so the potential difference keeps
the same direction. A battery is a direct supply. An alternating current keeps
changing direction, so the potential difference swaps ends many times each
second. The mains supply is alternating.

This answer is a model answer for you to compare with your own wording.
</details>

**2.** A mains kettle takes a current of 8.0 A. Calculate the charge that flows
through it in 1.5 minutes.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The equation $Q = It$ uses time in seconds. Change the time before you
substitute.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Work out $1.5 \times 60 = 90\,\text{s}$, then multiply by the current.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
t &= 1.5 \times 60 = 90\,\text{s} \\
Q &= It \\
  &= 8.0 \times 90 \\
  &= 720\,\text{C}
\end{aligned}
$$
</details>

**3.** Describe the job of the live wire, the neutral wire and the earth wire
in a mains cable.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

One wire carries the supply, one completes the circuit, and one is there only
for safety.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Match each job to a wire. Remember which wire is at 0 V in normal use.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The live wire carries the alternating potential difference from the supply. It
is at about 230 V relative to earth.

The neutral wire completes the circuit. It is at, or close to, earth potential,
which is 0 V.

The earth wire is a safety wire. It is at 0 V and carries a current only if
there is a fault. It stops the metal case of an appliance becoming live.

This answer is a model answer for you to compare with your own wording.
</details>

**4.** Explain why a live wire can be dangerous even when a switch in the mains
circuit is open.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

An open switch breaks the circuit, but it does not remove the supply from every
wire.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say where the switch might be placed, and which wire stays connected to the
supply even when the switch is open.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The switch might be in the neutral wire, or between the appliance and the live
wire. The wire that goes back to the supply is still connected to the live
supply, so it stays at about 230 V. If a person touches it, a current can flow
through the body to earth. That is why the live wire is dangerous even when the
appliance appears to be switched off.

This answer is a model answer for you to compare with your own wording.
</details>

**5.** Explain why the metal case of a mains appliance is connected to the earth
wire.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Imagine the live wire coming loose inside the appliance and touching the metal
case.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Say what would happen to the case without an earth wire, then what the earth
wire does instead.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

Without an earth wire, a loose live wire touching the metal case would make the
case live at about 230 V. A person touching the case could receive a dangerous
current.

With the case joined to the earth wire, the fault current has a low-resistance
path to earth. A large current flows to earth, and the case does not stay at
230 V, so it is much safer to touch.

This answer is a model answer for you to compare with your own wording.
</details>

**6.** A mains lamp is switched on. Explain why the current in the live wire is
equal to the current in the neutral wire.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The lamp and the two wires form a single closed loop.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Next step</summary>

Use the idea from Section 6.1 that the current is the same at every point in a
single closed loop.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full working</summary>

The lamp, the live wire and the neutral wire form a single closed loop. The
charge that flows into the lamp must flow out of it again, because charge is not
used up. The rate of flow of charge is the current, so the current in the live
wire equals the current in the neutral wire. Only the energy carried by the
charge is transferred to the lamp.

This answer is a model answer for you to compare with your own wording.
</details>
