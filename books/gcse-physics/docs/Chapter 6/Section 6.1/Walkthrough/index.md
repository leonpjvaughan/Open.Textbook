# 6.1 Current, potential difference and resistance — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 1, especially energy stores and energy transfers;
Section 5.1, especially the prefixes milli, kilo and mega; multiplying and
dividing decimals and numbers in standard form.

**By the end of this section you can:** describe an electric current as a flow
of charge; use the equation $Q = It$; use the equation $V = IR$; and describe
how the resistance of a lamp, a diode, a thermistor and an LDR changes.

## 1. Electric charge and current

**Electric charge** is a property of matter. There are two kinds of charge:
positive and negative. An electron carries a negative charge. Charge is measured
in **coulombs** (C).

For charge to flow, a circuit needs two things: a source of potential
difference, and a closed loop of conductor. A battery or a mains supply is a
source of potential difference. Break the loop, and the charge stops flowing.

An **electric current** is the rate of flow of electric charge. In one second,
a current of one ampere carries one coulomb of charge past a point. Current is
measured in **amperes** (A). The ampere is often called the amp.

$$ Q = It $$

where $Q$ is the charge flow in coulombs, $I$ is the current in amperes and $t$
is the time in seconds.

Current is measured with an **ammeter**. The ammeter is connected in series with
the component, so that the current through the ammeter is the current through the
component.

In a single closed loop, the current has the **same value at every point**.
Charge is not used up as it flows around the circuit. The energy it carries is
transferred to the components, but the charge itself keeps moving.

## 2. Potential difference, current and resistance

**Potential difference** (pd) is the energy transferred to or from each unit of
charge as it moves between two points in a circuit. It is measured in **volts**
(V). One volt means one joule of energy is transferred by each coulomb of
charge. Many books and exam papers also use the word **voltage** for potential
difference.

The potential difference across a component is measured with a **voltmeter**.
The voltmeter is connected in parallel with the component, so that it measures
the difference between the two ends.

The current through a component depends on two things: the potential difference
across it, and the **resistance** of the component. Resistance is measured in
**ohms** ($\Omega$). For a given potential difference, a larger resistance gives
a smaller current. The three quantities are linked by

$$ V = IR $$

where $V$ is the potential difference in volts, $I$ is the current in amperes
and $R$ is the resistance in ohms.

## 3. Circuit symbols and diagrams

Circuit diagrams use standard symbols, so that every physicist reads the same
circuit. These are the components this chapter uses.

| Component | What it does |
|---|---|
| Cell and battery | Supplies a potential difference |
| Switch | Opens or closes the circuit |
| Lamp | Transfers energy to the thermal and light stores |
| Resistor | Opposes the flow of charge |
| Variable resistor | A resistor whose value can be changed |
| Ammeter | Measures current; connected in series |
| Voltmeter | Measures potential difference; connected in parallel |
| Diode | Allows current in one direction only |
| Thermistor | Resistance falls as temperature rises |
| LDR | Resistance falls as light intensity rises |

A circuit diagram shows the components joined by straight lines that represent
the connecting wires. The long line of the cell symbol is the positive terminal,
and the short thick line is the negative terminal.

## 4. Worked examples

**Worked example 1.** A current of 3.0 A flows through a lamp for 20 s.
Calculate the charge that flows.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= It \\
  &= 3.0 \times 20 \\
  &= 60\,\text{C}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 2.** A charge of 120 C passes a point in a circuit in 40 s.
Calculate the current.

$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \frac{Q}{t} \\
  &= \frac{120}{40} \\
  &= 3.0\,\text{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 3.** A current of 0.50 A flows through a resistor of resistance
12 $\Omega$. Calculate the potential difference across the resistor.

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= IR \\
  &= 0.50 \times 12 \\
  &= 6.0\,\text{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 4.** The potential difference across a resistor is 9.0 V, and
the current through it is 0.30 A. Calculate the resistance.

$$
\begin{aligned}
R &= \frac{V}{I} \\
  &= \frac{9.0}{0.30} \\
  &= 30\,\Omega
\end{aligned}
$$

## 5. Resistors whose resistance changes

For some resistors, the resistance stays the same as the current changes. For
others, it changes.

- An **ohmic conductor** at a constant temperature has a constant resistance.
  The current through it is directly proportional to the potential difference
  across it. A graph of $V$ against $I$ is a straight line through the origin.
- A **filament lamp** is not ohmic. As the current rises, the filament gets
  hotter, and its resistance increases. The graph curves.
- A **diode** allows current to flow in one direction only. In the reverse
  direction, its resistance is very high, so almost no current flows.
- A **thermistor** has a resistance that decreases as its temperature increases.
  A thermistor is used in a thermostat to switch a heater on and off.
- An **LDR** (a light-dependent resistor) has a resistance that decreases as the
  light intensity increases. An LDR is used to switch a lamp on when it gets
  dark.

You can find the resistance of a linear component from its $V$–$I$ graph. The
gradient of a graph of $V$ against $I$ is the resistance $R$, because
$V = IR$ rearranges to $V = R \times I$. Take care: if $I$ is on the vertical
axis instead, the gradient is $\dfrac{1}{R}$.

**Explain.** The current is directly proportional to the potential difference
when the resistance is constant. Why does doubling the potential difference
double the current? Write your answer in two or three sentences.

> **Watch out.** The current is not used up as it goes around a circuit. In a
> single closed loop the current is the same at every point. What the components
> change is the energy carried by each coulomb, not the number of coulombs
> flowing each second. Also keep current and potential difference apart: current
> is measured in amperes with an ammeter in series, and potential difference is
> measured in volts with a voltmeter in parallel. Re-check which one a question
> asks for before you choose an equation.

- describe an electric current as a rate of flow of charge;
- state that a circuit needs a source of potential difference and a closed loop;
