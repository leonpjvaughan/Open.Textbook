# 3.3 Safety in public transport — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 3.1, especially speed, acceleration and
$v^2 - u^2 = 2as$; Section 3.2, especially $F = ma$ and Newton's first law;
Section 1.1, especially the kinetic energy store.

**By the end of this section you can:** work out a thinking distance, a braking
distance and a stopping distance; explain the factors that affect each one;
explain why large decelerations are dangerous; and, at Higher tier, estimate the
force in a large deceleration.

## 1. Stopping distance

The **stopping distance** of a vehicle is the total distance it travels from the
moment the driver sees a hazard to the moment the vehicle stops. It has two
parts.

- The **thinking distance** is the distance travelled while the driver reacts.
- The **braking distance** is the distance travelled while the brakes act.

$$ \text{stopping distance} = \text{thinking distance} + \text{braking distance} $$

## 2. Thinking distance and reaction time

The **reaction time** is the time between seeing a hazard and starting to brake.
It varies from person to person. A typical value is between **0.2 s and 0.9 s**.

During the reaction time the vehicle keeps moving at its original speed, so

$$ \text{thinking distance} = \text{speed} \times \text{reaction time} $$

**Worked example 1.** A car travels at 15 m/s. The driver has a reaction time of
0.80 s. Calculate the thinking distance.

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= v t \\
  &= 15 \times 0.80 \\
  &= 12\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reaction time is longer when a driver is tired, or has taken drugs or
alcohol. Distractions inside or outside the vehicle also make it longer. A longer
reaction time means a longer thinking distance, and so a longer stopping
distance.

You can measure a reaction time with a simple ruler test. A partner holds a ruler
vertically above your open hand. Your partner drops it without warning, and you
catch it as quickly as you can. The distance the ruler falls before you catch it
measures your reaction time: a longer distance means a slower reaction. Repeating
the test and taking an average gives a more reliable result.

## 3. Braking distance

The **braking distance** depends on the speed of the vehicle, the braking force,
and the conditions of the road, the brakes and the tyres.

- The greater the speed, the greater the braking distance. The braking distance
  increases with the **square** of the speed, so doubling the speed makes the
  braking distance four times as large.
- A wet or icy road gives less grip, so the same braking force produces a smaller
  deceleration and a longer braking distance.
- Worn brakes or worn tyres reduce the braking force, which also makes the
  braking distance longer.

When the driver brakes, the friction force between the brakes and the wheels does
work. This work reduces the kinetic energy of the vehicle, and the temperature of
the brakes rises. The kinetic energy is transferred to the thermal store of the
brakes and the surroundings.

**Worked example 2.** A car of mass 1200 kg travels at 15 m/s. Its braking
distance is 25 m. Calculate the deceleration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v^2 - u^2 &= 2 a s \\
0^2 - 15^2 &= 2 \times a \times 25 \\
-225 &= 50 a \\
a &= \frac{-225}{50} \\
  &= -4.5\,\text{m/s}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

The deceleration is $4.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.

The stopping distance of this car is $12 + 25 = 37\,\text{m}$: a thinking
distance of 12 m from Worked example 1, plus a braking distance of 25 m.

The greater the speed, the greater the braking force needed to stop the vehicle
in a certain distance. A greater braking force gives a greater deceleration.

> **Watch out.** Thinking distance and braking distance do not grow in the same
> way. Thinking distance is proportional to speed, so doubling the speed doubles
> it. Braking distance depends on the square of the speed, so doubling the speed
> makes it four times as large. If a question doubles the speed, do not double
> the whole stopping distance. Work out the two parts separately.

## 4. Estimating the force in a large deceleration

You can use $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$ to find the deceleration, and then $F = ma$ to find
the force.

**Worked example 3.** Calculate the braking force on the car in Worked example 2.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= m a \\
  &= 1200 \times 4.5 \\
  &= 5400\,\text{N} \\
  &= 5.4 \times 10^3\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 4.** A car of mass 1500 kg brakes from 24 m/s to rest in a
distance of 36 m. Calculate the deceleration and the braking force.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v^2 - u^2 &= 2 a s \\
0^2 - 24^2 &= 2 \times a \times 36 \\
-576 &= 72 a \\
a &= -8.0\,\text{m/s}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

The deceleration is $8.0\,\text{m/s}^2$, which is close to the acceleration of a
falling object. The braking force is

$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= m a \\
  &= 1500 \times 8.0 \\
  &= 12000\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

A deceleration this large is dangerous. If a vehicle stops suddenly, the people
inside continue to move forwards until something stops them. Without a seat belt,
that something is the dashboard or the windscreen.

## 5. The dangers of large decelerations

A large deceleration can be dangerous in two ways.

- The brakes may **overheat**. The kinetic energy of the vehicle becomes thermal
  energy in the brakes. A long, hard braking effort can make the brakes too hot,
  so they work less well.
- The vehicle may **lose control**. If the braking force is too large for the
  grip of the tyres, the wheels lock and the vehicle skids. A skidding vehicle
  cannot be steered.

Both effects mean that a driver should brake firmly enough to stop, and gently
enough to keep control.
