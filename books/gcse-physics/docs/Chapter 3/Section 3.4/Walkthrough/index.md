# 3.4 Momentum — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 3.1, especially velocity; Section 3.2, especially
$F = ma$ and Newton's third law; rearranging a formula.

**By the end of this section you can:** define and calculate momentum; use
conservation of momentum in a collision; calculate a force from the rate of
change of momentum; and explain how safety features reduce the force in a crash.

## 1. Momentum

**Momentum** is a property of a moving object. It is the product of the mass and
the velocity:

$$ p = m v $$

where $p$ is the momentum in kilogram metres per second (kg m/s), $m$ is the
mass in kilograms, and $v$ is the velocity in metres per second.

Momentum is a **vector**, so its direction matters. A car moving north and an
identical car moving south at the same speed have equal and opposite momenta.

**Worked example 1.** A car of mass 1200 kg travels at 15 m/s. Calculate its
momentum.

$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= m v \\
  &= 1200 \times 15 \\
  &= 18000\,\text{kg m/s} \\
  &= 1.8 \times 10^4\,\text{kg m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 2.** In the chapter problem the bus has a mass of 12000 kg and
travels at 12 m/s. Calculate its momentum.

$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= m v \\
  &= 12000 \times 12 \\
  &= 144000\,\text{kg m/s} \\
  &= 1.44 \times 10^5\,\text{kg m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 2. Conservation of momentum

A **closed system** is a system that no mass and no energy enter or leave. In a
closed system, the total momentum before an event equals the total momentum after
the event. This is the **conservation of momentum**.

In a collision, the objects push on each other. By Newton's third law the two
forces are equal and opposite, so one object gains the momentum that the other
one loses. The total momentum does not change.

To use conservation of momentum:

1. add the momenta before the event, with a sign for the direction;
2. add the momenta after the event, with a sign for the direction;
3. set the two totals equal.

**Worked example 3.** In the chapter problem the bus of mass 12000 kg travels at
12 m/s. It hits a stationary van of mass 8000 kg, and the two move off together.
Calculate their common velocity.

The total momentum before the collision is the momentum of the bus:

$$ p = 12000 \times 12 = 144000\,\text{kg m/s} $$

After the collision the two vehicles move together with a combined mass of
$12000 + 8000 = 20000\,\text{kg}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{total momentum before} &= \text{total momentum after} \\
144000 &= 20000 \times v \\
v &= \frac{144000}{20000} \\
  &= 7.2\,\text{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 4.** A trolley of mass 4.0 kg moves at 3.0 m/s and hits a
stationary trolley of mass 2.0 kg. The two trolleys stick together. Calculate
their common velocity.

$$
\begin{aligned}
4.0 \times 3.0 &= (4.0 + 2.0) \times v \\
12 &= 6.0 v \\
v &= 2.0\,\text{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 3. The force from a change in momentum

When a force acts on a moving object, the momentum of the object changes.
Newton's second law links the force to the **rate of change of momentum**.

$$ F = \frac{m \Delta v}{\Delta t} $$

where $m\Delta v$ is the change in momentum and $\Delta t$ is the time taken. The
force equals the rate of change of momentum.

You can see where this comes from. Write $F = ma$, and replace the acceleration
$a$ with $\dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$.

**Worked example 5.** A car of mass 1200 kg travels at 15 m/s and stops in
0.30 s. Calculate the force on the car.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= \frac{m \Delta v}{\Delta t} \\
  &= \frac{1200 \times (15 - 0)}{0.30} \\
  &= \frac{18000}{0.30} \\
  &= 60000\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 6.** In a crash test, the same car stops in 0.60 s because the
front of the car crumples. Calculate the force now.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= \frac{m \Delta v}{\Delta t} \\
  &= \frac{1200 \times 15}{0.60} \\
  &= \frac{18000}{0.60} \\
  &= 30000\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

The change in momentum is the same in both crashes, but the force is halved when
the time is doubled. A longer time means a smaller force.

**Explain.** The change in momentum is the same on every setting. Why does a
longer collision time give a smaller force? Write your answer in two or three
sentences.

> **Watch out.** Momentum is a vector, so opposite directions need opposite
> signs. If one object moves to the right and another moves to the left, give one
> momentum a positive sign and the other a negative sign before you add them. A
> common error is to add the sizes and lose the direction. Also keep the unit as
> kg m/s, and check that the mass is in kilograms before you multiply.

## 4. Safety features

A safety feature works by making the change in momentum take **longer**, so the
force is smaller. The change in momentum itself is fixed by the mass and the
speed, so the only way to reduce the force is to increase the time.

| Safety feature | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Seat belt | Stretches a little, so the wearer stops over a longer time and over a larger area. |
| Air bag | Inflates to give a soft, slow stop for the head and chest. |
| Crumple zone | The front of the car crumples, so the whole car stops over a longer time. |
| Cycle helmet | The foam crushes, so the head stops over a longer time. |
| Crash mat | Compresses when a gymnast lands, so the body stops over a longer time. |

Each feature increases $\Delta t$ in $F = \dfrac{m \Delta v}{\Delta t}$. A smaller
force means a smaller chance of injury.

## You should now be able to

- define momentum and calculate it from mass and velocity;
- use the conservation of momentum in a collision or an explosion;
- calculate the force from the rate of change of momentum, using
  $F = \dfrac{m \Delta v}{\Delta t}$;
- explain how seat belts, air bags, crumple zones, cycle helmets and crash mats
  reduce the force in a crash.
