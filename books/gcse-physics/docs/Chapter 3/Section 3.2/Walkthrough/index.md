# 3.2 Forces, acceleration and Newton's laws of motion — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 3.1, especially velocity and acceleration; Section 2.1,
especially forces, resultant forces, mass and weight; substituting into a
formula and rearranging it.

**By the end of this section you can:** state and use Newton's three laws of
motion; calculate a resultant force, a mass or an acceleration using $F = ma$;
and, at Higher tier, explain what inertial mass means.

## 1. Newton's first law

A **resultant force** is the single force that has the same effect as all the
forces acting on an object together. If the resultant force on an object is zero,
the forces are balanced.

**Newton's first law** says:

- if the resultant force on a stationary object is zero, the object stays
  stationary;
- if the resultant force on a moving object is zero, the object continues to move
  at the same speed and in the same direction.

So an object keeps a steady velocity until a resultant force acts on it. A car
that travels at a steady speed along a straight road has a driving force from the
engine, and resistive forces from friction and air resistance. The two are equal
and opposite, so the resultant force is zero.

## 2. Newton's second law

A resultant force changes the velocity of an object. The object accelerates.

**Newton's second law** says that the acceleration of an object is proportional
to the resultant force, and inversely proportional to the mass of the object.

$$ a \propto F \qquad a \propto \frac{1}{m} $$

The symbol $\propto$ means "is proportional to". As an equation,

$$ F = m a $$

where $F$ is the resultant force in newtons, $m$ is the mass in kilograms, and
$a$ is the acceleration in metres per second squared.

**Worked example 1.** A car of mass 1200 kg accelerates at $2.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.
Calculate the resultant force.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= m a \\
  &= 1200 \times 2.5 \\
  &= 3000\,\text{N} \\
  &= 3.0 \times 10^3\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 2.** A resultant force of 60 N acts on an object. The
acceleration is $4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. Calculate the mass of the object.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= \frac{F}{a} \\
  &= \frac{60}{4.0} \\
  &= 15\,\text{kg}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 3.** A resultant force of 240 N acts on a trolley of mass
80 kg. Calculate the acceleration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= \frac{F}{m} \\
  &= \frac{240}{80} \\
  &= 3.0\,\text{m/s}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Watch out.** Use the **resultant** force in $F = ma$, not one of the
> individual forces. If a car is pushed forwards with 800 N and air resistance
> is 300 N backwards, the resultant force is $800 - 300 = 500\,\text{N}$. Using
> 800 N gives an acceleration that is too large. Also check that the mass is in
> kilograms: 500 g is 0.5 kg, not 500 kg. Re-check by asking whether your answer
> is sensible for the object in front of you.

## 3. Inertia and inertial mass

The tendency of an object to stay at rest, or to keep moving at a constant
velocity, is called **inertia**. A heavy object is harder to start moving and
harder to stop than a light one.

**Inertial mass** is a measure of how difficult it is to change the velocity of
an object. It is defined as the ratio of the force acting on the object to the
acceleration it produces:

$$ \text{inertial mass} = \frac{\text{force}}{\text{acceleration}} \qquad m = \frac{F}{a} $$

**Worked example 4.** A resultant force of 250 N gives an object an acceleration
of $0.50\,\text{m/s}^2$. Calculate the inertial mass of the object.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= \frac{F}{a} \\
  &= \frac{250}{0.50} \\
  &= 500\,\text{kg}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 4. Newton's third law

**Newton's third law** says that whenever two objects interact, the forces they
exert on each other are equal and opposite.

The two forces act on **different** objects, so they do not cancel. If you push a
wall, your hand pushes the wall and the wall pushes your hand back with the same
size force in the opposite direction.

Here are two examples of equilibrium. A book rests on a table: the weight of the
book pulls down on the table, and the table pushes up on the book with an equal
force. A swimmer pushes the water backwards, and the water pushes the swimmer
forwards.

## 5. Estimating large accelerations and forces

You can use $F = ma$ to estimate the force in an everyday situation. This helps
you judge whether a force is safe.

**Worked example 5.** A car of mass 1500 kg accelerates at $6.0\,\text{m/s}^2$.
Calculate the resultant force on the car.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= m a \\
  &= 1500 \times 6.0 \\
  &= 9000\,\text{N} \\
  &= 9.0 \times 10^3\,\text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

A force of 9000 N is large. Large forces and large accelerations are why safety
features matter. Section 3.3 looks at how to reduce the danger in a crash.

## 6. The force and acceleration investigation

You can investigate the effect of the force on the acceleration of a trolley of
constant mass.

1. Set up a trolley on a runway. Attach a string that passes over a pulley to a
   hanging mass. The hanging weight is the force that pulls the trolley.
2. Use light gates or a motion sensor to measure the velocity of the trolley as
   it passes, and find the acceleration from a velocity-time graph.
3. Keep the mass of the trolley constant. Increase the hanging mass to increase
   the force, and record the acceleration each time.
4. Then keep the force constant and add masses to the trolley. Record the
   acceleration each time.

A graph of acceleration against force is a straight line through the origin. A
graph of acceleration against mass is a curve that falls, and a graph of
acceleration against $\dfrac{1}{\text{mass}}$ is a straight line through the
origin. This is the evidence behind Newton's second law.
