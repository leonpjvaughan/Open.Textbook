# 3.1 Speed, velocity and motion graphs — Learn

**Prerequisites:** Section 2.1, especially forces and vectors; the kinetic energy
store from Section 1.1; reading a straight-line graph; dividing and multiplying
numbers.

**By the end of this section you can:** tell distance from displacement, and
speed from velocity; use typical speeds; calculate speed, distance and time;
calculate acceleration; find speed and acceleration from a motion graph, and, at
Higher tier, read a tangent and an area; use $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$; and describe
terminal velocity.

## 1. Distance and displacement

**Distance** is how far an object moves. It has a size but no direction, so it is
a **scalar**. A runner who completes a 400 m lap has run a distance of 400 m.

**Displacement** is the distance in a straight line from the start point to the
finish point, together with the direction of that line. Displacement is a
**vector**. The same runner finishes a 400 m lap back at the start, so the
displacement is zero.

## 2. Speed

**Speed** is how fast an object moves. It has a size but no direction, so it is a
scalar. For an object that moves at a steady speed,

$$ \text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} \qquad s = v t $$

where $s$ is the distance in metres, $v$ is the speed in metres per second, and
$t$ is the time in seconds.

The speed of a moving object is rarely steady. A car speeds up, slows down and
stops. For a journey with changing speed, the **average speed** describes the
whole journey:

$$ \text{average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}} $$

**Worked example 1.** A cyclist travels 90 m in 15 s. Calculate her average
speed.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= \frac{s}{t} \\
  &= \frac{90}{15} \\
  &= 6.0\,\text{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example 2.** A car travels at a steady 24 m/s for 5.0 s. Calculate the
distance it travels.

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= v t \\
  &= 24 \times 5.0 \\
  &= 120\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 3. Typical speeds

Some speeds are worth remembering, because they help you judge whether an answer
is sensible.

| Object | Typical speed |
|---|---|
| A person walking | 1.5 m/s |
| A person running | 3 m/s |
| A person cycling | 6 m/s |
| Sound in air | 330 m/s |

A car in a town travels at about 12 m/s. A car on a motorway travels at about
30 m/s. A train travels at about 60 m/s. The speed of the wind changes from day
to day, so there is no single typical value for it.

The speed of a person walking, running or cycling depends on age, the ground, and
how far they travel. Each value above is a guide, not a rule.

## 4. Velocity

**Velocity** is speed in a given direction. It is a vector. Two cars can have the
same speed and different velocities, because they travel in different directions.

<!-- tier:h -->
A car on a roundabout can move at a steady speed and still change velocity. The
speed stays the same, but the direction of travel keeps changing, so the velocity
keeps changing. The same is true for a satellite in a circular orbit. It moves at
a constant speed, and its velocity changes all the time, because its direction
changes all the time.
<!-- /tier:h -->

## 5. Distance-time graphs

A **distance-time graph** shows how far an object has travelled. Time goes along
the horizontal axis. Distance goes along the vertical axis.

- A horizontal line means the object is stationary.
- A straight sloping line means a steady speed.
- A steeper line means a greater speed.

The speed is the **gradient** of the line:

$$ \text{speed} = \frac{\text{change in distance}}{\text{change in time}} $$

**Worked example 3.** A distance-time graph for a walker is a straight line. The
line passes through $(0\,\text{s}, 0\,\text{m})$, $(4.0\,\text{s}, 32\,\text{m})$
and $(8.0\,\text{s}, 64\,\text{m})$. Calculate the speed.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= \frac{\text{change in distance}}{\text{change in time}} \\
  &= \frac{64 - 0}{8.0 - 0} \\
  &= 8.0\,\text{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<!-- tier:h -->
If the object is accelerating, the distance-time graph is curved. To find the
speed at one moment, draw a **tangent** to the curve at that time. The gradient of
the tangent is the speed at that moment.
<!-- /tier:h -->

## 6. Acceleration

**Acceleration** is the rate at which velocity changes. For motion in a straight
line,

$$ \text{acceleration} = \frac{\text{change in velocity}}{\text{time taken}} \qquad a = \frac{\Delta v}{t} $$

where $a$ is the acceleration in metres per second squared, $\Delta v$ is the
change in velocity in metres per second, and $t$ is the time in seconds.

An object that slows down has a **negative** acceleration. We often say that it
**decelerates**.

**Worked example 4.** A bus speeds up from rest to 9.0 m/s in 6.0 s. Calculate
its acceleration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= \frac{\Delta v}{t} \\
  &= \frac{9.0 - 0}{6.0} \\
  &= 1.5\,\text{m/s}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Near the Earth's surface, an object that falls freely under gravity accelerates
at about $9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. A car pulling away from traffic lights accelerates
at about $2\,\text{m/s}^2$. A train braking at a station decelerates at about
$1\,\text{m/s}^2$.

## 7. Velocity-time graphs

A **velocity-time graph** shows how the velocity changes. The gradient of the
line is the acceleration:

$$ a = \frac{\text{change in velocity}}{\text{change in time}} $$

**Worked example 5.** A velocity-time graph is a straight line through
$(0\,\text{s}, 0\,\text{m/s})$, $(5.0\,\text{s}, 15\,\text{m/s})$ and
$(10\,\text{s}, 30\,\text{m/s})$. Calculate the acceleration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= \frac{30 - 0}{10 - 0} \\
  &= 3.0\,\text{m/s}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

<!-- tier:h -->
The **area under a velocity-time graph** is the distance travelled. When the
graph is a straight line from rest, the area is the area of a triangle:

$$ \text{distance} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} $$

**Worked example 6.** A velocity-time graph rises in a straight line from
$0\,\text{m/s}$ at $0\,\text{s}$ to $12\,\text{m/s}$ at $6.0\,\text{s}$. Calculate
the distance travelled.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{distance} &= \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} \\
                &= 0.5 \times 6.0 \times 12 \\
                &= 36\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

You can also find the area by counting the squares under the line.
<!-- /tier:h -->

## 8. Motion with uniform acceleration

For motion with a steady acceleration, one equation links the speeds, the
acceleration and the distance:

$$ v^2 - u^2 = 2 a s $$

where $v$ is the final velocity, $u$ is the initial velocity, $a$ is the
acceleration, and $s$ is the distance. This equation is given on the equations
sheet, so you choose it and use it; you do not need to remember it.

**Worked example 7.** A car travels at 20 m/s. The driver brakes with a steady
deceleration of $4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ until the car stops. Calculate the distance
the car travels while braking.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v^2 - u^2 &= 2 a s \\
0^2 - 20^2 &= 2 \times (-4.0) \times s \\
-400 &= -8.0 s \\
s &= \frac{400}{8.0} \\
  &= 50\,\text{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 9. Terminal velocity

When an object falls through a fluid, two forces act on it. Its weight pulls it
down, and the **drag** force from the fluid pushes it up. At the start the weight
is larger than the drag, so the object speeds up.

As the object gets faster, the drag force increases. Eventually the drag force
equals the weight. The resultant force is then zero, so the object stops speeding
up. It falls at a steady velocity called the **terminal velocity**.

A velocity-time graph for a falling object shows a steep rise at first. The
gradient then gets smaller, and the line becomes horizontal at the terminal
velocity.

> **Watch out.** A horizontal line on a velocity-time graph does **not** mean the
> object is stationary. It means the velocity is steady. The object is moving at
> a constant velocity. A horizontal line on a distance-time graph is the one that
> means the object is stationary. Check which quantity is on the vertical axis
> before you read the line.
