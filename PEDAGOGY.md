# PEDAGOGY — the evidence behind the chapter formula

This is the rationale for the operative rules in `AGENTS.md`. It is a faithful
reading of `../edu-research/summary/build/pedagogy.pdf` (an evidence briefing,
3 October 2026), with the briefing's own caveats preserved.

**Headline finding.** Combine opportunities to discover personal meaning with
teaching that enables pupils to succeed, explain their thinking and revisit
knowledge. Interest interventions and memory interventions address different
outcomes; neither automatically delivers the other. A lively lesson, a high
immediate score, and choosing further study are three distinct outcomes.

## Principle → evidence → design

### P1 — Teach first, then fade the support
- The EEF mathematics review supports explicit teaching and structured
  collaboration for younger or less experienced learners: model a solution,
  explain decisions, then reduce support [3, §§6.2–6.4].
- A meta-analysis of 72 inquiry-learning studies found guidance improved
  outcomes over less or no guidance (d = 0.50, 95% CI 0.37–0.62) [8]. It does
  not show inquiry always beats explicit teaching.
- **Design:** Walkthrough = worked example with narrated reasoning; Guided
  problems fade hint → step → full working; Problems are independent. This is
  the existing Walkthrough / Guided / Problems split, made deliberate.

### P2 — Give the chapter a destination and return to it
- Hidi and Renninger distinguish triggered and maintained situational interest
  from emerging and established individual interest [2]. A surprising puzzle can
  open a topic; returning with growing knowledge may help interest endure. This
  is a developmental framework, not proof of a recipe.
- EEF Science recommends revisiting preconceptions and using practical work with
  a clear learning purpose [4].
- **Design:** chapter anchor problem, solvable by the end, revisited at 2–3
  checkpoints, resolved on the Review page. It is an *interest* device, so it is
  budgeted and must not gate early learning or demoralise: first contact is
  low-stakes.

### P3 — Relevance is constructed by the learner, not announced
- Hulleman and Harackiewicz randomised 262 pupils (mostly US grade 9) to write
  either connections between science and their lives or content summaries, every
  three or four weeks over a semester. The relevance condition improved interest
  and grades, particularly for pupils with low expectations of success [1].
  Grades existed for only 100 pupils; it was mixed science.
- The briefing's own distinction: "A teacher announcing that a topic is useful is
  a different intervention from pupils constructing its value."
- **Design:** short maths-tied contexts as *offers*, plus a learner-written
  relevance prompt that recurs, revisits, and is stored client-side.

### P4 — Retrieve from memory, over gaps
- Dunlosky et al. rate practice testing and distributed practice as highly useful
  across learners and materials [5]; EEF Science also recommends retrieval and
  revisiting [4]. This is the briefing's **most secure recommendation**.
- **Design:** retrieval starter on every Guided problems page; spaced revisits
  across sections and chapters; corrective feedback.

### P5 — Interleave, and make the learner choose the method
- Rohrer et al. found interleaved practice beat blocked practice after 30 days,
  74% vs 42%, with 63 grade-7 pupils on graphs and slope [6]; a 54-class trial
  reported a benefit on an unannounced test [7]. Teach a new method with enough
  initial practice first, then mix.
- **Design:** blocked practice to learn, then a chapter Review page that mixes
  current and earlier problem types and asks "which method applies, and why?".

### P6 — Guide every investigation or tool
- Guidance improved inquiry outcomes [8]; EEF Science recommends eliciting a
  prediction, justifying it, and reconsidering with evidence [4].
- **Design:** every figure/tool runs Predict → Test → Explain. No free sandboxes.
  A bespoke application tool is reserved for chapters where the application *is*
  the maths. The briefing's age adaptation for 14–16 (our GCSE band) is exactly
  "pupil-chosen applications and guided investigations".

### Misconceptions
- EEF Science recommends identifying and revisiting preconceptions through
  explanation and calculation [4].
- **Design:** at least one "common error" callout per Walkthrough, with a check.

### Budget
- The briefing warns against reducing the evidence to "fun activities work" and
  against expecting a brief motivational exercise to compensate for gaps in
  teaching and understanding.
- **Design:** hooks + applications + tools ≤ ~¼ of a chapter; practice dominates.

## What the formula deliberately does not claim
- The anchor problem is not evidenced to improve **retention**; it is an interest
  mechanism. Retrieval, spacing and interleaving carry the retention load.
- Relevance evidence is causal but mostly science, US, and mixed; transfer to
  GCSE maths is an adaptation, not a tested recipe.
- Interleaving evidence is early-secondary maths (graphs and slope) and may not
  generalise to every topic or age.
- Structured collaboration helps, but a web textbook cannot make pair work
  accountable; "explain it" prompts are a partial substitute, not an equivalent.
